Use of Control Function to Interaction Term with Endogenous Variable

The control function approach offers advantages over the standard instrumental variable approach, particularly when dealing with interactions (Papies, Ebbes and Van Heerde 2017; Wooldridge 2015). The inclusion of the control function offers a robust method to address potential endogeneity in both the primary regressor and its interaction terms. Theoretically, the origin of the potential endogeneity in the interaction term can be traced back to the main endogenous variable. This is because the interaction term is essentially a product of the main variable and another variable. If the main variable is endogenous, this endogeneity will naturally propagate to the interaction term.

However, the control function, by construction, captures and corrects for the endogenous components of the main variable. Consequently, when the control function adjusts for the endogeneity in the main variable, it also inherently corrects for the endogeneity present in the interaction term. Thus, the control function does not need to be interacted with moderators (See also Han, Mittal and Zhang 2017; Shen and Xiao 2014). A detailed mathematical illustration of this concept is provided below.

C.1. Selection Model of Social Match Participation

We present the empirical results of the first-stage regression on social match participation using an instrumental variable (IV). Our IV consists of two components: 𝑁𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡𝑃𝑙𝑎𝑦𝑃𝑜𝑟𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛𝑖, which computes the ratio of total gameplay minutes during the night (from 6:00 PM – 6:00 AM) to the total gameplay minutes, and 𝑆𝑒𝑟𝑣𝑒𝑟𝐷𝑜𝑤𝑛𝑡, a binary indicator of unexpected game server outages for maintenance.

Potential concerns in the use of this IV include: (1) the possibility of a degenerate condition where the IV has no variation at all during the entire observation period. Exclusion. Another essential criterion for a valid instrument is the exclusion restriction, which indicates that the instrument should be correlated with target dependent variable solely through its relationship with the endogenous variable (i.e., social match participation). To test for the exogeneity of our IV, we follow the exclusion test proposed by Barron, Kung, and Proserpio (2021). Specifically, if our instrument is valid, we should observe no change in purchase activities among players who did not participate in social matches in response to the instrument. To confirm this, we regressed the weekly purchase amount (i.e., the dependent variable in our main analysis) on the instrument, focusing on the sample of player-week observations without social match participation. From this regression, we find no significant relationship between our instrument and the dependent variable (𝛽= .023;𝑝> .10). This result demonstrates the validity of our IV as a tool for accounting for the selection process into social matches using solely exogenous variation in the data.

We calculate the inverse Mills ratio (IMR, hereafter) based on the full model presented in Table C1 and include it as an additional regressor in all analyses performed in the manuscript. IMR effectively accounts for the selection bias associated with participating or not participating in
