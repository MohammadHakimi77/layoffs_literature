1 | INTRODUCTION

Over the last few years, there has been increasing interest in ecosystems. This is due to the fact that in many industries, the competitive advantage for firms is shifting “from superior standalone products and services to integrated value propositions based on an ecosystem of interdependent products from multiple, independent firms” (Adner, 2017). In particular, strategic management and antitrust scholars, as well as practitioners and regulators, are paying increasing attention to these dynamics in the context of “platform ecosystems,” as platform organizational forms and related business models are ubiquitous in the digital age (Gawer, 2022; G. Parker & Alstyne, 2008) and ecosystems tend to be based on platforms (Jacobides et al., 2020).

Following Jacobides et al. (2018), we define “ecosystems” as “a set of actors with varying degrees of multilateral, nongeneric complementarities that are not fully hierarchically controlled.” The extensive literature on platform ecosystems has so far focused on cases in which the ecosystem is constituted by a platform owner, (non-platform) complementors and end users (i.e., “intra-platform ecosystems”). However, as of yet, platfom ecosystem theory has overlooked cases in which a platform is a complementor to another platform. We call these “inter-platform ecosystems.”

For example, the popular platform TripAdvisor offers end users the possibility to find, compare, and book hotels, apartments, restaurants, and other services related to traveling. In order to do so, it relies on multiple complementors. Some of them (e.g., individuals providing ratings, independent hotels sharing real-time information on room prices and availability, etc.) are nonplatform complementors. Others are platform complementors, which in some cases orchestrate their own ecosystems. For example, platforms such as Booking.com or Hotels use modular technologies like application programing interfaces (APIs) to feed TripAdvisor with real-time customized discounts, prices, and availability information about accommodations from their own ecosystems. Similarly, when a user visits a restaurant on TripAdvisor, a button redirecting to the restaurant-booking platform The Fork is embedded. Google Maps, in turn, provides code to allow TripAdvisor and other platforms to embed a map of the location being consulted in their websites. Inter-platform ecosystems are increasingly important organizational forms. “Super apps” like WeChat, Alipay, Grab, and Gojek—integrating multiple platforms into single interfaces— dominate Asian and African digital markets. Western platforms are following suit: Facebook incorporates Meta Pay, Marketplace, and Dating; Uber integrates food delivery, public transit, and reservations. Consumer demand supports continued Western expansion (Deloitte, 2023; Senterre et al., 2024), with 44%–55% of consumers interested in super apps (PYMNTS, 2022).

Generative AI should facilitate future inter-platform integration within an inter-platform ecosystem (Heimburg et al., 2024; Schmidt et al., 2024). ChatGPT exemplifies this through complementor integrations: Kayak GPT augments ChatGPT responses with live flight data, creating a conversational travel assistant for search, booking, and hotel/car rental recommendations. OpenTable, Expedia, and Instacart have developed analogous plugins. Moreover, this article shows empirically that multiple inter-platform ecosystems interweaving some of the most popular European platforms already exist.

Due to their increasing relevance, inter-platform ecosystems have become the subject of growing antitrust scrutiny. The merger between the online travel agency (OTA) platform Booking and eTraveli, which owns several flight and hotel booking platforms, is a landmark case. The European Commission blocked the merger, concluding that it would facilitate the embedding of content from some platforms into others and redirecting users between them, which would “likely increase barriers to entry/expansion for rival hotel OTAs and strengthen network effects” (European Commission, 2024, Section 6.7.2.3, p. 203). Similar concerns have been raised by other authorities in mergers involving inter-platform ecosystems spanning across multiple markets such as gambling (FDJ/Zeturf), job-seeking (Seek Asia Investments/JobStreet), wedding planning (Wedding Planner/Zank you), classified ads and car sales (Schibsted/Nettbil), e-commerce and real estate (Trade Me/Property NZ), and ticketing (viagogo/Stubhub).

These trends suggest that inter-platform ecosystems are more than a niche phenomenon, marking an important strategic development across various industries in the digital economy. However, existing platform ecosystem theory has yet to fully capture their distinctive characteristics and the strategies that drive them.

While inter-platform ecosystems share building blocks with intra-platform ones, key defining elements fall outside current theory's scope. In inter-platform ecosystems, platforms serve as complementors to each other, creating or strengthening cross-platform network effects while simultaneously generating value through their own intra-platform network effects and complementor ecosystems. This results in a constellation of interconnected value propositions connected within an inter-platform ecosystem. In contrast, current intra-platform ecosystem theory considers a single orchestrator, non-platform complementors, and one platform as the focal product with network effects bounded within that platform.

Relatedly, there is a gap in the literature regarding strategies motivating and enabling interplatform ecosystem creation. While recent research explains intra-platform ecosystem emergence through exogenous matching failures, innovation spillovers, and cooperation failures (Jacobides et al., 2024), and platform strategy theory addresses platform-centric and envelopment strategies (Schreieck et al., 2024), neither explains how platforms engage in inter-platform collaborations to establish inter-platform ecosystems.

We address these gaps by applying and extending the conceptual framework of the (platform) ecosystems literature. To guide this conceptualization, we develop an empirical methodology. This allows us to empirically identify signs of cross-platform failures, delineate demand-side inter-platform ecosystems (hereinafter DSIPEs), and detect the coordination and

The extension of current intra-platform ecosystem theory allows us to draw from a consolidated conceptual framework while ensuring its coherence with the theoretical insights we provide on inter-platform ecosystems. However, given the lack of theoretical and empirical literature on inter-platform ecosystems, we cannot rely on alreadystudied cases or examples to accomplish theory building.

To overcome this barrier, we carry out a quantitative identification of a subset of interplatform ecosystems: demand-side inter-platform ecosystems. The methodology builds on detecting nongeneric complementarities in consumption between platforms.1 This allows us to constitute an empirical base of cases on which to build theory. Moreover, our approach has the advantage of generating a large and comprehensive sample of platforms. Hence, it makes our empirical base robust to build general findings.

We find that inter-platform ecosystems are built to minimize cross-platform negative information externalities or to solve cross-platform matching failures. The former arise from the search costs which the lack of inter-platform alignment can impose on their shared user base. For example, the higher the number of online retail platforms selling similar products and presenting product information differently, the more costly it is for multihoming users to search across them. If platforms coordinate and cooperate (e.g., through APIs and commercial agreements) with a price comparison platform, they can minimize these externalities, allowing users to access a one-stop shop platform that compares the offerings of multiple platforms. Cross-platform matching failures result from unhinged positive interaction externalities between users from different platforms. For example, the sellers and the buyers of two real estate platforms would benefit from interacting in order to have access to a wider network. In the absence of inter-platform alignment (e.g., by facilitating cross-posting), only multi-homing users would benefit from these additional interactions. Moreover, we posit that the strategies platforms implement to solve these failures depend on the nature of the externalities that trigger them, the types of platforms involved, and their competitive relationship. We identify four of such strategies: “network fusion,” “user-community-driven cross-platform interactions,” “meta-platform,” and “platform concatenation.” For each of them, we show the nature and the source of the cross-platform failure addressed by it; we identify the players and the actions taken by them to do so, and the pillars of joint value creation.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the empirical methodology used and the resulting stylized facts.2 Building on this empirical base and on intra-platform ecosystem theory, Section 3 provides theoretical insights into why and how demand-side inter-platform ecosystems emerge. In particular, it shows the types of crossplatform endogenous failures DSIPEs solve and the strategies their actors employ to do so. Section 4 offers implications for theory and sets out directions for further research.

3 | FROM CROSS-PLATFORM EXTERNALITIES TO INTER- PLATFORM ECOSYSTEM STRATEGIES

Intra-platform ecosystems emerge to solve exogenous “matching” or “systemic innovation” failures, on the one hand, and a coordination failure with and between its non-platform complementors, on the other hand (Jacobides et al., 2024). In contrast, inter-platform ecosystems solve endogenous cross-platform failures. When a platform's (ecosystem) generates externalities on another one, platforms can act as complementors to each other to internalize them.

We argue that inter-platform ecosystems are built to minimize cross-platform information externalities (increased search costs) or to solve cross-platform matching failures. The former arise between platforms with a shared user base, and result in less and lower-quality matches in each platform. The latter result from unhinged positive interaction externalities between users from different platforms. Moreover, we posit that the strategies implemented by platforms and/or users to solve these failures depend on the nature of the externalities that trigger them, the types of platforms involved and their competitive relationship.

Positive cross-platform interaction externalities exist when the value of a platform to (some of) its users increases with the interactions taking place in another platform. In the absence of

Negative information externalities arise between platforms sharing a user base when, in absence of inter-platform alignment, their co-existence imposes search costs to the shared user base. In this case, platforms do not internalize this negative externality. The case of the popular Hungarian classifieds (Jofogas) and product comparison online shopping comparison (Arukereso) platforms illustrates it. Given that Jofogas does not offer a built-in functionality to link a posting to its corresponding Arukereso page, information-savvy users of both platforms have to navigate them separately and face search costs.

When platforms face a cross-platform matching failure, both platform owners and users may solve it through a “network fusion” strategy. Alternatively, their users can lead a “usercommunity-driven cross-platform interactions” strategy, depending on the types of platforms involved and their competitive relations. When platforms impose negative information externalities on each other, they can align through a “meta-platform” or a “platform concatenation” strategy, depending on the types of platforms involved and their competitive relations.

Whether these strategies can be user-driven or not depends on the nature of the externality underlying them. In the case of strategies motivated by the internalization of negative information externalities (meta-platform and platform concatenation), and contrary to the strategies that solve matching failures (network fusion and user-driven cross-platform interactions), only platform owners, not users, can implement them. The reason is that the internalization of negative information externalities requires access to and the processing of vasts amounts of (protected) information. Hence, only through the cooperation between platforms (e.g, though a commercial partnership) and the deployment of technical means beyond those available to end users (e.g., APIs) can the meta-platform and platform-concatenation strategy be deployed. On the contrary, users located in different platforms wishing to interact can use the means provided by several platforms' architectures (e.g., cross-posting) to interact across platforms. Platform owners, in turn, may choose to facilitate (e.g., by providing a single user log-in or a crossposting functionality) these cross-platform interactions.

All the strategies behind DSIPEs lead to the generation of cross-platform non-generic complementarities, resulting in a joint value proposition around a cross-platform “focal user experience.” This contrasts with intra-platform ecosystems, in which the locus of joint value creation is the focal product: the platform. As in intra-platform ecosystems, in inter-platform ecosystems, a platform uses boundary resources to facilitate value creation by enabling third parties to build on it, interconnect and offer complementary services Gawer (2021). In the DSIPEs detected, Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) stand out as a key boundary resource.10 They act as formal control mechanisms defining how platform owners expose functionalities and data to external actors, enabling modularity and coordinated interactions (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). Boundary resources like APIs enable platforms not only to foster innovation but primarily to unlock and scale value jointly created across ecosystem participants by allowing seamless integration of distributed resources (Constantinides et al., 2018; Gawer, 2021).

Within the empirical demand-side inter-platform strategies studied in this section, APIs serve as crucial technological interfaces enabling real-time data integration, functionality sharing, and cross-platform user experiences. For example, when platform-owner-driven, the network fusion strategy relies on APIs like Trivago's Fast Connect to facilitate direct interaction between autonomous hotel booking platforms, while the meta-platform strategy leverages intermediaries such as NDC Exchange to overcome API standardization challenges in airline bookings. These APIs enable unlocking new value configurations jointly created by multiple independent platforms without merging governance structures.

3.1 | The network fusion strategy

When two types of users have preferences for competing transaction platforms and incentives to interact, there can be positive interaction externalities between platforms. In this case, user interactions in a platform increase the value of a competing platform to another type of user. In other words, there are cross-platform indirect network effects.

Consider the case of a generalist marketplace and a marketplace specialized in electronics. The first one attracts generalist consumers, while the second one attracts electronics-savvy consumers. Consequently, the electronics marketplace attracts more sellers of electronics than the generalist one. However, sometimes generalist users want to purchase electronics. In absence of cross-platform alignment, non-multihoming generalist consumers will not transact with nonmultihoming electronics sellers in the competing platform. Thus, there is a cross-platform matching failure. More generally, as shown in the “Pattern 1” column of Table A4, this failure occurs between (partially-) competing transaction platforms. Trivago is a lodging platform that offers both hotels and other lodging platforms the possibility to list their accommodation offering on its platform.11 When the network fusion strategy is platform-owner-driven, it is implemented through a technological interface allowing a platform to technically connect the two networks. For example, Trivago developed a tool called “trivago FastConnect.” It is an API that establishes a direct connection with platforms' booking engines or reservation systems. It also allows Trivago to request live prices and availability for lodgings whenever a user makes a search on Trivago. Some hosting platforms have developed their own technical solutions (e.g., TripAdvisor's TripConnect), while others rely on a third-party connector they tailor to their needs (e.g., Seznam's template for the third-party Shopify Feed solution). Platforms can also coordinate more deeply by allowing users to use a common log-in to interact in multiple platforms. This is the case of Willhaben (a generalist marketplace) and Landwirt (an agriculture machinery and related products marketplace). The two platforms have set up a partnership for Landwirt to have a dedicated section within Willhaben called “Farm Shop—Culinary” where users can buy food products from farmers that post their offerings on Landwirt while using a single account. All of these examples illustrate how platforms can use technical interfaces to create non-generic complementarities that support a joint value proposition built on a crossplatform focal user experience. In contrast, in none of the 29 non-DSIPE cases fitting Pattern 1 did we find evidence of the use of platforms using technical interfaces to interconnect. Alternatively, the network fusion strategy can be user-driven. This is the case of real estate platforms Daft/Myhome and of the price comparison tools Skroutz and Best Price. In none of these cases does a platform display listings from another one, nor does it offer that possibility to other platforms using a technical interface. The statistically relevant demand-side linkages depicted in Figure 1 result instead from user cross-posting. We found evidence of this in publicly available cached pages in these three within-DSIPE cases.12 This could be the case because one platform has a larger user base in a certain area than the other, or because its listing fees or commissions are higher for certain areas and types of users. Importantly, although platforms could easily impede or hinder user posting of a link to a competitor,13 they consciously choose not to. This shows that platforms accommodate, at least to a certain extent, user-driven complementarities that fuse their networks.

3.2 | User-community-driven cross-platform interactions

When complementary resources and/or user communities are split across multiple platforms, there can be positive interaction externalities between them. This happens when the value of a platform to multi-homing users increases with the interactions that take place within a user community in another platform. As in the case of the network fusion strategy, there are crossplatform network effects.

As shown in the “Pattern 2” column of Table A4, this failure occurs between a platform hosting an active user community and any other type of platform. In our sample, we observe that, as a result of this failure, the demand-side linkages between platforms outside a DSIPE are 15 percentage points lower in intensity than those inside a DSIPE. The inter-platform ecosystem around the gaming news and forum JeuxVidéo offers an illustration of this strategy. As shown by our analysis of cached pages of JeuxVidéo with links to Le Bon Coin and Vinted, many JeuxVidéo users are also active in Le Bon Coin (a generalist classifieds platform) and Vinted (a secondhand fashion marketplace). They use JeuxVidéo's forum to seek advice from its community regarding the use of these platforms (user experience, payment options, how to haggle with sellers, warnings against scams, etc.) and some of the products offered there. This is because these platforms do not offer a forum functionality where users can obtain this type of information. More importantly, the community they know, regularly engage with and likely trust better on this information, is located in another platform, JeuxVidéo. Similarly, JeuxVidéo users that are also present in the platform Doctissimo (a health news and forum platform) would like to discuss content produced by the Doctissimo forum community with nonmulti-homing users from JeuxVidéo. In absence of cross-platform coordination allowing JeuxVidéo users to share content from other platforms within their community, they face disincentives to interact with it, leading to a cross-platform matching failure. Similar types of interactions were found by analyzing the cached pages of JeuxVidéo linking to other platforms for which demand-side linkages of an intensity between 1% and 10% were found. However, as translated by the value of this indicator, these interactions are not sufficiently frequent to signal the existence of nongeneric complementarities in consumption between the platforms. For example, we found 8940 user posts in JeuxVidéo with links to the marketplace Cdiscount and 11,500 (i.e., 29% more) with links to Le Bon Coin.14 Moreover, Cdiscount received 36% more referral traffic in 2020 than Le Bon Coin.

3.3 | The meta-platform strategy

The co-existence of multiple competing platforms (ecosystems) offering distinct designs, information categorizations, price structures, services, and complementors increases the necessary cognitive effort multihoming users have to engage in to compare their offerings. This, coupled with the fragmentation of such information across platforms, translates into a negative information externality competing platforms impose on each other. The more fragmented and dissimilar the information is across competing platforms, the higher the search costs users face when comparing offerings on multiple platforms. This leads to less and lower-quality matches.

Consider the case of a user wanting to buy a smart TV online. She will try to find the brand and model that fits her needs and budget. In order to do so, she will compare offerings from generalist and specialized online marketplaces. Given that platforms differ in the type of information provided about each TV, their design, the prices at which the same brand and model is offered and other complementary services (e.g., warranty period, insurance costs, delivery conditions, etc.), the user faces search costs when comparing them. This might lead her not to (correctly) compare all the options and, as a result, to abandon the purchase or to settle for an a product that is not the best available match. 3.4 | The platform concatenation strategy

When platforms with a shared user base offer complementary products in an uncoordinated way, they impose a negative information externality on multi-homing users. Given the lack of inter-platform alignment, multi-homing users purchasing complementary products from different platforms face search costs.16

For example, a user planning a trip has to buy complementary services such as transportation and lodging. The user has to combine two services, typically offered by different platforms (a flight metasearch and/or an airline platform, on the one hand, and a lodging metasearch and/or a lodging platform, on the other hand) so as to fit a schedule and a budget. Similarly, in order to locate the airport, the hotel, a restaurant and tourist attractions booked or found in different platforms, a user can resort to an online maps platform to plan her itinerary. In absence of inter-platform alignment, after interacting with the flight metasearch platform, the user faces search costs to find an accommodation in a lodging platform that fits the flight she booked in the metasearch platform. As shown in the “Pattern 4” column of Table A4, this happens between various types of platforms that do not fit the patterns described in patterns 1, 2 and 3. Examples include but are not limited to marketplaces leading end users to delivery platforms and platforms offering special deals and discounts from participating retailers or tourism platforms leading to online mapping platforms. In our sample, we observe that, as a result of this failure, the demand-side linkages between platforms offering complementary products outside a DSIPE are 22 percentage points lower in intensity than those inside a DSIPE.

In order to minimize this negative information externality, platforms offering complementary products can align through a platform concatenation strategy. This strategy consists in a platform proposing to a user an offering from another platform that is customized to the service it has previously offered to her. Indeed, we observe this in the cached pages linking the platforms identified in the 30 within- and outside-DSIPE cases fitting pattern 3 in Table A4. For example, once a user purchases a flight ticket through Wizzair airline's platform, she is offered lodging accommodation that fits her schedule, budget and destination through the lodging platform Booking. Another example of this strategy is the embedding of Google Map's maps and information into the tourism platform TripAdvisor. In the latter, when a user consults a touristic point, a restaurant or an accommodation, a map from Google Maps with key pieces of information (address, telephone number, website, etc.) is embedded along with TripAdvisor's intra-platform content such as user reviews and rating. As the examples show, in this strategy each platform becomes the complementor of the platform that precedes it in the chain of services that constitute the user journey.

We have studied how platforms solve different types of cross-platform failures by aligning within demand-side inter-platform ecosystems. The strategies they employ to do so depend on the nature of the externalities that trigger these failures, the types of platforms involved and their competitive relationship. In this respect, our findings present similarities, but also many differences with intra-platform ecosystem theory. We summarize the comparison between the two in Table 3.

The differences between the features of intra- and (demand-side) inter-platform ecosystems open up a promising research agenda that extends platform ecosystem theory to (demand-side) inter-platform ecosystems in multiple directions. 4.1.1 | Pricing structure

It follows from the above that, within DSIPEs, the value of network membership to a platform's user (be it an end user or a complementor) depends on the decisions of users on other platforms. Thus, platform owners not only have to decide on a pricing structure that takes the interdependencies between the platforms' sides into account (as shown in the early platform literature Caillaud & Jullien, 2001; Hagiu, 2006; Rochet & Tirole, 2003; G. G. Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005), they also have to take into account cross-platform interdependencies. How does this affect the pricing structure of a platform that is part of an inter-platform ecosystem, as opposed to one that is not? Does the pricing strategy depend on the strategy underlying the cross-platform interdependencies?

4.1.2 | Determinants of user-driven DSIPEs

We have seen that when DSIPEs solve matching failures (as opposed to information externalities), both users and platform owners may take the lead in solving them. This contrasts with intraplatform ecosystems, in which users do not lead the creation of nongeneric complementarities. However, we still do not know when we should expect users or platform owners to take up this role. A hypothesis to be tested in this respect is that only when platforms are partial competitors do platform owners drive the strategy. Our sample confirms this hypothesis. In the cases identified in which the platforms' markets fully overlap (e.g., the real estate platforms Daft/Myhome, the price comparison tools Skroutz and Best Price) and in the cases in which the platforms are unrelated (the DSIPE to which Jeux Vidéo belongs) we find evidence of the network fusion strategy being userdriven. On the contrary, platform-driven strategies take place between partially-competing platforms such as Trivago (which offers a wide range of tourism-related services, including lodging) and lodging platforms, or between Willhaben (a generalist retail marketplace) and Landwirt (a machinery marketplace). A possible explanation could be that, although, as shown above, DSIPEs increase the value generated for end users, they might not allow platforms to capture it, or even decrease their value capture. In the case of directly competing platforms, the negative impact of increased competition with a rival platform (e.g., facilitating user migration to a rival platform) might outweigh the benefits (e.g., monetizing cross-platform interactions) associated with DSIPE. In the case of unrelated platforms, the technical costs of facilitating cross-platform interactions might be too high with respect to the capacity to monetize them, given the difference in the nature of the platforms involved. Partially-competing platforms could constitute a middle point in which solving matching failures increases the value captured by both the platform and end users, which in turn creates incentives for the former to lead the creation of a DSIPE, rather than users.

Second, if platforms are complementors to each other (as in the network fusion strategy, for example), it is not clear why one of the platforms would be the “natural,” sole orchestrator of the interplatform ecosystem. Third, digital interfaces, which constitute one of the boundary decisions of a platform (Gawer, 2021), are set up at the platform (not the DISPE) level. A focus on the role played by APIs in DSIPEs illuminates this last point.

In intra-platform ecosystems, APIs “define the degree of openness and the balance of directionality of the 2-way exchange of data between the platform and each of its sides” (Gawer, 2021). In DSIPEs, APIs also regulate inter-platform interactions. When platform A grants API access to B, and B connects to C, A loses direct control, creating decentralized API governance. This logic extends to other boundary resources (plugins, moderation policies), reducing any platform's control over critical ecosystem assets and transaction characteristics. Decentralized API control thus intensifies the inherent tension between enabling third-party value creation and maintaining control (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Yoo et al., 2012).

Moreover, DSIPEs' governance is more complex than in intra-platform ecosystems due to the existence of two layers of governance and transitive network effects. In as much as the participating platforms orchestrate their own intra-platform ecosystems (as it is the case of the vast majority of the platforms in our final sample), governance is two-layered. In the first layer, a platform owner orchestrates its intra-platform ecosystem. In the second layer, platforms have to decide how to govern the demand-side linkages that interrelate them at the DSIPE level. In addition, given that, as seen in Figure 1, inter-platform ecosystems can indirectly connect two platforms through a common third one, they exhibit “transitive network effects.” The latter occur when “when the relationship between two actor groups indirectly influences a third group, even though no direct link exists between the first and third parties” (Schmidt et al., 2024).

The complex nature of inter-platform ecosystems' governance opens up research questions to which case studies could contribute in answering. Does the decentralized governance of demand-side inter-platform ecosystems lead to the same functional ecosystem failures observed in other decentralized ecosystems (Hsieh & Vergne, 2023; Pidun et al., 2021)? Contrary to many decentralized ecosystems studied in the digital realm (e.g., ecosystems based on blockchain), DSIPEs do not suffer from the rigidity of a decentralized decision-making binding protocol. Nor do they seem to be in a technologically fast-paced environment requiring rapid decisionmaking.

Under which conditions do actors in inter-platform ecosystems set up governance instances, as in open-source communities (O'Mahony & Ferraro, 2007)? Recent studies of supply-side inter-platform ecosystems indicate that the platform supplying the “ecosystem good” plays the role of the orchestrator at the inter-platform level. In China, Alipay orchestrates an interplatform ecosystem based on its mobile payment service, which is embedded in multiple platforms (Zhang & Williamson, 2021). Similarly, ChatGPT uses plugins to integrate information generated by travel booking, journey planning and specialized travel platforms (Schmidt et al., 2024). However, in demand-side inter-platform ecosystems, as our sample shows, there is not an ecosystem good. 4.1.4 | Membership

It follows from the above that in demand-side inter-platform ecosystems membership is more fluid than in intra-platform ecosystems. In the former, after an end user or an intra-platform complementor joins a platform ecosystem, it can later also become (perhaps unwillingly) part of a DSIPE if the platform owner or end users (communities) implement one of the strategies studied in this article. How do platform owners decide the extent and the permeability of the inter-platform ecosystem memberships they create or accommodate? Although, as shown in this article, demand-side inter-platform ecosystems are solutions to endogenous cross-platform failures that benefit end users, they might also disadvantage intra-platform complementors if membership is too open. For example, by working with flight metasearch platforms, airlines' platforms face increased competition from other airlines. This can hurt their profits and their DSIPE complementors', such as associated lodging or car rental platforms. This takes us to the next avenue for further research: coopetition.

4.2 | Coopetition within inter-platform ecosystems

The literature has widely analyzed how, in intra-platform ecosystems, complementors can compete both with the platform owner and other complementors for value capture. This results in coopetition dynamics, as firms belonging to the same ecosystem need to simultaneously cooperate through the creation of nongeneric complementarities in order to create value and compete for value capture (Ansari et al., 2016; Gawer & Henderson, 2007; Hannah & Eisenhardt, 2018; Panico & Cennamo, 2022; Zhu & Liu, 2018).

Revisiting these findings in light of demand-side inter-platform ecosystems opens an interesting avenue for research, notably when the “network fusion” or the “meta-platform” strategy underpins platforms' interactions. Our findings suggest that, as a result of these strategies, not only inter-platform cooperation increases but also competition. In the case of the meta-platform strategy, competition between the platforms that complement the aggregating platform should increase, as the latter makes each platform's user base less captive. At the same time, interplatform cooperation results in a larger potential user base for each complementor. Platforms, in turn, prefer attracting users directly to avoid paying referral fees to the aggregating platform, as is the case of third-party vendors in transaction platforms (Hagiu & Wright, 2023). In the case of the network fusion strategy, competition between the complementors of each platform should intensify in conjunction with the expansion of the network size the fusion entails.

Moreover, the owners of the platforms making up the inter-platform ecosystem are likely to have different incentives on the balance between competition and cooperation at the inter-platform ecosystem level. What mechanisms can platforms set up to navigate the coopetitive dynamics at this level? Do they cooperate to find this balance? How does this affect platforms' architectural choices and their own intra-platform ecosystem coopetition dynamics?

This raises a larger question on the competitive strategies platforms might pursue within an inter-platform ecosystem. Two polar strategies can be envisioned. Platforms may try to develop into the gateway to other complementary platforms in order to become users' primary access point to the ecosystem, as in Heidhues et al. (2024). Alternatively, platforms could try to become ubiquitous, high-value complementors to all other platforms with a shared user base. We could expect the first strategy to be preferred by platforms that followed a meta-search platform or a platform concatenation strategy. In these strategies, the user journey begins at a single platform: the aggregator or the platform that triggers the purchase of subsequent complementary services, respectively. This should give more control and bargaining power to that platform and may lead to envelopment attempts by which the other platforms try to become the platform with privileged access to the inter-platform ecosystem's user base. In contrast, we could expect the DSIPEs that follow a user-community-driven cross-platform interactions strategy to be made of platforms opting for the second strategy, as there is no clear beginning of the user journey and the platforms are not active in overlapping markets.

A related avenue for research is the exploration of envelopment strategies in inter-platform ecosystems. Network fusion and platform envelopment strategies share some key characteristics. They both leverage overlapping user bases and common components. For instance, the marketplaces and price comparison platforms identified in this strategy share components such as product catalog and transaction management systems, seller management tools, review and rating mechanisms, and product data aggregation technologies (e.g., direct feeds, APIs, web scraping). Both strategies also seek to provide users with more functionalities than singleplatform experiences. As in envelopment attacks of weak substitutes, the platforms in the network fusion strategy “serve the same broad purpose but satisfy different sets of users needs” (Eisenmann et al., 2011), enabling them to exploit economies of scope in marketing to multihoming users.

However, the two strategies differ in how they achieve these objectives and in the nature of the inter-platform relations they entail. In the network fusion strategy, platforms cooperate by facilitating cross-platform interactions between multi-homing users. Envelopment, by contrast, involves a unilateral decision by a platform (the attacker) to bundle its functionalities with some of the target platforms'. In doing so, it seeks to gain a competitive advantage over the target platform and reduce users' incentives to multihome (Eisenmann et al., 2011).

4.3 | Inter-platform ecosystems' performance

We have shown that demand-side inter-platform ecosystems solve either cross-platform information externalities or cross-platform matching failures. In doing so, they spur interactions that would not have taken place otherwise, which should increase value creation. However, we do not know what the impact is of DSIPEs on each platform's value capture and its performance. Let us first consider market shares as a measure of performance. Consider the case of two online retail platforms, H and S, following the network fusion strategy. Platform H hosts listings from platform S. Cross-platform network effects make platform S more valuable to sellers, as they can access buyers from both platforms. Conversely, they make platform H more valuable to consumers, as they can access both platforms' sellers. It is a priori ambiguous which platform's seller and consumer user base will increase or decrease as a result. The extent of pre- DSIPE multihoming by consumers, on the one hand, and sellers, on the other hand, could also alter these outcomes. The more multihoming by a user group there is pre-DSIPE, the smaller the benefits of introducing cross-platform indirect network effects are for the other user group. Turning to revenues, the effect of DSIPEs becomes less clear when considering that platform H typically charges referral fees to platform S. Although increased access to the other user group could enable both platforms to raise prices (H to consumers, S to sellers), platforms might instead decrease prices or increase them moderately to avoid losing users to competitors. This ultimately depends on price sensitivity, network benefits of each user type, and pre-DSIPE competition intensity.

The limitations of this article's approach do not allow us to assess the effect of DSIPEs on performance. A theoretical model could help in distilling conditions under which increases or decreases in each platform's performance are expected. A longitudinal dataset with platforms' user bases, referral traffic, and other performance (e.g., market shares, revenues, profits, market capitalization, etc.) and control variables could allow us to test them empirically.

Moreover, it would be interesting to understand how the features of the DSIPEs could boost performance. Do some strategies lead to superior value creation? For example, we could hypothesize that the platform concatenation strategy should lead to superior value creation compared to other strategies, as platforms' services are complementary and firms are not exposed to increased competition within the DSIPE. How is performance affected by the number of complementing platforms? And by the intensity of demand-side linkages? Does the structure of the network of platforms making up the inter-platform ecosystem (cf. Figure 1) play a role?

Our objective in this article has been to extend the theory of platform ecosystems to the unexplored but relevant case of demand-side inter-platform ecosystems. As this section shows, our contribution is a primer that raises many questions that remain to be answered. We hope the directions for further research pointed to will nurture the strategy literature and help it increase our understanding of platform ecosystems.

Calculation of a proxy of demand-side nongeneric complementarities and choice of a threshold

This subsection provides further methodological details about how we calculated a proxy of demand-side nongeneric complementarities and determined a threshold value of this indicator to differentiate occasional from significant cross-platform traffic.

Exclusion of non-referral traffic

In Section 2.2, we explained that we only take into consideration referral traffic in our analysis, as it is the only type of traffic that translates the notion of nongeneric complementarities in consumption on which the concept of ecosystems builds. Here we provide further details on the other categories of traffic and why we decided to exclude them from the analysis.

“Direct” refers to traffic coming from users typing the URL of the website into a browser, using a link saved in a bookmark or coming from outside of the browser (e.g., a hyperlink in a Word file). In that regard, it does not entail any type of complementarity with another website. “Mail” and “Social” (the latter referring to traffic sent from social media platforms such as Facebook) traffic implies that individuals and organizations use a communication service nested in a platform to share links to another one. We do not consider this to imply that these actors are making a specific investment and hence does not imply that there are nongeneric complementarities between the two platforms. In an offline context, this would be analogous to using a telephone to order a pizza: neither the pizza shop nor the telephone company have done any specific investment to jointly create the value coming from combining their services. Like the telephone in this example, emails and social networks are general-purpose technologies that, although complementary to many other, do not require firms to do specific investments in order to generate these complementarities. Note in that respect that traffic originated in themed online communities such as the French-speaking videogame website and online forum “JeuxVidéo” are not classified as “Social.” This is consistent with our identification of nongeneric complementarities in consumption. Indeed, platforms such as JeuxVidéo do a specific investment to gather and entertain a specific type of user base that can only increase the value of certain other types of platforms with which the attention and input of this particular community is complementary.

Platforms sending the traffic simply show the ad of the company that paid the most for the eyeballs of a particular type of user (e.g., a male 20–25 years old that likes motorbikes). This does not entail any type of specific investment from the platform sending or receiving the traffic.

Once we eliminated all non-referral traffic, we corrected the dataset by eliminating traffic that was (mis)categorized as “referral traffic” coming from generalist search engines such as Google or from generalist social networks such as Facebook. In the latter case, referral traffic distinguishes clicks on links sent through these platforms' messaging services as opposed to clicks appearing on a timeline. However, the same reasoning applies. Given that there is no specific investment from a platform such as Facebook for people to connect to other platforms through links sent on Messenger, we exclude this traffic from the sample.

Determination of a threshold value to differentiate occasional from significant cross-platform traffic

The classification of a certain share of total received referral traffic by platform A from platform B as nongeneric complementarities in consumption between them requires distinguishing incidental traffic (e.g., platform B sending only three visits per year to platform A) from significant traffic. Only the latter should be considered to reveal nongeneric complementarities in consumption. A threshold of the share of total received referral traffic by a platform (cf. Equation 1) needs to be determined to make that distinction. In principle, this threshold could be determined qualitatively on the basis of the analysis of traffic between platforms that are known to be complementary, similarly to market share thresholds indicating dominance in a market, which are established on the basis of past antitrust cases. We lack such information. Moreover, a qualitative approach would put a blind spot in demand-side linkages between platforms that could not be expected to be complementary. As our results show, these cases exist. Hence, we adopt an empirical approach.

Detection of matching problems and coordination and cooperation patterns

In this subsection, we explain how we empirically detect signs of matching problems and obtain information on the nature of the inter-platform coordination and cooperation strategies employed to solve them. To do so, we proceed in the following way. We begin by classifying platforms in terms of the main (sub)topic they cover and the (sub)service they provide, as well as regarding their competitive relations. This results in four patterns of demand-side linkages. For each pattern, we compute the difference between the intensity of demand-side linkages between the platforms (cf. Equation 1) previously identified as being within and outside a DSIPE to proxy the extent of cross-platform failures for each pattern in our sample. Then, for each pattern, we study the nature of the demand-side linkages patterns and the platforms involved. Furthermore, we analyze the cached pages containing links between the origin and the destination platform for within and outside DSIPE cases to obtain additional information on the nature of these failures and how they are solved.

Limitation of the original dataset

The major limitation of the original dataset is that it only captures desktop browser traffic. As a consequence, platforms such as food delivery platforms in which mobile app use is more relevant are underrepresented. However, mobile apps rarely link to each other or to external websites. Consequently, demand-side linkages would be more difficult to find using mobile app data. Moreover, the data do not allow us to distinguish business users from final users traffic. Hence, we cannot take the two-sideness of platforms (intra-platform demand-side linkages) into account in our analysis.
