In the Company of Strangers: Social Influence from Anonymous Peers in Online Game Settings

WOOYONG JO SARANG SUNDER JEONGHYE CHOI MINAKSHI TRIVEDI

Social interactions among consumers, especially in the consumption of digital goods, have become commonplace in an increasingly interconnected market place. As such, understanding the impact of such interactions on consumer behavior carries major academic and practitioner significance. This research focuses on an important but under-researched aspect of social influence and the underlying mechanisms that may be driving peer effects. Using the context of a massive online peer-to-peer game, we estimate peer effects arising from anony mous peers. The findings suggest a robust and significant influence of anonymous peers on one’s own purchase behavior. Further, we find that peer effects arising from anonymous peers are largely driven by informational reasons as opposed to competitive concern even in a competitive online gaming context. That is, players rely on others’ purchase decisions despite anonymity; moreover, such influence is more pronounced for inexperienced players with little contextual knowledge. To demonstrate the effectiveness of leveraging the nature of peer influence in online game settings, we provide an illustrative example using an intervention simulation. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings for marketing academia and practitioners in the video game industry.

Keywords: peer effects, social influence, informational influence, competitive con cern, video game

INTRODUCTION H

uman beings are inherently social. Our daily lives are often defined by social interactions with friends, fam ily, as well as random happenstance interactions with strangers. Much of what is known about social interactions or social influence has to do with interactions with known peers, such as ties that individuals actively form with friends, colleagues, family, etc. (Goldenberg et al. 2009; Hartmann et al. 2008; Iyengar, Van den Bulte, and Valente 2011b; Sunder, Kim, and Yorkston 2019; Zhang and Godes 2018). However, one’s interactions need not only be con fined to known individuals. Often, people encounter ran dom strangers for brief moments of time. Such interactions are ubiquitous in offline settings (e.g., interactions with shoppers at retail stores, Uber drivers, service personnel, crowds at stadiums, etc.) and are ever increasing in online spaces such as gaming, metaverse, streaming, etc. (Bollinger et al. 2022; Gardete 2015; Lanz et al. 2019; Lusch 2017; McShane, Bradlow and Berger 2012; Shen, Malter and Rindfleisch 2023). Indeed, almost every online game released recently has a “social gaming” compo nent—one where players may collaborate with or compete against other players. For example, Counter-Strike: Global Offensive is a popular game where players collaborate toward a shared goal. Often these “teams” are formed by players being matched randomly with one another. On the other hand, Chess.com allows adversarial interactions where individuals compete against randomly chosen anon ymous opponents (Dean 2020). In fact, such one-on-one “random” matches have become a mainstay feature for most online games, including popular titles such as Halo, Mortal Kombat, EA sports’ FIFA series, etc. However, do such happenstance/random interactions actually influence individual behavior?

Given the ubiquitous nature of such interactions, espe cially in online settings, the potential influence of strangers on consumer behavior becomes a critically important ques tion from both an academic and managerial perspective. Surprisingly, however, there is a paucity of academic research investigating these effects in an online context, with little to no guidance on the theoretical underpinnings of these effects, especially from an empirical standpoint. While strangers can influence our behaviors in conven tional offline environments, much of what is known comes from experimental/survey-based analyses in offline con texts such as retail stores (Argo, Dahl, and Manchanda 2005; Blanchard, Dyachenko, and Kettle 2020; Ferraro, Bettman, and Chartrand 2009; Martin 2012). The few empirical works on the topic typically treat peer effects in the aggregate (e.g., crowd influences, new product diffu sion, etc.) and/or are focused on offline/durable goods con sumption (Bollinger et al. 2022; Bollinger and Gillingham 2012; Gardete 2015; McShane et al. 2012). Nevertheless, despite the prevalence of stranger interactions in online

contexts, there is no dedicated investigation into the role of strangers in online environments, whether such effects exist and why. This broadly forms the motivation for this research: to empirically document the effect of unknown individuals in an online gaming context, and, more impor tantly, to examine the underlying theoretical mechanisms that may be driving the effect.

The focus on identifying and empirically testing the underlying theoretical mechanisms is particularly important, both from a consumer behavior perspective and a manage rial one. Academic research on peer influence has proposed multiple mechanisms of social learning and normative influ ence. However, empirical research has been inconclusive regarding how the effects play out in the field, especially in the online context. Much of what we know comes from new product diffusion, offline settings, and durable goods con sumption. For instance, in the offline context, some have found that classical social influence theories, such as infor mational or normative influence, do not explain peer influ ence (Gardete 2015), while others have found evidence of status signaling (Hinz, Spann, and Hann 2015; Hu and Van den Bulte 2014), opinion leadership (Iyengar et al. 2011b), and information cascades (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch 1992; McShane et al. 2012). From a manager’s point of view, knowing the theoretical underpinnings of such peer effects helps to effectively leverage their impact in the field. The purpose of this article then is to extend our understand ing of social influence arising from stranger interactions to the online setting, especially given the prevalent consumer behavior and managerial focus on social consumption in online contexts.

Using purchase and game log data from a large (compet itive) multiplayer videogame, and leveraging key contex tual factors that enable empirical identification, we find robust evidence of the “stranger” effect in online purchase behavior. In terms of dollar value, a 10% increase in spend ing behavior among anonymous peers can lead to a 0.70% increase in the focal individual’s spending ($), highlighting the relevance of happenstance interactions between indi viduals in online contexts. Clearly, strangers do indeed have a profound effect on consumer behavior.

More importantly, we develop and empirically test two potential mechanisms that may underlie peer effects— informational influence and competitive concern. Contrary to expectations, given the adversarial nature of the focal game, we find that the primary mechanism is not a concern with winning or losing but, rather, the perceived informa tional value in others’ purchases. That is, individuals rely on the informational value embedded in peer behaviors regardless of anonymity to inform their own decisions.

In this research, we make two important contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the peer effects and social influence literature by examining a unique type of social interactions that are happenstance/random. While some behavioral studies have considered the valence of con versations between close friends and distant acquaintances (Chen 2017; Dubois, Bonezzi, and De Angelis 2016), few studies have considered the consequences of interactions with anonymous peers on one’s purchase decisions. We find that, even in an adversarial/competitive setting, strangers exert influence on others through the perceived information embedded in their purchases (the informational influence mechanism). We show that managers, particularly video game and app developers, can leverage peer effects stemming from infor mational influence to implement creative game designs and targeted strategies that promote social consumption.

LITERATURE AND THEORY

The Influence of Strangers

While the effect of peers on an individual’s behavior has been extensively studied in marketing, management, eco nomics, and other social science disciplines (Ameri, Honka, and Xie 2019; Du and Kamakura 2011; Gardete 2015; Hu and Van den Bulte 2014; Iyengar et al. 2015; McShane et al. 2012; Zhang 2010), the focus of our research is on the literature addressing strangers’ peer influence. Unlike the aggregate crowd and known peers, behaviors of anonymous peers (or strangers) are distinct in that the agents do not have prior relationships and have lit tle chance of interacting again in the future. Their opinions may not be reflective of the overall community or local

neighborhood (Ameri et al. 2019; Du and Kamakura 2011; Sunder et al. 2019) and they are less likely to exert the influence required to comply with social norms. As such, strangers’ social influence may be uniquely different from the aggregate crowd or known peer effects highlighted in prior works (Bollinger et al. 2022; Iyengar et al. 2015; McShane et al. 2012; Narayanan and Nair 2013). Furthermore, anonymous peers or strangers, unlike friends, may not share product preferences (Zhang, Liu, and Chen 2015) or mutual trust (Zhang and Godes 2018). It is unclear then whether conventional theories on social influence would apply in the case of stranger interactions. Figure 1 describes the conceptual differences in the peer effect liter ature. Note that our focus is on the middle box of figure 1— the impact of strangers on behavior in online settings.

A small stream of work—much of it conceptual— explores interactions with strangers. In a conceptual piece, Lusch (2017) suggests that individuals may be influenced by day-to-day interactions with strangers (such as taking a flight, checking into a hotel, etc.), a sentiment also echoed strongly in digital contexts by Shen et al. (2023). Much of what is known about the effects of stranger interactions comes from behavioral work in offline contexts (such as retail). For example, Argo et al. (2005) demonstrate that consumers’ emotions and self-presentation behaviors may be governed by the presence of strangers in a retail environ ment. Chen (2017) shows that consumers tend to self- enhance when communicating with strangers, as opposed to connecting emotionally (e.g., sharing personal events) with friends. Consumers may also modify their consumption pat terns in the presence of strangers. Ferraro et al. (2009) show, through a series of experiments, that consumers are more likely to choose a brand when they see a stranger using the same brand. While interactions with friends or known peers may be governed by communal norms such as interdepend ence and reciprocity, interactions with strangers may be gov erned by exchange norms such as self-interest (Clark and Mills 1979). Empirical work studying the role of strangers largely comes from offline contexts. Gardete (2015) explores peer effects in offline purchase behavior using data from airline purchases. In the durable goods market, McShane et al. (2012) find significant peer effects largely driven by visibility, a result that was also evident in the adoption of solar technology (Bollinger et al. 2022).

In online contexts, much of what is known about peer influence comes from studying “friend” networks or known peers (see table 1 for a selected review of recent literature). As we elaborate above, influence from known peers is likely to be different compared to happenstance online interactions with strangers. Whether such interactions have an impact on consumer behavior (especially in an adversa rial gaming context) remains an open question. If such interactions do indeed influence behavior, the underlying theoretical mechanisms call for more research—a primary objective of this article.

Underlying Theoretical Mechanisms of Peer Effects

Numerous theories have been proposed to explain social influence (Deutsch and Gerard 1955; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001). Broadly, the theoretical mechanisms driving peer effects may be classified into (1) informational influ ence (in which individuals infer quality signals through peer behaviors), (2) competitive concern (in which individ uals perceive a status/performance disadvantage in not complying with peer behaviors), (3) normative influence (in which individuals conform to and comply with in-group norms), and (4) network effects (in which benefits accrue as the number of peers increases). The institutional setup of our empirical context, a competitive one-on-one video game, is such that normative and network influence are unlikely. In the interest of completeness, however, we describe the mechanisms in brief below and discuss their relevance to the focal research context.

Informational Influence. Informational influence occurs when an individual relies on information from his/ her peers in an effort to reduce the uncertainty of an out come (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch 1998; Deutsch and Gerard 1955). As such, the informational influence or social learning mechanism suggests that peo ple learn from others’ actions and converge on similar behaviors. The impact of informational influence depends on the perceived value of the outcome and the credibility of the peer as a source. As a result, peer effects driven by social learning/informational influence are stronger when individuals perceive higher uncertainty and weaker when individuals are knowledgeable or are opinion leaders

(Iyengar et al. 2011b; Nair, Manchanda, and Bhatia 2010; Sunder et al. 2019). Thus, as individuals accrue contextual knowledge and expertise, they become more certain about their preferences/decisions and therefore may not need to rely on peers as much.

Competitive Concern. Another mechanism that offers a compelling rationale for social influence (especially in a competitive context) is the role of competitive concern. Purchases or innovation adoptions by one’s opponents may offer a competitive edge unless one adopts as well, which can lead to peer effects driven by competitive concern (Burt 1987; Hannan and McDowell 1987; Jo 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001; Van den Bulte and Wuyts 2009). Early applications come from studies focused on innovation adoption (Burt 1987; Hannan and McDowell 1984a, 1984b, 1987), where an agent’s adoption decision is modeled as a function of previous “structurally equivalent” (or similar) peers’ decisions. Relatedly, competitive con cern can also stem from status signaling behavior; that is, when an individual’s social status or position within a social structure is threatened (Bronnenberg and Mela 2004; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001). Indeed, these status effects may be nonlinear. Hu and Van den Bulte (2014) show that, in medical device adoption behavior, competitive concern is most pronounced for mid- status individuals due to anxiety associated with their status and conformity. In online contexts, Hinz et al. (2015) dem onstrate that conspicuous consumption increases social sta tus, and Lanz et al. (2019) take advantage of the effect when developing seeding strategies. Normative Influence and Network Effects. Normative influence (or normative pressure) denotes a conformation to an in-group norm and expectation (Deutsch and Gerard 1955; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001; Van den Bulte and Wuyts 2009). Examples of such pressures may include product choices (Wooten and Reed 2004) and sustainable behaviors (White and Simpson 2013), among others. Much of the research on normative pressures has its origins in conformity studies (Asch 1953), where normative influence occurs when there is a pressure to conform and a cost of deviating from the norm. Generally, the cost of divergence is amplified when mem bers belong to in-group networks (Bikhchandani et al. 1992). The stronger the in-group ties, the higher the pres sure to conform (Mackie and Cooper 1984) and the higher the barriers for dissention (Harmeling et al. 2017). Normative influence is unlikely in our setting because of the lack of strong in-group ties among users.1 Players in the focal game rarely form friendships with others, and there are very few opportunities for the game’s community to exert influence on individual users. Similarly, network effects exist when there are shared benefits to individual behaviors such that an individual’s purchase would enhance the benefits for others in the social network. Such network effects do not operate in this competitive gaming setting. Thus, we rule out normative influence and network effects as potential mechanisms.

We therefore explore two main theoretical mechanisms that may be driving peer effects in this context—informa tional influence and competitive concern (note that we do empirically rule out “normative influence” in the Robustness Checks and Supplementary Analyses section). The above theoretical mechanisms are conceptually distinct, suggesting the need to distinguish them from an empirical standpoint. Next, we describe the empirical context and data that form the basis of our investigation. Following this, we discuss our strategy to identify anonymous peer effects using observatio nal data and then ascertain the underlying mechanisms through theory-driven empirical testing (Iyengar, Van den Bulte, and Choi 2011a).

EMPIRICAL CONTEXT AND DATA

Empirical Context: Online Video Game

The data come from one of the largest online game pub lishers in South Korea that wishes to remain anonymous.

The focal game is categorized as a sports management game in which users manage a baseball team (by designing ath letes, finances, contracts, coaching strategy, etc.). The data include individual-level playing and spending logs of 4,000 randomly drawn users for 8 months between July 2011 and February 2012 (32 weeks in total). The focal game is consid ered a freemium product where customers play for free but can make in-game purchases that may enhance performance (e.g., defense skill boosters) or change aesthetic features (e.g., uniforms or logo colors). There are a total of 63 unique game items that can be purchased (32 functional items that enhance performance and 31 that are aesthetic). More than 90% of the total observed revenues come from functional item purchases such as defense boosts and other perform ance enhancements. Once used in a game, the item expires and cannot be used again. The weekly proportion of users making voluntary purchases is about 14%, and conditional on making purchases, the average spending per purchase is $2.984, with the median being $3.

Players can enjoy the game in two modes: single play mode (played against bots) or social play mode (played against anonymous individual peers). By default, all players begin in the single play mode where they can choose from a curated list of teams and rebrand the team according to their own tastes. For instance, a player can manage the composi tion of the team, position team members, set up additional training for the team, and recruit promising rookies. The player’s team is automatically included to participate in a weekly virtual sports league (single play), and the players are rewarded based on their team’s performance. In the social play mode, players opt in to play one-on-one matches with other anonymous players. If a player opts in by clicking a queue for social matches, then the game server randomly matches her/him to another anonymous player that also wishes to have a social match.

The key difference between the social play mode and the single play mode is that in the former, players can interact with the opponent on a real-time basis. Since social matches are free for any player, they are quite popular. About 99% of our sample (4,000 players) participated in at least one social match during our observation period. On average, players participated in social matches for about 13.27 weeks out of the 32-week period, making the weekly average participation rate 41%. The game publisher pro vided detailed social match logs between players enabling us to track peer behaviors.2 These logs contain complete information on all social matches stored on the game server, including data for players not included in our 4,000- user dataset. Challenges in Identification of Peer Effects

Estimating causal peer effects using observational (nonexperimental) data faces three well-known challenges: endogenous group formation (i.e., homophily), simultane ity, and correlated unobservables (Manski 1993). For instance, game users may opt to play social games at spe cific times of the day, thus matching with players who have similar time preferences. If purchase decisions are in part correlated with the time of gameplay (e.g., night-time gamers are heavy spenders), then endogeneity bias in group formation may still be a concern. Additionally, players of similar expertise levels (indicated by their tiers) may have varying propensities for joining the social match queue, which could also lead to endogenous group formation.

To examine this, we revisit the raw social match logs (conditional on social match participation) and plot the combinations of users’ tiers in panel A of figure 2. The user tier represents the rank of each player in the focal game, reflecting their performance. The figure suggests two insights. First, the plot shows that all kinds of tier com binations are present in social match logs (marked as black dots), confirming the firm’s description of social matches where all kinds of matches are possible. Second, however, when we draw the contour lines of density (in blue), they indicate frequent matches along the 45-degree line of the map (see web appendix A1 for a heatmap figure). While part of this can be the result of a high concentration of players in the middle tiers (between tiers 4 and 12, as revealed by marginal distributions), these high-density areas may suggest that even though the firm’s pairing strat egy is purportedly random, the actual data patterns suggest the social matches are not entirely randomly determined.

There may be other confounding factors influencing the formation of peer groups. In panel B of figure 2, we also compute the distribution of cases where players are matched with the same tiers (jDiffj ¼ 0), one- or two-tier differences (jDiffj ¼ 1 or 2), and more than two tier

differences (jDiffj > 2). As shown in panel B, a significant portion of social matches occur with opponents of the same tier (37.18%), which motivates us to directly address the potential endogenous formation of social matches.

To address this issue, we exploit the availability of intransitive triads or partially overlapping pairs of ties in a player’s network to create exclusion restrictions (Bramoulle et al. 2009). The intuition behind intransitive triads is that the characteristics of peers’ peers who are not in the focal player’s network can be viable instruments for peer exposure (Shi, Grewal, and Sridhar 2021; Sunder et al. 2019). Figure 3 is an illustration of this using a six-person network. If our goal is to estimate the causal effect of users B and C on A, then the behaviors of users D, E, and F (who are connected to B and C, but not to A) can be used as instruments. In our case, the behavior in question is pur chase amount. Therefore, we use as our instrument the average purchase amount of peers’ peers in week t–2 who have not interacted with the focal user.

In our application, we employ a control function techni que since it provides a more robust solution to the endoge neity problem than predicting the endogenous variable directly from the instrument (Papies, Ebbes, and Van Heerde 2017; Wooldridge 2015). This approach is also known for its efficiency in handling interaction terms of potentially endogenous variables, which we will perform in our subsequent analyses (Papies et al. 2017). Specifically, we derive the residual by regressing the endogenous varia ble (the peer effect variable) on an instrument and a set of control variables. Then, we include the residual (i.e., the control function) as an additional regressor in our main equation to account for the possible endogeneity of the peer effect variable. This approach further helps prevent the need for additional instrumental variables (IV, here after) in the presence of interacted endogenous variables (see web appendix B for details).

Research Question 2: What Drives Peer Effects?

Recall that there are two competing theoretical mecha nisms that may drive peer effects—informational influence and competitive concern. We empirically test both and ascertain the dominant mechanism that drives peer effects arising from anonymous peers. We begin by discussing the test strategies for each mechanism and then provide the empirical findings.

Test Strategy for Informational Influence. Previous research defines informational influence as learning from others by updating one’s beliefs about the costs and bene fits of product adoption or purchase (Iyengar et al. 2015; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001; Van den Bulte and Wuyts 2009). In other words, the more knowledgeable an individual is, the less likely they are to be susceptible to peer behaviors. Given that peer effects are driven by informational benefits, the extent of herding behavior should gradually disappear as players have less to learn from peers. As such, her expertise may be measured by the number of cumulative visits made to the game (first measure) and the number of cumulative social matches she participated in (second measure). Second, we consider a player’s tenure or time since sign-up as it can approximate the user’s knowl edge or expertise within the focal video game (third meas ure). Test Strategy for Competitive Concern. Competitive concern refers to one’s reaction to the disadvantage, threat, or concern imposed by a competitor’s product purchases or innovation adoptions (Burt 1987). Such concern is likely to be amplified when an individual perceives a threat to their social status (Bronnenberg and Mela 2004; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001; Van den Bulte and Wuyts 2009). Extending this literature to our context, if competitive concern indeed operates, we would expect peer effects to be stronger when players compete against superior peer players (i.e., players in higher game tiers). The rationale is that when players compete against peers with superior gaming skills due to imbalances in the match ing process, they are more likely to lose than win, and possible failures may raise concerns about their social status (Behnke, Kosakowski, and Kaczmarek 2020). Thus, social interactions with superior players have the potential to amplify peer influence for competitive reasons. To examine this, we interact the main peer effect variable Peer exposureit−1 ð Þ with the proportion of social matches with peers of greater competence (% inferior matchesit−1). Specifically, we determine the social match as an “inferior match” if the focal user’s game tier is lower than that of the opponent. By construction, the “inferior match” variable ranges between zero and one, with a mean of 0.149 and an SD of 0.227. A positive and significant interaction would then suggest competitive con cern as a potential mechanism for peer effects. For robust ness, we define inferior matches more strictly by using the tier gap between two players exceeding one and two (web appendix D).

Cragg’s Two-Part Hurdle Model. The positive influ ence of peers’ spending could arise from an increase in the focal player’s purchase incidences (extensive margin), an increase in the player’s purchase amounts (intensive mar gin) conditional on making purchases, or both (Jo and Lewis 2025). To disentangle the effects, we apply a Cragg’s two-part hurdle model. Specifically, we first esti mate the weekly purchase incidence (Y > 0 vs. Y ¼ 0) as the first stage in a linear probability model and then estimate the purchase amount conditional on purchase (Y j Y > 0). The second part (after the purchase hurdle) of the analysis is performed on the observations where players made at least one purchase; thus, we use the simple log-scaled pur chase amount ($) without adding one as a dependent varia ble. Both stages are estimated separately and maintain the same structure to control for unobserved heterogeneity: player and weekly time-fixed effects.

Table 7 reports the results of estimation (full estimates are available in web appendix F). The results suggest that the positive influence of anonymous peers is present in the

purchase incidence stage but not in the purchase amount stage. This implies that peer effects in video gaming likely motivate players to make more frequent purchases but do not necessarily increase the per-purchase margin of their spending. Further, peer effects diminish as one’s contextual knowledge grows at the purchase incidence stage, indicat ing continued evidence suggestive of the informational influence mechanism, as we theorize. The results obtained from this additional analysis offer several implications. First, peer influence from strangers can motivate individuals to increase purchase frequency but not necessarily spend more than they used to on each purchase. This is an important finding because motivating customers to make repeat purchases is a crucial goal in freemium services. Second, the diminishing peer effects with increased contextual knowledge implies that players with less knowledge become more motivated to try paid game items when exposed to their peers’ purchases. The firm may consider framing promotional messages to these groups to highlight the benefits of purchase opportunities, rather than nudging them to buy more expensive game items.

Examining Potentially Masked Competitive Effects. In addition to the outcome-based analysis for testing competi tive concern, we examine two possible scenarios where competitive concern might be masked by other factors. First, our main analyses may fail to detect evidence because players face different situations even with similar levels of threats to status (% inferior matchit–1). Players may feel low suspense when the opponent’s tier is far

greater than theirs (low uncertainty in outcome) and high suspense when the opponent’s tier is slightly greater (high uncertainty in outcome). If competitive concern is masked by this heterogeneity in suspense, it requires further clarifi cation and empirical investigation. We compute the root mean squared tier difference between the focal player’s tier and the opponents’ tiers each week and multiply it by minus one to measure experienced suspense as a moderator (Simonov, Ursu, and Zheng 2023). A positive and signifi cant moderation would suggest competitive concern when suspense is high. However, we find that peer exposure is not significantly moderated by experienced suspense, rul ing out the possibility that competitive concern is at play here. Relevant details, results, and robustness checks are reported in web appendix G2.

Second, peer influence driven by competitive concern may be non-linear, as suggested by Hu and Van den Bulte (2014). Peer influence may be strongest for those in middle status due to mid-status anxiety. We use the weekly tier of each player to indicate their status and include both quad ratic and linear terms of the status variable, interacting them with the peer exposure variable. If mid-status anxiety, a component of competitive concern, is present, the impact of peer exposure should be moderated in an inverted U- shaped manner by one’s status. As reported in web appen dix G3, we do not find that one’s status or its square term significantly moderates the positive effect of peer expo sure. Taken together, the above results offer strong evi dence ruling out competitive concern as a theoretical mechanism driving peer effects.

Test for Normative Influence. Although normative influence is excluded as an underlying mechanism, we do nevertheless confirm this with empirical evidence. Previous studies suggest that normative influence is mainly driven by one’s desire to conform to group norms or expectations (Deutsch and Gerard 1955; Iyengar et al. 2015; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001). Since such need for conformation increases as one’s peer network expands (Iyengar et al. 2015; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2015), we examine whether the magnitude of the estimated peer effect varies by the size of the peer group. Specifically, we interact the main peer effect variable with the number of social matches and anonymous peers encountered in week t–1. If players con form to their peers because of normative pressure, then the greater the number of anonymous peers they interact with, the greater will be the influence of their purchases (Zhang et al. 2015). Thus, the interaction term will have a positive and significant effect if normative influence is at work. To test this, we include three interaction terms for three under lying mechanisms—informational influence, competitive concern, and normative influence—and repeat the analysis. We confirm that interaction terms for normative influence are non-significant (web appendix I).

Poisson Fixed Effect Model. To account for the fre quent occurrence of non-purchases in free-to-play games (i.e., zero spending), we re-estimate the main model with a Poisson fixed effect model using purchase frequency as a dependent variable (Wang and Goldfarb 2017). We confirm that all our findings remain consistent (web appendix J).

Accounting for Item Reloading. If players consume paid game items at a reasonable rate in social matches, par ticipating in more social matches may require more pur chases of game items in the following weeks (because the game items are perishable and immediately consumed). To account for such reloading of game items in modeling peer influence, we replace visit frequency with the number of social matches participated in during the preceding week as a control variable. ADDITIONAL ANALYSES

Heterogeneity in Peer Effects: Product Salience Perspective

A natural extension of our findings is to examine hetero geneity in the effects, focusing on the salience of the pur chased items. Prior literature on peer effects, primarily in offline settings, has pointed to visibility or salience being an important driver of social influence (Bollinger et al. 2022; McShane et al. 2012; Rogers 1995). Our goal is two fold: (1) to provide evidence of whether visibility/salience plays a significant role in online game settings and (2) to reaffirm the mechanism identified empirically. Specifically, item salience adds nuance to our main peer influence analyses by illustrating how peer learning occurs. If our theorizing is correct—that informational influence is the primary driver of the stranger peer effect—then the effect should be more pronounced when the information is more salient (i.e., purchased items are easily visible), because item purchase prominently signals peer preferen ces and item effectiveness during matches. Alternatively, if competitive concern is at work, we might expect a more pronounced peer influence when salience is low, since dis covering these items requires investigation into others’ strategies to improve one’s status.

To test the role of salience, we categorize the purchased items based on their visibility to the focal gamer: more sali ent items that are always displayed during the social match and less salient items that only appear when the focal player clicks on “additional information” on an opponent’s profile. Out of the 63 unique items purchased by users dur ing our observation period, 34 are directly visible on the social match screen (“high salience”), while the remaining 29 are only visible when the “additional information” but ton is clicked (“low salience”). Using this categorization, we divide the peer exposure variable into two distinct vari ables: exposure from purchases of more visible items and exposure from purchases of less visible items.

The results (table 8) suggest that peer influence is most pronounced for more salient items, reaffirming our main theorizing that informational influence is the underlying mechanism driving peer effects from strangers—a nod to prior findings in offline durable goods or technology inno vation adoption contexts (Bollinger et al. 2022; McShane et al. 2012; Rogers 1995). From a managerial standpoint, the results have important implications for the design of online video games. Specifically, our results indicate that enhancing the salience and visibility of other players’ game item usage during social matches is crucial if firms aim to generate a greater extent of peer influence, as opposed to encouraging users to learn and explore other players’ strat egies through the observation of less salient items.

A Simulation: Targeting Users to Leverage Peer Influence

While our investigation into the mechanisms of peer influence complements the academic literature, our find ings can also be applied to address a more fundamental question in business practice: Does understanding these mechanisms actually benefit or improve a firm’s effective management of online games? Specifically, how can firms increase social spillovers by leveraging informational influ ence in the field? To illustrate the managerial impact of tar geting, we conduct post-hoc simulations that aim to mimic a manager’s decision-making.

Post-Hoc Simulation Setup. Suppose that at the end of each week during our data period, the manager considers offering a targeted promotion (providing a free game item valued at $20) to 500 players. The question is: which play ers to target? We consider two targeting strategies.

1. Targeting Strategy #1: Target socially active play ers—those that play social games, assuming the manager has a general understanding of peer influence. 2. Targeting Strategy #2: Target socially active play ers who are more likely to engage in matches with players possessing less contextual knowledge, as indicated by match logs, thereby leveraging the informational influence mechanism.

Targeting Strategy #1 allows for the firm to make use of the general social spillovers that we highlight in our main analyses. The implementation approach is quite intuitive. Because behaviors from prior periods are observable, the firm can simply target players who were socially active in the previous week. The underlying assumption is that the firm expects these players to continue to engage in social matches next week and generate greater social influence.

Targeting Strategy #2 exploits the informational influ ence pathway to social influence. Using social match logs from the preceding week, the firm identifies potential tar gets by assessing whether a player was socially active and by evaluating the average tenure of the opponents they played with. Specifically, we divide the socially active sample from each preceding week at the median, based on the average tenure of opponent players. We then target individuals by randomly selecting from the lower half, which consists of players interacting with relatively less experienced opponents. These players are presumed to encounter and engage with other players who are more sus ceptible to social influence (informational influence) in the following week.6

Our objective is to simulate the economic effects of the two targeting strategies and demonstrate the managerial value of adopting a seeding strategy that leverages peer effects. As a benchmark, we compare the expected effec tiveness of these two interventions against a no- intervention scenario from the sample we have (i.e., as-is revenues). DISCUSSION

Summary and Intended Contributions

The managerial success of a large freemium-based serv ice requires an understanding of how social influence plays a role in consumer behavior. While there has been signifi cant focus in prior literature toward the role of known peers (friends) or aggregated crowd opinions, little attention has been given to the potential influence that anonymous peers may exert. This is an increasingly important issue given the growing popularity of socially interactive features embedded in many contexts, especially in online video

games. We aim to address this gap in the literature by (1) shining a spotlight on the outsized role that anonymous peers may have in shaping consumer behavior and, more importantly, (2) providing an empirical understanding of theoretical mechanisms driving peer effects.

Using purchase and game log data from a large gaming platform in Korea, we show that peer effects are indeed sig nificant among anonymous players. By investigating the theory behind the effect of strangers, we offer empirical evidence of the theoretical underpinnings (Bursztyn et al. 2014; Gardete 2015; Toker-Yildiz et al. 2017) in the con text of digital goods. We find that, contrary to expectations, peer effects are primarily driven by informational influence (as opposed to competitive concern) even in the empirical context of our competitive game. That is, the influence of anonymous peers decreases as the players accrue more con textual knowledge.

The current research makes three important contribu tions to the academic literature. First, our theory-based empirical investigation of the underlying mechanisms of peer influence directly complements existing research on peer effects in consumer behavior. Although the literature on peer influence is extensive and mature, there are rela tively few empirical investigations that explore the mecha nisms of how and why peer influence operates (see table 1 for details), especially in the online context. Our study identifies the theoretically plausible mechanisms of social effects (Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001) in a video game setting and validates them through a series of robustness checks. 1. Leverage peer influence to boost purchase behav ior: This research shows that, even in the absence of clearly defined friendship networks, strangers can influence our (online) behaviors in profound ways. This opens up opportunities for firms to lev erage such stranger interactions to trigger pur chase behavior in online environments. For instance, our analyses show that the influence from strangers is particularly effective in motivating more frequent purchase incidences (extensive mar gin) but does not necessarily increase the amount spent on each purchase (intensive margin). Video game companies could leverage this by imple menting strategies that encourage repeat purchases. Since peer influence is most effective in driving the decision to make a purchase, firms should focus on promotional efforts that emphasize fre quent buying opportunities, especially targeting players with less contextual knowledge who have been exposed to a greater extent of anonymous peers’ spending, rather than trying to increase the spending per transaction. 2. Product salience increases peer influence: Our results also directly point to guidance for video game designers interested in effectively leveraging peer influence. Limitations and Avenues for Future Research

This research is not without its limitations. First, despite the granularity of the data provided, we believe that a cer tain portion of peer influence may stem from word-of- mouth interactions between players. While this still falls under peer influence, it differs from observational learning that occurs without verbal communication (Zhang 2010). Using supplementary data, such as chat logs between play ers, future research in similar settings could aim to disen tangle peer influence into communication-based and non- communication-based types and explore additional impli cations. Second, with additional data such as team lineups, rookie trainings, and other performance metrics, it would be worthwhile to investigate whether peer effects impact users’ gaming performances. Third, we consistently observe a positive and significant main effect of the Threats to Status variable (% inferior matchesit–1). While not the focus of our interest, since managers of video games may be particularly interested in the factors influ encing voluntary purchases, future research could certainly explore this variable theoretically and empirically. Finally, while our context is limited to digital goods consumption, it would certainly be interesting to explore peer effects in other contexts such as offline arcades. We relegate these questions to future research explorations.
