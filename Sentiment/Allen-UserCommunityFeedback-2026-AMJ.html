Firms increasingly involve user communities in product development, and prior research documents many benefits in idea generation, knowledge sharing, and cost reduction. We theorize, however, that incorporating market feedback about products from these communities can harm products’ commercial performance. Because community feedback is volunteered by self-selected participants rather than sampled to represent the broader market, it may mislead firms when narrow community preferences diverge from mainstream demand. We predict that greater incorporation of community feedback will increase positive evaluations of a product within the community, but undermine its broader commercial success when there are strong user self-selection pressures (like for initially niche-targeted games). We test this theory in the personal-computer (PC) video-game industry using Steam Early Access, where developers integrate community feedback prior to release. Using a multimethod design that combines large-scale archival analyses with interviews of developers, executives, and community members, we find that feedback incorporation during Early Access improves post-launch commercial outcomes of games only when the Early Access community closely mirrors the distribution of users in the broader Steam market. However, in unrepresentative communities—even large ones—greater incorporation predicts lower post-launch revenues despite favorable community evaluations.

Firms increasingly turn to user communities for guidance in new product development. User communities are informal groups of self-selected volunteers who, collaboratively and with minimal oversight, contribute to products or technologies (Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Mollick, 2016; O’Mahony & Lakhani, 2011; Shah, 2006). Because user communities’ strengths complement traditional firms’ capabilities (Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011; Bremner & Eisenhardt, 2021; Felin & Zenger, 2014), scholars argue that the “engines of innovative activity,” historically residing within firms, now reside within firm– community interactions (Harhoff & Lakhani, 2016; O’Mahony & Lakhani, 2011; Shah & Nagle, 2020: 308).

Prior work has identified many benefits for firms that engage user communities at various stages of innovation. For instance, engaging with user communities boosts creative idea generation (Bayus, 2013; Lilien, Morrison, Searls, Sonnack & Hippel, 2002; Park, Piezunka & Dahlander, 2024; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015, 2019; Poetz & Schreier, 2012; Schweisfurth, 2017), expands knowledge access in new technology development (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Nagle, 2018, 2019), and reduces development and commercialization costs (Baldwin, Hienerth & von Hippel, 2006; Bremner & Eisenhardt, 2021; Shah & Tripsas, 2007). These studies converge on the conclusion that engaging with user communities benefits firms seeking to innovate across multiple stages of the innovation process.

Our theory builds on a key insight: unlike traditional market research, in which respondents are intentionally sampled or recruited to match a target audience, user community feedback is volunteered by self-selecting users (Harhoff & Lakhani, 2016; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; O’Mahony & Lakhani, 2011; Shah, 2006; Shah & Nagle, 2020; Von Krogh, Haefliger, Spaeth & Wallin, 2012). The very strengths of communities in other firm– community interactions—their enthusiasm and experience—create sample-selection issues when incorporating market feedback. If the most eager participants differ systematically from the broader market, then even large volumes of community feedback can be misleading rather than informative. For products with strong self-selection pressures (like initially niche-targeted products), incorporating feedback that caters to “what the community wants” will likely misalign with “what the broader market will buy.” Therefore, while incorporating more feedback from an unrepresentative community will increase positive within-community evaluations,2 this self-selection can create a scenario in which incorporating more feedback systematically diminishes the product’s broader commercial performance.

We test our theory in the context of PC video-game development. We analyze games available through Steam Early Access, a program that allows developers (firms) to incorporate feedback from dedicated user communities to refine features and tune balance and

difficulty before official release. We conduct a multimethod investigation using multiple large archival samples of Steam Early Access games supplemented by interviews with game developers, industry executives, and community members. We measure feedback incorporation as the textual similarity between developer (firm) announcements and user reviews during Early Access; we measure community representativeness as the extent to which the distribution of user types during Early Access mirrors the broader distribution of user types on Steam.

We find no association, on average, between firms’ feedback incorporation and their products’ commercial success. However, as our theory anticipates, we find significant heterogeneous effects driven by strong negative moderation from community unrepresentativeness. For games with unrepresentative communities (even large ones), incorporating more feedback from the community decreases commercial success (post-Early Access revenue), even as it increases positive within-community evaluations (positive ratings within the initial Early Access feedback community). However, for games with representative communities, feedback incorporation has a positive effect on commercial success. We observe a similar pattern for games with a high proportion of “power users” (the most frequent Early Access participants)—games that heavily incorporate this feedback are less commercially successful. We also investigate why firms would use such feedback if it is hurting commercial success. We find no significant relationship between the degree of community unrepresentativeness and the extent of feedback incorporation, strongly suggesting that detecting and adapting to unrepresentative feedback is challenging for firms.

The idea that unrepresentative samples can lead to incorrect inferences is well-established (Cao, Koning & Nanda, 2021; Denrell, 2003; Denrell & Kovacs, 2008). Yet, our findings emphasize that in product development with user community feedback, the degree of unrepresentativeness can be systematic (not merely noisy), difficult to detect, and challenging to mitigate. Crucially, this is not a problem that is solved by “more data” from the community: with strong selfselection into feedback, larger samples will still converge toward the preferences of the community rather than those of the broader market. This risk is most acute for products that initially attract niche user types; when the feedback community mirrors the broader market, incorporating more feedback improves both within-community evaluations and commercial performance.

These findings make three theoretical contributions. First, we extend the literature on user community innovation by highlighting an underappreciated boundary condition of the benefits of firm– community interaction: market feedback from selfselected community members can bias development toward within-community preferences. Second, we break with the prevailing assumption in entrepreneurship that more and richer market information improves learning. Third, we identify an important boundary condition in the literature on open organizational forms: while search with external users mitigates supply-side myopia, the self-selection of external users can systematically skew demand-side learning.

THEORY AND HYPOTHESES

Self-Selection and User Community Feedback

User communities are, by definition, characterized by open membership and self-selection into participation (Lakhani, 2016; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Mollick, 2016; Shah, 2006; Shah & Nagle, 2020). Prior research on user community innovation has documented extrinsic and intrinsic motivations for participation in communities (see Von Krogh et al., 2012). Intrinsic motivations include enthusiasm for the new product or technology (David & Shapiro, 2008; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003), the joy of participation (Hippel & Krogh, 2003; Shah, 2006), altruism (Osterloh & Rota, 2007), and belonging within a group of like-minded individuals (Lakhani & Wolf, 2005). Participants also face internalized extrinsic motivations such as attaining reputation and status within the community (Krishna, Shrestha & Von Krogh, 2024; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Lakhani & Wolf, 2005), reciprocity with other contributors (David & Shapiro, 2008; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003), or the need for a product or technology that does not exist elsewhere (David & Shapiro, 2008; Shah, 2006). While many such studies examine communities building technological solutions such as opensource software, other studies identify similar motivations in crowdsourcing communities contributing feedback and ideas to firms (Bayus, 2013; Harhoff & Lakhani, 2016; Preece, Nonnecke & Andrews, 2004). Though motivations like self-determination may be less common than in open-source software development, enthusiasm for the domain, altruism, participatory enjoyment, and product necessity still motivate users providing feedback to firms (Lakhani, 2016).

Potential Benefits of User Community Feedback

Prior research on user community innovation has not directly examined whether incorporating selfselected user community enhances the commercial success of firms’ products. However, research on entrepreneurial innovation (not based in user communities) broadly supports the idea that incorporating early customer feedback increases the likelihood of developing commercially successful products (Allen & McDonald, 2025; Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella & Spina, 2020; Contigiani & Levinthal, 2019; Koning, Hasan & Chatterji, 2022; McDonald & Gao, 2019). Soliciting feedback helps firms resolve market uncertainty by understanding customer preferences (Camuffo et al., 2020; McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2019; Murray & Tripsas, 2004; Toh & Kim, 2013), guiding product design decisions such as which features will attract a sufficiently large audience (MacCormack, Verganti & Iansiti, 2001).

Extrapolating from several strands of research focused on innovation phases like ideation and development suggests that feedback from self-selected communities could benefit firms seeking commercial success. Studies on user community innovation show that members who voluntarily join communities are highly motivated to enhance the focal technology or product (Lakhani, 2016; Mollick, 2016; O’Mahony, 2003; Shah, 2006; Von Krogh, Spaeth & Lakhani, 2003). Assuming that similar motivations drive community members providing feedback on products, we expect them to contribute more time and insight compared to typical market research participants.

Furthermore, studies on user-led innovation indicate that product users with actual experience using a product (or similar products) possess more knowledge about useful features through deeper, sustained interaction (Chatterji & Fabrizio, 2014; Foss, Laursen & Pedersen, 2011; Katila, Thatchenkery, Christensen & Zenios, 2017). For example, Chatterji and Fabrizio’s (2014) longitudinal analysis of 128 medical-device firms finds that firms collaborating with users (physicians, in their case) during the innovation process are more likely to produce radical innovations (for a similar conclusion in corporate venture capital, see Smith & Shah 2013). Given that community feedback comes from users who self-select into interacting with and thus gaining experience with the product, similar benefits could apply to user community feedback.

Relatedly, studies establish that lead users with the highest demand for, or understanding of, new products—though unrepresentative of the broader population—provide particularly novel and valuable

These positive effects may compound by engaging a large number of users. If market feedback from users follows similar patterns, these advantages should apply.

Finally, additional benefits may arise from learning within interactive user communities. Prior studies demonstrate that members engage in collaborative forums, which provide rich environments for interactive feedback and collaborative solution-building that surpass individual efforts (Autio, Dahlander & Frederiksen, 2013; Jeppesen & Molin, 2003; Shah & Tripsas, 2007). Shah and Tripsas (2007) show that successful ventures in the juvenile products industry leveraged collective creative activity with communities (through users socializing, discussing common needs, and building on others’ ideas), significantly improving the quality of innovations.

While prior studies have addressed firm– community interactions in other innovation phases (ideation, development), extrapolating their findings to market feedback contexts suggests that similar mechanisms could apply. Critical contextual caveats remain, as such studies typically focus on specialized domains like medical devices (Chatterji & Fabrizio, 2014; Katila et al., 2017), or new technological domains that are not well understood (Urban & von

Hippel, 1988). Nevertheless, without evidence to the contrary, existing user-innovation literature supports that user community feedback could enhance product quality and commercial success.

Potential Risks of Self-Selected User Community Feedback

However, when soliciting product feedback from self-selected user communities, firms face the risk of learning from unrepresentative samples. In traditional market research, firms select subjects to represent their target market when soliciting market feedback; in communities, users join and provide feedback for their own reasons, as mentioned previously (Harhoff & Lakhani, 2016; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; O’Mahony & Lakhani, 2011; Shah, 2006; Shah & Nagle, 2020; Von Krogh et al., 2012). This lack of control over the feedback sample raises risks of false inferences from selection bias in the user community feedback (Shah & Nagle, 2020).

Not all products attract communities whose preferences misrepresent the broader market. Communities vary in how they reflect potential customer preferences, ranging from niche-focused to broadly mainstream (Carroll, 1985; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Moore, 1991).3 Products positioned toward niche markets naturally attract users whose feedback is apt to exhibit selection biases, diverging from mainstream preferences (Moore, 1991). Importantly, for our theory, a product attracting an unrepresentative community (given a similar community size) does not automatically decrease the potential for commercial success. While products attracting mainstream customers generally have the highest likelihood of commercial success (Allen, 2025; MacCormack et al., 2001), products attracting niche customers can achieve significant commercial success when (a) the market has heterogeneous needs and preferences (Riggs & von Hippel, 1994), (b) the niche market is poised to expand into a broader population (Allen, 2025; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Rogers, 2003), or (c) mainstream markets are overly competitive (Allen, 2025; Chen, Katila, McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2010;

Instead, we argue that the interaction between community unrepresentativeness and firm feedback incorporation matters. Initially attracting an unrepresentative community need not harm commercial success, but incorporating feedback from such a sample may prove detrimental. Prior research in innovation, statistics, and marketing demonstrates that inferences from unrepresentative samples can mislead firms’ learning (Cao et al., 2021; Denrell, 2003; Denrell & Kovacs, 2008; Heckman, 1979; Pritchett & Sandefur, 2015). Consider Cao et al. (2021), who examined the online platform Product Hunt, where founders, developers, and designers provide feedback on early product ideas. They found that female-targeted products launching on days with randomly more male participants had lower chances of success, suggesting that biased feedback composition distorts inferences about market potential. Similarly, Denrell (2003) argued that organizations learn harmful practices by emulating a biased sample of successful peers (firms that engaged in risky practices but survived) while ignoring failed peers. In both cases, additional information—whether user preferences or organizational practices—proved harmful when the information’s representativeness4 was lacking (see also Denrell & Kovacs, 2008).

When members of a user community are predominantly a specific user type, feedback from the community may misrepresent mainstream market preferences (Moore, 1991). The more a product attracts unrepresentative users, the greater the risk that incorporating their feedback steers development toward niche features that are appealing to community members yet fail to achieve broader commercial success. This trajectory contradicts mainstream commercial success, which usually requires products to transition from niche appeal to broad market acceptance (Allen, 2025; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Moore, 1991; Rogers, 2003).

Hence, counterintuitively, more information (via community feedback) can undermine a product’s ultimate commercial success. When feedback comes from a representative sample, both positive and negative feedback prove valuable: positive feedback confirms the product is on track, while negative feedback prompts reconsideration of current features and

functionality (Allen & McDonald, 2025). However, for feedback from an unrepresentative sample, incorporating it can be detrimental, leading firms to either discard features that would attract a broader audience (Type I error) or pursue niche features that are unappealing to other potential customers (Type II error).

Consider a hypothetical scenario where a firm develops a prototype budgeting app with uniquely rich budgeting features. Due to its distinctive functionality, this app attracts a specific type of user who seeks specialized budgeting tools that are unavailable elsewhere and forms an enthusiastic community that contributes feedback to the product. Their enthusiasm for the uniquely rich features, however, becomes a double-edged sword. While these self-selected users offer valuable insights into preferences that are similar to their own, their preference for complex, featurerich interfaces skews development. Their feedback might encourage the prioritization of sophisticated features that, while valuable to them, could alienate mainstream users seeking simplicity. Rather than catering to these specialized interests, the app could achieve broader market success by adapting its niche functionality to attract casual budgeters (instead of entrenching with budgeting super-users that selected into the community).

Given the potential for systematic selection bias in community members’ preferences, increasing the sample size of the community or the volume of feedback will not correct its bias. The central limit theorem states that the mean of a sufficiently large number of random samples from a population will approximate the population mean; thus, increased sample size typically improves inference (Casella & Berger, 2024; Feller, 1991). However, a large sample of systematically unrepresentative, self-selected users (budgeting super-users) will converge not on the true population mean, but rather on the mean of that specific user type. Given a strong selection pressure for a particular type of user, preferences inferred from feedback will misrepresent the broader population, and inferences will mislead.

User Community Feedback Incorporation and Commercial Success

Thus, unlike typical market research, it remains ambiguous whether incorporating feedback from selfselected user communities will, on average, enhance or dampen the commercial success of a firm’s product. Indeed, the two theoretical traditions reviewed above suggest countervailing forces. On one hand, user feedback typically increases product performance; on the

Catering to users who gave feedback should make those users more positive about the product (Allen & McDonald, 2025; Camuffo et al., 2020; Gans, Stern & Wu, 2019; Koning et al., 2022; Morris, Oldroyd, Allen, Chng & Han, 2023), whether feedback is unrepresentative or representative.

A corollary to the logic underlying Hypothesis 1, then, is that incorporating unrepresentative users’ feedback should increase positive within-community evaluations—how much the users who provided feedback enjoy and positively rate the product. METHODS

Setting

We test our hypotheses in the setting of PC videogame development. The video-game industry, which originated in the 1970s, has become a major economic sector: its global revenue in 2018 was reported to be approximately $138 billion (Wijman, 2018). Of this total, PC game sales accounted for approximately 24%.

To conduct our analysis, we collected data on video games hosted by Steam Early Access. Steam, the leading online digital distribution platform for PC games, was launched in 2003 by Valve, a successful video-game developer and publisher (Plunkett, 2013). Firms can list games on the Steam Store in exchange for a share of sales revenue of up to 30%. In 2018, the Steam Store listed over 27,000 games for sale (Meer, 2019). Analysts estimate that PC game sales via Steam represent around 60% of global industry sales (Edwards, 2013; Hruska, 2018).

Steam offers Early Access as a specialized platform for firms “to develop your game with the community by giving them access to your title before it is officially released” (Steamworks, 2014). Early Access “encourages ongoing updates from [firms], while letting players participate in direct feedback through gameplay and community involvement” (Steam, n.d.). Based on our interviews and data, firms tend to use Early Access for smaller one-off “indie” games (as opposed to AAA or franchise games), or games with high “replay value” (as opposed to linear storyline games). This tends to include games in genres like grand strategy, simulations, and online multiplayer games, where changes to features and balance can significantly alter the game, giving Early Access considerable influence on its commercial success. We note that our findings with respect to unrepresentativeness and the degree of feedback incorporation should be

interpreted in the context of products that opt in to collaboration with user communities (rather than comparing products that do vs. do not opt in to collaborating with communities in the first place).

Consistent with the literature on user communities (Puranam, Alexy & Reitzig, 2014; Shah, 2005, 2006), any user can join and participate in Steam Early Access communities, whether they play the games or not. As is typical of user communities that form around new products, users participate not because the games are the best, most polished games available—for that, they can buy fully developed games—but because they exhibit motivations similar to those seen in prior studies of communities (Shah, 2006; Von Krogh et al., 2012). “Early Access games are a lot more fun to play,” one user explained. “They are typically much more innovative. [Big-budget, heavily marketed] titles are so incremental; they just improve the graphics or add a few new features to the same old formula.” Another motivation is social interaction (David & Shapiro, 2008). As one user explained: “The community is what makes [Early Access games] fun to play for me. It’s a social experience.” A third motivation expressed by users is altruism and a sense of belonging (Osterloh & Rota, 2007; Von Krogh et al., 2012); in one user’s words: “There is a certain joy in responding in forums and giving suggestions and feedback to [firms], and then seeing your ideas being implemented.”

By listing a game on Early Access, firms can incorporate community feedback to potentially improve the game. The firm is not formally obliged to maintain, change, or even complete the game. As a manager in one firm recounted:

We launched [our game] in Early Access with about an hour of gameplay. It was enough to give the player a decent experience right away. But it also gave us an opportunity to really incorporate [community] feedback as we continued to work on the game.

Firms also participate in discussions and rely on the community for feedback, which is typically viewed as valuable in development. As a game designer in another firm observed, “Listening to the community is extremely important. There were a couple of times where we ignored what the community was telling us, and I think that was a big mistake.”

Steam Early Access is an attractive setting for our research for two reasons. First, market feedback is the key objective of Early Access (Steamworks, 2014). Users provide ideas to firms (e.g., for features or game balance) through reviews and forums, but users do not directly create or modify the games themselves.

In our interviews, one developer at a prominent firm aptly commented, “Nobody knows how to always make a hit game.” Releasing an incomplete game during the experimental, early access phase thus enables firms to tweak and test a game’s design and its reception.

Data

We created multiple datasets to test our results. The primary datasets, which contain the highest level of detail, were compiled primarily from scraping and merging data from SteamDB and SteamSpy, and include all games that entered Steam Early Access between April 2015 (when commercial-performance data became available) and March 2018 (the month before SteamSpy shut down due to changes in Steam privacy updates).5 We also compiled a separate extension dataset, merging several sources, including the Video Game Insights database and the Steam application-programming interface (API), that extends through 2024. Although in some ways less granular than the primary dataset, it allows us to corroborate our main results with an independent and more up-todate data source.

For our primary analysis datasets covering April 2015–March 2018, we collected quantitative data on Steam games from two primary sources: SteamDB (steamdb.info) and SteamSpy (steamspy.- com). However, our theory presupposes the existence of at least a minimally sized community attracted to a product—without which the theory would not be applicable—and presupposes that the firm actively updates the product. Therefore, excluding games that do not meet these criteria allows us to more precisely test our theory. This approach, however, underscores a boundary condition: our theory is applicable to products that succeed in attracting communities in the first place.

We define customer segments by categorizing all 4 million users on SteamDB into distinct market segments using a K-means clustering algorithm. Employing an unsupervised clustering algorithm to categorize reviewers allows us to assess representativeness without relying on our own subjective classifications. Control variables. We included several control variables to rule out potential confounding explanations for the results. In the cross-sectional sample, each variable is calculated as the mean of its monthly values during the Early Access period. In the monthly panel sample, each variable is calculated as the monthly mean (during Early Access months). By controlling for these variables, we aim to isolate the effects on post-launch revenue from feedback incorporation and the user community’s unrepresentativeness.

First, we controlled for a game’s average price during Early Access. Price directly impacts the revenue outcome, and it is also often an indicator of a game’s depth, target audience, and production value. Second, because the quality or enjoyability of a game is a potential omitted variable that could affect both treatment (the feedback incorporation) and outcome (post-launch revenue), we also controlled for average within-community positivity toward a game, measured as the number of positive reviews divided by total reviews in the Early Access period. Third, we controlled for each game’s average monthly number of reviewers. This effectively controlled for the game’s volume of feedback and popularity during Early Access, which is likely to influence both the firm’s feedback incorporation and its game’s ultimate revenue after the official launch. Fourth, we controlled for the number of firm announcements during Early Access to disentangle firm activity in terms of frequency and volume from feedback incorporation as alignment with reviewer content. Finally, we controlled for firm experience as the number of prior games launched by the game’s developer firm, which helps to mitigate concerns around the potential alternative explanation that managerial or firm experience are confounding variables.

Our models also include several fixed effects. In the cross-sectional data, we added a fixed effect for the Early Access duration (in months) of the game prior to launch. The choice to launch quickly or to stay in for a long period could be an omitted variable, so we attempted to mitigate this potential confounder with fixed effects. We also added a fixed effect for the main game category (e.g., strategy, first-person shooter). This mitigates potential confounding by accounting for the fact that each category attracts a different type and size of audience. In robustness checks, we also used the more granular game tags as

fixed effects (see Appendix D). In the panel data, we used game and month fixed effects.

Statistical Estimation and Causality

To test our main hypothesis, we estimate a fixedeffects ordinary least squares (OLS) model that interacts user community unrepresentativeness and firm feedback incorporation in predicting the outcome of post-launch revenue. To identify the effect, we must validate the assumption that the interaction variables are exogenous. That is, we must assume that there is natural variation in a firm’s choice to align their feedback with user reviews, and this choice is based on unobserved factors that are not systematically related to the outcome of commercial success after adding controls. We also assume that unrepresentativeness corresponds to underlying game attributes and positioning that is not systematically related to commercial success, conditional upon important controls for community size and category fixed effects.

Given the necessity of exogeneity to identify our effect, the ideal empirical design would be to randomly assign varying degrees of feedback incorporation and community representativeness across games and compare their outcomes. Such a design was not feasible, and we did not have access to a valid exogenous shock or instrumental variable in our data. Therefore, when interpreting the results, it is important to consider potential endogeneity concerns. Reverse causality is not an issue; our dependent measure, post-launch revenue, is temporally sequenced after the independent and control variables. However, despite controlling for an array of observable confounders—including category fixed effects, prelaunch popularity, game quality, firm experience, and price—unobserved confounders might still create an endogenous relationship in firms’ propensity to incorporate to users’ feedback, or in the community’s representativeness, with regard to a game’s subsequent commercial performance.

We address the possibility of unobserved confounders in three ways. First, we conduct an array of analyses to rule out specific endogeneity concerns, such as the concern that games attracting narrow segments are simply more likely to fail. In the results section, we demonstrate that community unrepresentativeness does not directly affect commercial success—it has a negative effect only when interacting with feedback incorporation. Our findings also show no significant correlation between unrepresentativeness and feedback incorporation, indicating that it is their interaction, not their presence alone, that affects outcomes.

In the Appendix we conduct a range of additional robustness tests to address specific endogeneity concerns. We provide further details in the “Robustness Checks” section.

Second, in addition to our primary cross-sectional analysis, we use a separate game-month panel dataset. The panel-data approach allows us to measure within-game effects, effectively eliminating timeinvariant game attributes that could act as potential omitted variables. For example, it removes any variance explained by the firm’s initial choice of positioning of the game as appealing to either a narrow or broad segment of the market. It also removes another source of potential endogeneity in the cross-sectional dataset: whether the game ever officially launches (in the cross-sectional data, we only observe Early Access games that “graduate”).

Finally, we use non-parametric covariate balance propensity score weighting to balance the observable confounders for our continuous-treatment variable feedback incorporation as well as for the moderator

variable unrepresentativeness (results displayed in Appendix D) (Imai & Ratkovic, 2014). Weighting allows us to effectively create a comparative set of games with analogous attributes during the Early Access phase to establish a counterfactual scenario in which all variables remain static except the feedback incorporation or unrepresentativeness variables. Supplementary Tests: Panel-Data Analysis

We also conduct a panel-data analysis to supplement the primary cross-sectional analysis. The panel analysis estimates how firms’ monthly levels of feedback incorporation impact subsequent months’ commercial success for games while still in Early Access. Using a larger game-month panel dataset allows us to triangulate our cross-sectional results with additional data while controlling for time-invariant game attributes using game fixed effects. Although the outcome variable does not directly measure commercial success in the external market, increasing revenue from new users is an indication of a game’s increasing growth and success with users other than those who provided initial feedback.

Table 5 presents results from the monthly paneldata analysis, showing five columns of OLS models with game and month-year fixed effects, using log (revenue) as the dependent variable. Similar to the cross-sectional analysis in Table 3, columns 1 and 2 omit control variables. Column 1 indicates that feedback incorporation significantly increases revenue, whereas unrepresentativeness significantly decreases

it: a 1 SD increase in feedback incorporation (t – 1) corresponds to roughly a 25% revenue increase in month t, while a 1 SD increase in unrepresentativeness (t 2 2) relates to about a 22% revenue decrease. Column 2 introduces the interaction between these variables, yielding a marginally significant negative coefficient (p ,.10), implying a 12% reduction in revenue. Adding control variables in columns 3 and 4 confirms these findings, maintaining the interaction’s marginal significance (p ,.10).

Applying Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) in Column 5 aligns the results even more closely with the cross-sectional analysis (for methodology, see Appendix C). Here, feedback incorporation and unrepresentativeness are not individually significant, but their interaction remains significant. Specifically, for a community with unrepresentativeness 1 SD above the mean in t 2 2, a 1 SD increase in feedback incorporation in t 2 1 results in an 18% revenue decrease at time t. These monthly effects are consistent with, yet smaller than, those observed in the longer-term cross-sectional analysis, as expected.

Columns 6 and 7 of Table 5 mirror the specifications of columns 4 and 5, but replace the dependent variable log (revenue) with the percentage change in revenue from the prior month to the present month. Despite this difference in the dependent variable, the significance of the terms and the models’ implications for supporting our hypothesis remain similar to those observed in the log (revenue) versions of the models.

Antecedents of Community Unrepresentativeness

Given our theory that the effectiveness of incorporating user community feedback hinges on the unrepresentativeness of the community, we consider: Which products under development attract unrepresentative communities in the first place? According to our theory, we expect that games that are initially niche-targeted or that appeal strongly to a particular type of user will attract an unrepresentative community. While we do not directly observe which games are niche-targeted, we assume in our analyses that it is these niche-appealing games that attract unrepresentative communities, and we base our theory on the observed data in the resulting community composition.

To increase the validity of these assumptions, we conducted an analysis demonstrating that the narrowness of the category in which the game is

launched predicts the community’s unrepresentativeness (for variable construction, see Appendix A). Although this measure does not fully capture how niche-targeted a game is, we observe that a 10% increase in category narrowness leads to a statistically significant 2.5% increase in the unrepresentativeness of the game’s user community (see Appendix D). We also observed that a higher price was associated with lower community unrepresentativeness. This is also unsurprising—in the context of the video-game market, price is one of the primary indicators of production value and of the expectation for widespread commercial success, which would be likely to positively correlate with broad appeal.

We also found that a game’s novelty (measured using category-spanning, as in McDonald & Allen, 2022; see measure description in Appendix A) was positively correlated with unrepresentativeness, although this relationship was not statistically significant (see Appendix D). In a separate analysis, however, we found that our theory was more strongly supported among less novel games (Appendix D). This stronger support among less novel games suggests a potential boundary condition of our theory: the detrimental impact of unrepresentative feedback may be particularly critical for the majority of products which are not highly novel, which depend heavily on easily observable broad market appeal for commercial success.

We claim not that a game’s narrow category membership fully predicts its community’s unrepresentativeness, but that it is indicative of a game’s niche appeal, which in turn attracts an unrepresentative community. Therefore, our main analysis measures the community unrepresentativeness as the indicator of some upstream characteristic of the game (e.g., narrow category membership or targeting a particular type of user) that led it to strongly attract a certain type of user. We return to these results in the “Discussion” section as we discuss implications for decision-makers.

DISCUSSION

In this paper, we developed theory describing when incorporation of feedback from user communities in product development undermines commercial success. While incorporating more feedback unambiguously enhances positive evaluations within the community, we proposed that self-selection of users into the community can create a scenario where more feedback incorporation systematically diminishes commercial performance outside the community.

Page 23

This occurs for products that attract communities characterized by a strong self-selection of users that are unrepresentative of the broader market. Heavily incorporating such feedback leads firms to cater to specific user preferences that fail to transfer to the broader population, limiting mainstream commercial success. In the context of PC game development, we presented empirical evidence consistent with our theory. We found that, independent of community size, community unrepresentativeness had a strong, negative moderation effect on the relationship between feedback incorporation and commercial success (outside the community). This negative moderating effect produces a systematic negative impact from feedback incorporation when communities are highly unrepresentative (even when large). Conversely, we observed a positive relationship between feedback incorporation and positive evaluations within the community.

We also addressed the question of why firms would incorporate feedback that harms commercial success. We presented evidence suggesting that detecting and adapting to unrepresentative feedback is challenging for firms: we found no relationship between community unrepresentativeness and feedback incorporation. In this setting (as in other entrepreneurship contexts), firms have limited control over who provides feedback, and few resources for traditional market research. For such firms, immediate positive responses from community users may be a more salient signal than the longer-term lack of success in the broader market. Overall, these findings support our theory and contribute to user community innovation, data-driven entrepreneurship, and organizational search literatures.

Implications for Theories of Firm–Community Innovation

Prior research highlights benefits for firms leveraging user communities in several product-development phases, including ideation (Bayus, 2013; Lilien et al., 2002; Park et al., 2024; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015, 2019; Poetz & Schreier, 2012; Schweisfurth, 2017), development (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Nagle, 2018, 2019), and commercialization (Baldwin et al., 2006; Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011; Bremner & Eisenhardt, 2021; Shah & Tripsas, 2007). These interactions enable firms to develop more innovative and higher-performing products versus innovating on their own.

However, our theory and findings point to a significant boundary condition to the benefits of firm–community innovation. We show that such collaborations in the

market feedback phase, when firms solicit feedback from communities about features and functionalities of their partially developed products, may actually undermine the commercial success of new products. Products with strong selection pressure for a particular user type may receive rich and informed feedback from self-selected users, yet these community members likely possess preferences that are unrepresentative of the broader market, producing a negative relationship between feedback incorporation and commercial success. As one game designer warned: “Listen to your community. But I would also say: know when not to listen.”

Our empirical analysis of antecedents influencing community unrepresentativeness shows that products targeting narrow categories or those positioned as lower-priced (potentially indicating lower production value) tend to attract less representative user communities. While niche appeal alone need not mean these products will be less commercially successful (Allen, 2025; Christensen & Bower, 1996; Christensen, McDonald, Altman & Palmer, 2018; Rogers, 2003), our theory suggests that decision-makers would be more likely to develop a commercially successful product by ignoring community feedback for games with niche appeal—at least until the product attracts more representative, mainstream users.

Therefore, while prior research suggests that feedback from lead users significantly benefits innovation in emerging technologies (Urban & von Hippel, 1988; von Hippel, 1986), our results challenge these benefits in more established markets. In such markets, our theory suggests that feedback from self-selected users— even those with advanced knowledge and expertise— can harm firms seeking commercial success.

Implications for Theories of Data-Driven Strategy and Entrepreneurship

Prior studies within the strategy and entrepreneurship literature demonstrate the importance of gathering information about market preferences in innovation (Allen & McDonald, 2025; Camuffo et al., 2020; Gans et al., 2019; Gao & McDonald, 2019; Koning et al., 2022; Morris et al., 2023). This work suggests that feedback about a nascent product, whether positive or negative, benefits firms: positive feedback confirms that innovators are on track while negative feedback signals a need to quit or pivot (McDonald & Gao, 2019).

However, our theory identifies an underappreciated counterpoint: more information about early customers can actually be detrimental to innovation

success. We demonstrate that when feedback comes from an unrepresentative sample, firms may discard product features that would appeal to a broader audience (Type I error) or focus on niche features that do not resonate with other potential customers (Type II error).

That unrepresentative samples lead to incorrect inferences is established (Cao et al., 2021; Denrell, 2003; Denrell & Kovacs, 2008). Yet, our findings contribute by emphasizing that in product development with user community feedback, unrepresentativeness can be systematic (not merely noisy), difficult to detect, and challenging to mitigate. We found no clear link between community representativeness and the extent to which game-producing firms incorporated that community’s feedback. This pattern suggests that firms either do not fully understand how well their user communities’ preferences reflect the broader market (due to limited resources or user anonymity), or struggle to filter or adapt feedback (due to salient, often positive, within-community signals).

While most innovation and entrepreneurship does not involve formal user communities, many entrepreneurial settings nonetheless exhibit similar feedbackgeneration characteristics: a convenience bias in user selection, a lack of control over the sample using the early product, and limited resources for traditional market research. Given these parallels, our theory suggests that startup founders may need to be cautious of self-selected advice, particularly when developing products that are likely to initially attract niche users.

These observations also invite future research on whether artificial intelligence, which will be increasingly used to interpret early user signals and guide product and strategy choices (Allen & McDonald, 2026), will overweight data from self-selected early adopters and thereby reinforce the same errors we identify.

Implications for Theories of Organizational Search

As a broader theoretical contribution to organizational theories of search, we highlight the need to distinguish between supply-side knowledge recombination and demand-side customer validation. A long-standing body of literature has shown that external search mechanisms like crowdsourcing, alliances, or open-source development help to overcome the local myopia of supply-side knowledge recombination (Afuah & Tucci, 2012; Keil, 2002; Keil, Maula, Schildt & Zahra, 2008; Levinthal & March

1993; Park et al., 2024; Rosenkopf & Almeida, 2003; Rosenkopf & Nerkar, 2001). This positive view developed within contexts where organizations build new technologies or solutions that require external knowledge or complex problem-solving (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Jeppesen & Molin, 2003; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Nagle, 2019; O’Mahony, 2003; O’Mahony & Lakhani, 2011; Shah & Tripsas, 2007). External search can significantly enhance an organization’s capability to navigate technological uncertainties by generating a broad array of potential innovations, termed “long jumps,” that would not be possible within a firm’s boundaries (Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011; Bremner & Eisenhardt, 2021; Felin & Zenger, 2014; Levinthal, 2021: 78; Nagle, 2018). Communities involved in product development benefit, for instance, from the diverse skills, knowledge, and expertise of users who opt to participate.

However, our study again suggests caution when applying supply-side logic to demand-side search, such as customer validation for achieving commercial success. While user-led external search benefits knowledge recombination, it may adversely skew market learning. According to our study, this misalignment stems from self-selected contributors who, though highly knowledgeable and enthusiastic about the focal domain, may lack mechanisms to ensure that their preferred features also appeal to a broader, mainstream audience. Our findings suggest that focusing solely on whether a community of users can attain what they want, without considering broader market appeal, jeopardizes commercial success. In other words, traditional search paradigms resolve “Can we build what we want?” but cannot fully address “Will what we build have broad appeal?” Future research can further explore how the classical organizational search paradigms face limitations when guiding decision-makers in contexts where resolving market uncertainty outweighs resolving technological uncertainty.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several boundary conditions of our theory remain for future research. First, our observations address only later stages of selection and development for products already being launched, not initial product-concept generation (Criscuolo et al., 2017; Keum & See, 2017). Our results do not imply that user communities (a) generating ideas for their own use or (b) engaging with firms in earlier development stages (ideation) necessarily harm commercial success.

Page 25

Second, in other contexts, incorporating unrepresentative user feedback could yield neutral—or even positive—commercial outcomes. Our empirical setting examined a mature marketplace with established categories and expectations. By contrast, as our analysis of novelty suggests (Appendix D), in highly novel or ambiguous contexts, unrepresentative feedback may prove valuable, as lead users can provide forwardlooking insights that diffuse more broadly over time (Allen, 2025; Moore, 1991). Our results may also depend on the heterogeneity and fragmentation of preferences in a market. In our context, success requires big hits appealing to a large base of gamers with heterogeneous preferences. In other contexts, with relatively homogeneous preferences, unrepresentativeness may be less consequential; or, where success requires niche appeal, tailoring products to small clusters may prove advantageous (Akcigit & Kerr, 2018).

Third, we must better understand antecedents of community unrepresentativeness. Our post hoc analyses indicate that product category and price influence unrepresentativeness; however, further research could explore how a product’s design, attributes, and positioning attract unrepresentative users. For example, unrepresentative users may emerge from mechanics rewarding mastery, the signaling aesthetic (e.g., retro pixel art), narrow platform or language support (PC-only, English-only), higher price points, and marketing through hardcore subcommunities (tags like “difficult,” influencer channels, invite-only Discords). Such choices may disproportionately attract expert enthusiasts, but our data limitations preclude deeper analysis, warranting further exploration.

Finally, our analysis does not account for development costs. Even if incorporating feedback reduces revenue, it may still improve profitability by lowering costs (Baldwin & von Hippel, 2011; Bremner & Eisenhardt, 2021; Felin & Zenger, 2014; Lakhani, Lifshitz- Assaf & Tushman, 2013). While our study cannot conclusively assess profitability, it identifies the tradeoff firms face: potential revenue losses versus efficiency gains from relying on community feedback.
