The relative incidence of positive and negative word of mouth:

A multi-category study

Robert East a,⁎, Kathy Hammond b, Malcolm Wright c

a School of Business, Kingston University, Kingston Hill, Kingston, KT2 7LB, Surrey, UK

b Duke Corporate Education, 165, Fleet Street, London, EC4A 2DY, UK c Department of Marketing, Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand

Abstract

In 15 studies, positive word of mouth (PWOM) is more common than negative word of mouth (NWOM) in every case. The incidence ratio averages 3 to 1. Categories with high levels of NWOM tend to have high levels of PWOM, and individuals who produce NWOM also tend to produce PWOM. In follow-up studies, using two alternative measures of WOM, we confirm that PWOM occurs approximately three times as often as NWOM. Further exploratory studies indicate that most PWOM is about a consumer's main brand; as a consequence, the incidence of PWOM in a category is distributed according to brand market share. We discuss possible explanations for these results and develop six propositions to guide further research on word of mouth. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Word of mouth; Recommendation

1. Introduction

People receive information from their physical surroundings, from the mass media and through their dealings with other persons. Information from other persons about goods and services may come from sales personnel or from other consumers. In marketing, word of mouth (WOM) is normally used to describe advice from other consumers. The interactivity, speed, and lack of commercial bias of WOM make it a very effective source of information about prospective consumer choices, particularly with regard to services for which prepurchase experience may be limited. In consumer choice, WOM is often the dominant factor; for example, Keaveney (1995) observed that positive word of mouth (PWOM) was the main source of information when people found a new service supplier.

Since WOM guides much of our consumption, it is important that marketers understand recurrent patterns in the incidence of WOM. There are indications that marketers believe NWOM is more common than PWOM; for example, Naylor and Kleiser

(2000) titled their work “Negative Versus Positive Word of Mouth: An Exception to the Rule” when they found more PWOM than NWOM. However, if a large majority of goods and service users are satisfied, it is likely that PWOM is more common than NWOM. We seek to test this judgment with evidence. We also investigate how the incidence of PWOM and NWOM differs by category, individual consumer and brand. In addition, we determine whether a few people give WOM often or many people give it infrequently and what proportions of PWOM and NWOM are about respondents' main brands. We also observe how the production of WOM relates to market share. A continuing problem in this field has been the reliance on single recall measures; we use three different measures (one of which is not based on recall) to establish our results.

In summary, we seek answers to the following questions:

• What is the relative occurrence of PWOM and NWOM? • Do categories that elicit more NWOM also elicit more PWOM? • How does the production of PWOM and NWOM depend on the penetration (the proportion of the sample that produces it) and the frequency (the average production rate of those that produce it)? • How is the production of PWOM and NWOM related to a brand's market share?

• Is PWOM mostly about the consumer's main brand and NWOM mostly about other brands? • Do individuals who produce more NWOM about a category also produce more PWOM about the category? • Are similar results obtained when alternative measures of WOM are used?

We measure the incidence (occurrences in a period) of PWOM and NWOM. We investigate users of brands and choose established categories that are generally well known and used by most consumers. Thus, we are not concerned about the way in which WOM may be involved in the adoption of new categories, as studied, for example, by Rogers (2003).

There are a number of potential applications of this work. First, measures of the incidence of PWOM and NWOM may be used, together with measures of publicity, advertising, distribution, and other determinants of sales, to predict changes in demand for brands in a category. Second, evidence about the pattern of PWOM and NWOM may help managers promote PWOM and restrict NWOM and target segments of the user base that are particularly influential. Third, Bayus (1985) has shown that advertising may stimulate WOM, which suggests that marketers might be able to engender more WOM and raise the effectiveness of marketing communications if they had a better understanding of how WOM occurs. Fourth, we need to understand the general patterns evident in WOM in order to provide a basis for further theory development and research.

The paper is organized in two phases. In Phase 1, we first examine existing evidence on the occurrence of PWOM and NWOM and the circumstances in which PWOM and NWOM occur. Second, we discuss the expected linkage between the penetration and the frequency of WOM. Third, we describe measures and surveys and report results for 15 studies.

In Phase 2, we address issues arising from Phase 1 using further studies. First, we consider the possibility that the relative rates of incidence that we find for PWOM and NWOM could be distorted by a method artifact and report corroborative research using two alternative methods. Second, we report evidence on whether the brand cited in PWOM and NWOM is more often the respondent's main brand or another brand. Third, we present findings on how the incidence of PWOM and NWOM relates to brand market share.

Finally, we summarize our findings and discuss possible reasons for the patterns we find in WOM production. This summary leads us to develop six propositions to guide future research. We discuss limitations of our work and outline the implications of our observations for managerial practice.

2. Phase 1: review of related research

2.1. Positive information is more common than negative information

When markets are competitive, products do not easily survive if they cause dissatisfaction. As a result, unsatisfactory products disappear and most product experience is positive. Mittal and Lassar (1998) find that satisfied customers

outnumber dissatisfied customers by more than four to one in the health care and car repair services. Peterson and Wilson (1992) conducted a comprehensive review of studies of satisfaction in the United States; in one table, they show that, on average, 83% of customers were satisfied, with the remaining 17% being distributed between neutrality and dissatisfaction. Thus, unless dissatisfied customers are exceptionally vociferous, we would expect PWOM to exceed NWOM because there are so many more satisfied customers.

We note two studies comparing the volume of positive and negative advice. First, Godes and Mayzlin (2004) studied comment on television shows on Web sites; they find that positive film appraisals occurred nearly twice as often as negative appraisals. However, comments on Web sites may not reflect common WOM. Second, Naylor and Kleiser (2000) studied users of a health and fitness resort. They find that, of the 97 respondents, 94 engaged in positive comments and 62 in negative comments. All those who gave negative comments also gave some positive comments. Among those primarily engaging in negative comments, the frequency of comments was half the level of those who just engaged in positive comments. However, the Naylor and Kleiser study shows a very high satisfaction level with the resort, and other resorts giving less satisfaction could therefore receive more NWOM. A more representative method of studying the relative incidence of PWOM and NWOM covers all the brands in a category.

2.2. Do dissatisfied customers advise more people than satisfied customers?

Marketing textbooks such as those by Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger (1997) and Hanna and Wosniak (2001) and consultants such as Silverman (1997) have reported WOM ratios of two or three to one when comparing dissatisfied and satisfied customers. The origin of these reports appears to be studies by the Technical Assistance Research Program (TARP). A report from TARP suggests that NWOM from dissatisfied customers occurs, on average, about twice as frequently as PWOM from satisfied customers, though the ratio varies with the category (Goodman & Newman, 2003).

Academic research shows a rather different picture. Two single brand studies indicate less NWOM from dissatisfied customers than PWOM from satisfied customers (Engel, Kegerreis, & Blackwell, 1969; Holmes & Lett, 1977). However, these results may reflect the particular character of the brand studied. Anderson (1998), using the Swedish Customer Satisfaction Barometer and the American Customer Satisfaction Index, which cover many industries, finds greater WOM among those who were very dissatisfied compared with those who were very satisfied but noted:

“The widespread belief in a high degree of word of mouth by dissatisfied customers may be unwarranted. In fact, in a sizable proportion of cases, the difference between the two is probably not significant.”

Anderson's research reveals a U-shaped frequency of WOM across the satisfaction–dissatisfaction range. In the middle of this range, where respondents are neither satisfied nor

dissatisfied, the frequency of WOM production (among those producing it at least once) is still approximately 80% of the level that occurs with high satisfaction or high dissatisfaction. This finding suggests that factors other than satisfaction affect the production of WOM. Mangold, Miller, and Brockway (1999) illustrate some of these factors. They asked respondents to recall one positive and one negative communication made about a service in the previous six months, and then describe the circumstances that gave rise to the WOM. The predominant circumstance (50%) was the felt need of the receiver, usually prompted by a request for information. The next most common circumstance (19%) was coincidental communication when the advice arose out of a conversation. Mangold et al. find that the communicator's satisfaction or dissatisfaction was a primary circumstance in only 9% of the cases, and the receiver's satisfaction or dissatisfaction was primary in only 3% of the cases.

The work of Mangold et al. (1999) suggests that we should be careful about assuming that satisfied customers always produce PWOM and dissatisfied customers always produce NWOM. Because people often take into account the needs of others, they may give advice that is contrary to their feelings because the brand will, or will not, suit the other person. In some cases, they may give both positive and negative advice about the same brand to different persons. For example, we may recommend a Thai restaurant to someone who likes spicy cooking but advise against the restaurant when dealing with a person with less adventurous tastes.

2.3. Incidence, penetration, and frequency

The incidence of WOM depends on the proportion of the sample expressing it (the penetration) and the mean number of times those who express it do so (the frequency). It is useful to know whether the incidence occurs because many people (high penetration) give it infrequently or because few people (low penetration) give it very frequently. Thus, PWOM incidence might differ from NWOM incidence because of penetration, frequency, or both.

Normally, penetration and frequency are positively associated because these measures are statistically related. This effect is often seen as an association between the penetration and the frequency of purchase of brands in a category; bigger brands have greater penetration and purchase frequency than smaller brands, an effect called double jeopardy (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt, & Barwise, 1990). The association between penetration and frequency may also be found when we compare WOM about different categories; if a large proportion of a sample talks about brands in a category, we might expect those who talk about the brands to do so more frequently.

A number of conditions can affect the relationship between penetration and frequency. In the case of WOM, it may be difficult to work out a realistic NWOM penetration because many people may be unable to advise negatively through lack of knowledge of poor brands. Thus, an anomalous relationship between penetration and frequency may indicate a lack of relevant knowledge among users.

3. Phase 1: research

3.1. Method

Data on WOM were collected from users of the 15 categories listed in Table 1 (column 1) over the period 2001–03. All surveys were single-wave and were conducted in the United Kingdom. Samples from the general public were used, except in two cases where we used university samples. Some surveys covered two categories and these are reported as two separate studies. Questionnaires were delivered mainly by hand to households, with return by personal collection or free mail, but the surveys on restaurants and opticians used supplementary email and face-to-face distribution. For the ISP (general public), we used an Internet survey, and for the elementary school research, data were gathered via the schools (children took the questionnaire to their parents and brought it back to the school for collection). When hand delivery to households was employed, several locations were used to reduce location effects. Response rates (shown in Table 1) were generally high, ranging from 32 to 63%, except for the school study (14%) and the Internet study (2%).

3.2. Measures

In all cases, the form of the question used for PWOM was “In the last six months, how many times have you recommended any X?” For NWOM, we used, “In the last six months, how many times have you advised against X?” In the surveys, X was a dentist, bank, credit card provider, etc. For eight of the surveys, we used an ordinal response format: 0, 1, 2, 3–4, 5–8, more than 8. From responses, we derived an approximate figure for the number of WOM occurrences using scale midpoints or, in the case of the last response, 1.5×n. For example, if “3–4 times” was ticked, we treated this as 3.5, and “more than 8 times” was treated as 12. We were concerned that this method stopped extreme responses, so we allowed extreme responses in the subsequent seven studies by using an open response format: “Please write in (0, 1, 2, etc.)….” Less than half of 1% of responses were extreme (numbers greater than 10), and all responses were used.

Our measures of PWOM and NWOM depend on recall and will be affected by measurement bias. If the proportionate errors associated with PWOM and NWOM are the same, the ratio measure will be true, but we cannot assume so. Our approach is to present results based on the recall of WOM in this phase and address this problem in Phase 2.

4. Phase 2 research

In Phase 2, we address issues arising from the research in Phase 1. We use alternative measures of WOM to test the finding that PWOM occurs more often than NWOM, and we examine the extent to which a respondent's main brand is the object of PWOM and NWOM. We also explore the incidence of PWOM and NWOM in relation to market share. In Phase 2, we also introduce some non-U.K. data and vary the phrasing of the questions about WOM to generalize our findings further. Data for Phase 2 were collected in 2004–05.

4.1. Alternative measures of word of mouth

Research on WOM relies on the ability of survey respondents to recall their past behavior; recall is prone to bias, and because the bias could differ between PWOM and NWOM, it might distort the measurement of relative incidence. There is no clear evidence that biases affect PWOM and NWOM differentially, but a number of mechanisms could produce this effect. Social desirability effects (Fisher, 1993) could favor recall of PWOM, and Rosen and Tesser (1972) have suggested that people avoid making

negative statements for this reason. Also, Soderlund (1998) proposes that people process pleasant items more accurately and efficiently. Contrary to this argument, we note that receiving negative advice about brands is not normally unpleasant to the recipient. Also, the rarity of NWOM could give it surprise value or affect its diagnostic value, which might increase its recall. Aspects of an event that affect its retrieval from memory are discussed by Taylor (1982), and Godes and Mayzlin (2004) also review these issues.

To obtain unbiased measures, we should observe WOM as it occurs, but it is too infrequent in any category for this procedure to be feasible. An alternative is to induce WOM, for example in focus groups, but the moderation of focus groups could easily prime the production of one form of WOM at the expense of the other. Taking account of these problems, we selected two further methods for measuring the ratio of PWOM to NWOM.

4.1.1. Method 1: received WOM Phase 1 results were based on WOM that was given, so an alternative approach surveys WOM received. Recall is involved in both giving and receiving, but the biases may differ between the two methods. In new studies, we asked respondents to report both received and given WOM in the last six months, and we used two different question phrasings about received WOM: advise in favor and advise against, and advise positively and advise negatively.

5. General discussion

5.1. The importance of multiple methods

Research findings that are based on a single category may relate to particular features of that category. To establish generalizations, we need to study a number of categories. Similarly, consistent results across a variety of population samples, using different methods of measurement, help support a generalization. Such generalizations show empirical patterns that can provide a basis for prediction as well as theory development. The value of such empirical patterns has been illustrated by different authors in a special issue of Marketing Science (1995, Vol. 14, Issue 3).

Our research covers a number of categories and many samples and employs different methods for measuring WOM and distributing questionnaires. Each study may have weaknesses – for example, the distribution of questionnaires via friends could produce some shared responses – but overall, the wide empirical basis gives greater weight to the evidence. We now suggest explanations for our findings, following Bass's (1995) “Empirical–Theoretical” route. We summarize our results and then discuss and develop six propositions for further research.

5.2. Summary of results

We find that PWOM is more common than NWOM. This occurs because there are more people producing PWOM rather than because those individuals who produce PWOM do so more frequently than those producing NWOM. We corroborate our basic finding that PWOM is more common than NWOM by using two other measures of WOM. Categories receiving high levels of PWOM tend to receive high levels of NWOM, and those individuals who give NWOM are much more likely to give PWOM. We observe that PWOM is usually about the current main brand; NWOM is usually about past brands or brands that have never been owned. The occurrence of PWOM, and sometimes NWOM, is positively related to market share.

5.3. Propositions for further research

In the past, research on WOM often has been associated with customer satisfaction and dissatisfaction. The picture that emerges from our work attributes more importance to the salience of the category in everyday life, as well as to the interest and knowledge that people have about the category. We suggest that if a category is important to many people, advice will be more valuable, which raises the rate of both PWOM and NWOM. Similarly, if the brands in a category differ significantly, it will be more useful to recommend better brands and to warn others about weaker brands. If a category is frequently encountered, this should raise the incidence of both positive and negative advice.

Because those individuals who give NWOM are more likely to give PWOM, there are likely to be common individual-level determinants. Those who value and have frequent experience with a category, understand it well, and have more social contacts are likely to give more PWOM and more NWOM. These points are expressed in Propositions 1 and 2.

Proposition 1. Production of WOM at an aggregate level depends on the relative importance of the category to consumers, the differences between brands within the category and the prominence of the category in the everyday environment.

Proposition 2. Production of WOM at an individual level depends on the value, knowledge and experience of the category, as well as on the range of the individual's social contacts.

The association between the production of PWOM and market share is also important, though not surprising. Big brands have more customers, and customers usually recommend their main brand. Across five categories, the correlation between market share and PWOM is very high (Table 5). In the case of NWOM, the relationship with market share is less clear. Much of NWOM is about brands that have been used in the past, which would produce a positive association between NWOM and market share when brand shares do not change rapidly. But a large minority of NWOM is about brands that have never been owned, which will reduce any association between NWOM incidence and market share. Our data in Table 5 show that NWOM is correlated with market share in three out of five cases.

These data suggest that market share provides a benchmark for assessing comments about brands. Superior brand performance occurs when the rates of PWOM and NWOM run, respectively, above and below the market share rates. We propose Proposition 3 as a norm against which brands may be assessed.

Proposition 3. Within a category, the incidence of PWOM and NWOM for each brand tends to be a function of that brand's market share.

Although our findings confirm a positive association between penetration and frequency for PWOM, this relationship does not occur for NWOM. A possible explanation is that respondents often lack negative examples that could serve as the basis for NWOM. In some categories, such as cell phone handsets and airtime, most brands are familiar to users, so they may make negative judgments about these brands more easily. In these cases, the incidence of PWOM and NWOM may be closer (as they were in our work). In other categories, such as dentists, consumers are

likely to lack the knowledge required for NWOM, and correspondingly, we find much more PWOM than NWOM about dentists. If many people lack experience of negative examples, NWOM penetration figures will be artificially low, because they will have been calculated using all respondents in the category rather than by using the smaller number of respondents who could advise against a brand by virtue of their knowledge. If NWOM penetrations are computed only for those consumers who know of poor brands, penetrations will be greater and may then be consistent with the relatively high NWOM frequency figures. The lack of a coherent relationship between frequency and penetration for NWOM may occur because knowledge of negative examples varies widely across categories.

Proposition 4. The ratio of PWOM to NWOM in a category increases when knowledge of negative examples is limited.

A priority for further research is the relative impact of PWOM and NWOM. If we canmeasure theimpact ofPWOM andNWOM on brand choice, we would be able to combine the evidence from this research with findings on incidence and establish a metric for WOM influence. With such a metric, it might be possible to indicate the impact of current WOM on future brand sales.

Proposition 5. A measure combining the relative incidence and impact of PWOM and NWOM about brands could be an indicator of future changes in brand sales.

There is evidence in psychological research on judgment that negative information often has a greater impact than positive information. The most common explanation for this effect is that most information is positive, so negative information is rarer and more extreme compared with positive information (Fiske, 1980). The more common positive information may reflect a situation assumed by a recipient, whereas rarer negative information alerts people to facts they do not know.

However, psychological research concerns the formation of judgments, whereas we are interested in brand choice. Evidence in this area is meager, but Ahluwalia (2002) has shown that positive statements about brands have much the same impact as negative statements when the brands are familiar, as in our work. Thus, there is no clear evidence about the relative impact of PWOM and NWOM, and we opt for the following:

Proposition 6. The impact of PWOM on brand choice is much the same as the impact of NWOM in familiar categories.

5.4. Limitations

We noted previously a number of problems associated with the measurement of WOM. These problems are difficult to overcome. Our survey-based methods could be prone to biased recall, so it would be helpful to develop other methods to measure WOM to test our findings further.

Our purpose was to establish the main patterns in the reported occurrence of WOM in widely used categories. We excluded rarely used categories and non-users of categories;

also, we did not report on characteristics of WOM, such as whether it occurred between friends or acquaintances, whether it was requested or volunteered, or how strongly it was expressed. More studies are needed on these issues, as well as on the propositions detailed previously.

5.5. Some implications for practice: counteracting NWOM and promoting PWOM

How much effort should practitioners put into combating NWOM? We hesitate to suggest that practitioners should ignore NWOM, despite our evidence that NWOM is less common than PWOM. First, in some categories, NWOM is nearly as common as PWOM, and certain brands (especially those with small market share) may be the object of more NWOM than PWOM. It appears that, sometimes, the failures of a brand can cause NWOM to “take off” and be passed among non-users of the brand. Managers should be worried about this possibility. Second, we find that relatively few people produce NWOM but they do so quite often, which means that marketing efforts to reduce NWOM can focus on a limited number of persons, if these persons can be identified. Third, most of those who produce NWOM are also active producers of PWOM, so these people represent a double hazard. When consumers advise against a brand and recommend a competitive brand, the challenge for the manager is to reverse this pattern of advice.

The practical problem therefore becomes identifying those who give, or who are likely to give, NWOM. We suggest that there are three identifiable groups that may produce NWOM. One group comprises those consumers who complain to the supplier, because this activity is likely associated with negative advice to other consumers as well. A second group includes current users, a minority of whom may advise against their brand. For this group, we need to find out why these users advise against their supplier and whether they do so to all consumers or only to certain types of consumers with particular needs. The third and largest group is past users, who may believe that their current brand is superior to the one they used previously. Normally, companies contact past customers in recovery attempts, but even when these past customers cannot be recovered, it may be worth telling them about improvements in the brand that they previously owned if this would stop them from producing NWOM. All three of these groups can be identified, the first through their complaints and the second and third via databases of current and lapsed customers.

PWOM may be promoted via databases of users and also by using advertising. Bayus (1985) has shown that marketing communications may raise the level of WOM. It seems likely that some messages to customers are particularly effective because they contain ideas that are easily passed on; more research is required to identify the characteristics of such ideas.

5.6. Conclusion

Despite their clear importance in consumer choice, patterns of WOM production are poorly supported by empirical research. We have conducted systematic research in this area. Our multi-category

studies identify a number of general patterns of interest to managers and academics alike. We have reported and discussed these patterns and used them to develop six propositions to guide future research about the production of WOM at the category, brand, and individual level. We hope that other marketing researchers will join us in examining this important but neglected area.
