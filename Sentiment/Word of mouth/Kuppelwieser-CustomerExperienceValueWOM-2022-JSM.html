Abstract Purpose – The customer experience (CX), as revealed in the literature-based debate, has been variously viewed as either a driver or an outcome of customer-perceived value (CPV). However, the association of CPV, CX and word-of-mouth (WoM) behavior remains nebulous to date, thereby generating an important research gap. In response and to bridge this gap, this study aims to explore CX’s role in the CPV–WoM behavior relationship, the role of WoM behavior arising from CX and whether CX acts as a core mediator (vs a moderator) in the association of CPV and subsequent consumer-behavior outcomes. Design/methodology/approach – By conducting two studies spanning a broad range of services, this paper explores the relationship between CPV, CX, and WoM behavior through structural equation modeling. Findings – The findings are that CX plays a crucial role in the CPV–WoM relationship, thereby confirming the existence of a direct link between CPV (social/hedonic/utilitarian value), CX and WoM. The results also highlight CX’s mediating role in the relationship between social and utilitarian (but not hedonic) values. Moreover, the results reveal that the EXQ scale, measuring CX, comprises distinct experiences perceived by high and low CXbased customer segments, respectively. Practical implications – CPV (utilitarian, hedonic, social) not only affects consumers’ behavioral intentions but also, more importantly, their WoM behavior. Therefore, managers need to consider all three values. Moreover, managers should shift their focus from social value perceptions to CX. The results suggest that managers need to devote additional resources to the development of a suitable CX, which will help mitigate consumers’ online and/ or offline brand-related WoM. This study indicates the context in which managers must emphasize the construct that produces positive outcomes. Originality/value – By identifying a direct relationship between CPV, CX and the ensuing consumer-behavior outcomes, the study offers important theoretical insight into CX’s nomological network.

Keywords Consumer-perceived value (CPV), Customer experience (CX), Word-of-mouth (WoM) behavior, Mediator, Moderator, Utilitarian/hedonic/social value, Structural equation modeling

1. Introduction

Understanding the nature and dynamics of customer-perceived value (CPV) has long been regarded as a crucial component of designing and executing successful marketing strategies (Ulaga and Eggert, 2006). Consequently, the exploration of CPV and its marketing outcomes has been identified as a theoretical and practical priority (Kumar and Reinartz, 2016; Figueiredo and Scaraboto, 2016). Various scholars have emphasized CPV’s importance for service marketing research

(Kelleher et al., 2019; Vargo and Lusch, 2017; Zeithaml et al., 2020). In this respect, Grönroos and Voima (2013) maintain that CPV, which is widely defined as a trade-off between benefits (i.e. get) and sacrifices (i.e. give; Zeithaml, 1988), is crucial for “establishing the service perspective on marketing.”

Although CPV has been variously conceptualized as a unidimensional or a multidimensional construct (Gallarza et al., 2017), most studies adopt a unidimensional view of the concept (Chiu et al., 2014). Therefore, this approach treats all value-related aspects, including utilitarian, hedonic and social value, as reflecting a single value conceptualization (Kim et al., 2007; Hollebeek, 2013). Previous research mainly focused on estimating CPV’s nomological network-based associations, including customer satisfaction (Ryu et al., 2012), behavioral intentions and loyalty (Floh et al., 2014). However, only a few studies have linked CPV to specific consumer-behavior outcomes, including purchase or word-of-mouth (WoM) (Chang and Wang, 2011). Among others, researchers emphasized WoM as a key CPV outcome (Babic Rosario et al., 2016) because, in addition to exposing a consumer’s focal WoM-disseminating behavior, CPV also influences other behavioral outcomes (Cheung and Thadani, 2012), all of, which, in turn, affect firm performance (Ye et al., 2011). Likewise, Leroi-Werelds (2019) highlights that CPV, “besides its crucial role in decision-making, plays a key role in the post-purchase phase, as customers evaluate the value of the product or service, which determines their satisfaction, repurchase intentions and WoM.” The author implies that a service encounter’s ultimate outcome should be to create value-in-context, which is conducive to the development of a favorable customer experience (CX).

However, CPV’s link to CX remains tenuous (Komulainen et al., 2018; Andrews et al., 2012). Given the experiential, contextual, idiosyncratic and meaning-laden nature of value (Lusch and Vargo, 2014; Vargo and Lusch, 2008), it comes as no surprise that some scholars assume that CX affects the link between CPV and consumer behavior (Lin et al., 2014; De Keyser et al., 2020). Helkkula (2011) notes that CX is strongly linked to hedonic consumption, powerful emotions and enjoyment-derived value. Certain authors, including (Patrício et al., 2011; Candi et al., 2013), conceptualize CX more broadly to include multiple value perceptions that encapsulate emotionally engaging sensations (hedonic benefits), functional utility (utilitarian benefits) and social benefits (Chitturi et al., 2008). However, studies investigating CX’s nature and dynamics, through which it influences CPV and subsequent consumer behavior (e.g. WoM), remain few and far between (Klaus, 2014; Varshneya and Das, 2017).

Based on these gaps, we explore the relationship between CPV, CX and consumers’ behavioral intent/behavior. This research endeavor leads to the following study objectives:

To explore the direct effect of value on WoM and the effect of values on CX and WoM.

To investigate the proposed mediating role of CX in the value–WoM relationship.

To determine the moderating role of CX in the value– WoM relationship.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we analyze how different CPV dimensions (i.e. utilitarian, hedonic and social) affect consumers’ WoM behavior, defined as consumers’ dissemination of positive or negative product/service experiences (Anderson, 1998). Although several authors have assessed the CPV–WoM relationship (Kumar et al., 2007; Gruen et al., 2006), their inconsistent results generate the need to further test and validate the nature of this theoretical association. Throughout this study, we, therefore, aim to substantiate and solidify insights into this association by addressing prior, inconclusive results.

Second and relatedly, we explore CPV’s effect on CX and its subsequent impact on WoM behavior (Klaus, 2014). That is, in addition to the proposed direct relationship between CPV and WoM behavior, we explore the potential mediating effect of CX in this association. The addition of CX as a mediator is based on the following rationale. Although CPV may reflect a customer’s perceived value regarding a particular brand or firm interaction, the individual’s broader product/service experience throughout the purchase journey (i.e. CX; Lemon and Verhoef, 2016) may fully align with CPV generated by focal interactions. Consequently, it is of interest to explore the extent to which CPV affects CX (Nasution and Mavondo, 2008), as done in this study. In turn, as we expect CX to affect customers’ WoM behavior (Cetin and Dincer, 2014; Zhang et al., 2014), we also test this relationship in our study.

Third, in addition to the aforesaid mediating role of CX, we explore CX’s potential moderating role in the association of CPV and WoM behavior. That is, whereas the aforesaid mediating effect intuitively makes sense, it could be argued that a customer’s experience level acts as an important determinant of the strength of the association between CPV and WoM behavior, as expressed in a moderating relationship (De Keyser et al., 2015). In this respect, an elevated (weak) CX would strengthen (lower) the CPV–WoM behavior nexus, which also makes sense. Overall, our main contribution stems from empirically testing and validating the CPV–CX–WoM behavior-based nomological network by unraveling the nature of the relationship between the respective constructs, thereby adding more conclusive evidence results to the way in which these concepts are addressed.

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a review of the most important literature, followed, in Section 3, by the conceptual development undertaken in this study. Section 4 outlines our research design, followed by the presentation of the study’s main results in Section 5. Section 6 concludes the paper with an overview of important implications that arise from the research.

2. Theoretical foundations

In this section, we review important CPV literature and discuss the association of CPV with CX and WoM. First, we follow Holbrook’s (1994) and Rintamäki et al.’s (2006) multidimensional view of CPV based on the utilitarian (Babin et al., 1994), hedonic (Berlyne, 1970) and social value dimensions (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001; Sheth, 1981; Westbrook and Black, 1985). For the purposes of this research, we use Sweeney and Soutar’s (2001) PERVAL model as a theoretical foundation to develop utilitarian, hedonic and social values. Utilitarian value refers to “the utility derived from the (product’s) perceived quality and expected performance” (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001). This value is mainly functional, task-related and rational by nature (e.g. fair pricing, convenience; Babin et al., 1994). Hedonic value is “the utility derived from the feelings or affective states that a product generates” (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001). This emotional value is exemplified by consumer pleasure, fun and entertainment (Berlyne, 1970; Holbrook and Hirschman, 1982). Social value refers to “the utility derived from the product’s ability to enhance social self-concept” (Sweeney and Soutar, 2001). It

Second, most definitions highlight CPV’s customer-perceived nature, which is based on a customer’s personal, subjective evaluations of an offering, thus revealing CX’s key link to CPV (Berry et al., 2002). However, despite this insight, a dearth of research explores the dynamics through which CX influences the link between CPV and focal consumer-behavior outcomes. Third, given CX’s context-specific nature (Lemke et al., 2011), it is important to explore the CPV–CX–WoM relationship in different settings. We, therefore, advocate assessing all three clusters of Silvestro et al.’s (1992) service classification scheme to identify potentially differing crosscontextual dynamics (Klaus and Maklan, 2013). For our empirical research, we specifically chose a high-end professional service, a mass (grocery) service and a utilitarian service (retail banking). Given CX’s inclination to differ contextually (Lemke et al., 2011), it can either mediate or moderate the CPV–WoM link, depending on the specific CPV dimension (i.e. social, hedonic or utilitarian; see (Jamshidi et al., 2017; Walls, 2013). Fourth, having previously introduced the notions of utilitarian, hedonic and social value, we subsequently delve further into their respective links with CX and ensuing consumer behaviors. Utilitarian and hedonic values have been found to be closely linked to online and offline CX (Lin and Kuo, 2016). For example, Wan et al. Although social value’s link to CX has received less attention, it is presumed to exert a positive effect on CX (Kang and Lee, 2018). Given the proposed effect of social media on CX (Hollebeek et al., 2014; Klaus, 2013), scholars have mainly explored the social value–CX relationship in online settings (Srivastava and Kaul, 2014). Kang and Lee (2018), for example, drew on experiments to investigate the impact of social presence/value on e-service-based CX. Moreover, Chen and Lin (2015) and Zhang et al. 3. Method

We designed two quantitative studies to shed light on CX’s role in the CPV–WoM relationship. Study 1 uses a series of exploratory factor analyses (EFAs), focusing on an exploration of the factor structures of our conceptual model’s constructs. Study 2 validates the scales in subsequent confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and applies them in a structural equation model. When altering the model’s depicted relationships, we gained insights into CX’s role in the CPV–consumer-behavior outcome relationship.

3.1 Study 1 – measurement and scales We first collected 150 customers’ data covering each of Silvestro et al.’s (1992) major service types (Dagger et al., 2007). The UK-based market research agency collected the responses of the UK customers from their existing panel. To qualify for survey participation, respondents had to confirm that they have had an experience with the service provider in question during the six weeks prior to receiving the survey. Responses were obtained through an online platform. As previously mentioned, the data set originated from a professional service (n = 57), the respondents’ main bank (n = 47) and their main grocery retailer (n = 46). Table 4 provides an overview of the data set’s descriptive statistics.

We adapted the scale items from established measures and adjusted them to the particular research context of this study. Utilitarian value was gauged by adapting relevant items from the scales of Sweeney and Soutar (2001), Jones et al. (2006) and Anderson and Srinivasan (2003). Hedonic value was measured by using the scales of Sweeney and Soutar (2001), Soutar and Sweeney (2003) and Jones et al. (2006). Social value was assessed by using Sweeney and Soutar’s (2001) and Rintamäki et al.’s (2006) items. We used Brown et al.’s (2005) established seven-item scale, which has been widely adopted (Sweeney et al., 2012) and used to measure WoM in CX research (Klaus and Maklan, 2013). The appendix provides an overview of the items used. To measure CX, we followed the guidance offered by e.g. Lemon and Verhoef (2016) and used a multidimensional scale encompassing multiple touchpoints. We applied Klaus’s (2012, 2013) EXQ scale, validated by subsequent research (Roy, 2018; Deshwal, 2016). Lemon and Verhoef (2016) recommend Klaus’s EXQ scale as a more advanced CE measure than, for example, the more “exploratory measures” (p. 69) offered, among others, by Brakus et al.’s (2009) brand experience scale. Prior to our study, several researchers have drawn on the EXQ scale and its underlying theoretical considerations (Chang and Chiu, 2016).

Following Klaus and Maklan (2013) and recent works on EXQ (Kuppelwieser and Klaus, 2021), we started with an EFA to extract appropriate items from the relevant scales for our research context. Brand experience, service provider experience and post-purchase experience were each included in a principal axis factor (PAF) analysis with promax rotation (k = 4). 4. Discussion and theoretical and practical implications

In this paper, we set out to address three objectives deemed crucial to advance knowledge of the CPV–consumer-behavior outcomes relationship and of how CX affects this relationship. We subsequently discuss our findings and their key implications for marketing theory and practice in greater depth. First, our finding is that CPV (utilitarian, hedonic and social) not only affects consumers’ behavioral intentions but, more importantly, also their WoM behaviors, thereby adding this insight to the literature. More specifically, in line with Lin et al. (2005), we provide insight into the ways in which CPV affects consumer behavior, thereby extending it beyond the realm of behavioral intentions. By investigating the relationship between utilitarian, hedonic and social CPV and WoM behavior, we not only contribute to our understanding of the way in which CPV drives behavior but also explore how CX affects these associations.

Second, since this – to the best of our knowledge – is the first empirical study to identify a direct relationship between CPV, CX and consumer behavior, it makes a related theoretical contribution. This contribution allows managers, who until now have tended to rely on CPV’s effect on consumers’ behavioral intentions, to take a significant stride forward.

Consequently, our finding that CPV exerts an important effect on the ensuing consumer behavior (i.e. WoM) has important practical implications, as managers may want to allocate additional resources to ensure a suitable customer value experience, which could help mitigate consumers’ online and/ or offline brand-related WoM (Hollebeek et al., 2019). As WoM can be either positive or negative, we advise managers to regard our findings as a key strategic imperative that could enhance their businesses’ performance outcomes. Depending on their brands’ nature, managers might also want to determine which of utilitarian, hedonic or social value is most important in their particular context, and to draw appropriate strategic conclusions from their analyzes (e.g. by focusing – in line with Study 2’s results – more on utilitarian and social value and less on hedonic value).

Third, our finding is that CX has a mediating effect on the CPV–WoM relationship, which indicates that CX reduces this relationship’s strength between customers’ value perceptions and their WoM behavior. However, our finding is also that this mediating effect only applies to social and utilitarian value perceptions. The CX mediating effect is dominant when consumers perceive a social or a utilitarian value, but more specifically the case when they perceive a social value. We found no mediating effect in respect of hedonic value perceptions. These findings imply that – in terms of social value derived from interactions with others – CX can weaken the relationship between social value and WoM behavior. Emphasizing social value leads to less WoM behavior. Consequently, if the managerial emphasis is on increasing positive WoM behavior, managers should shift their focus from social value perceptions to CX. Conversely, reduced negative WoM is expected to benefit the firm, which means that specific WoM communications’ valence should also be taken into account.

How can managers achieve this? They could, for example, give customers an opportunity to reflect on and recall their brand-related experiences by soliciting customer feedback or raising their awareness of specific CX-based touchpoints (e.g. in-store, online) by offering brand-related incentives, free gifts, etc. The same holds for utilitarian value perceptions, which CX also mediates to a significant extent. We therefore specifically suggest that utilitarian value requires the necessary items or needs (jobs to be done) to be fulfilled, to allow the CX of this fulfillment to become the focus, thereby mediating the relationship between utilitarian value perceptions and WoM.

Consequently, if a product or the experience of obtaining a product fails to fulfill consumers’ utilitarian needs, this part of their experience strongly influences their willingness to engage in (negative) WoM behavior, even when they enjoyed the experience in other respects (e.g. high hedonic value). We, therefore, identify a tiering or hierarchy of consumer needs that impact on the nature of their experience, with utilitarian needs (if present) trumping hedonic or social needs, particularly to meet core services (e.g. retail banking) required by or the core needs of the consumers.

Social value arises when, for example, we share our CX with others, by definition offering a strong link to WoM behavior. In addition, consumers tend to focus on hedonic value perceptions more inwardly. Within themselves, their willingness to share their brand-related experiences with others and to engage in WoM behavior (Groeger et al., 2016) may to a lesser extent be due to the nature of the hedonic value. However, these value types may significantly overlap, for example, when consumers enjoy brand-related social interactions (e.g. dance classes).

Our findings imply that managers of hedonic value brands should not only emphasize this hedonic value in brand-related communications but should also emphasize the outcome of customers’ brand-related experiences (e.g. achieving a sense of delight, competence, etc., after using the service) as opposed to focusing on the experience as such (i.e. while using the service). This approach is expected to work well in respect of aspirational brands, especially when consumers strive to achieve particular goals and to better themselves in some way (e.g. education or beauty and the use of physical appearancerelated products, such as weight loss items and haircare products). As such, the nature of these customer outcomes could become utilitarian in their own way (e.g. having shinier hair after using a specific shampoo), thereby offering not only a hedonic but also a utilitarian benefit.

Fourth, we find that CX does not moderate the CPV–WoM behavior relationship. Fundamentally, researchers need invariant models if they intend to test for moderation. However, our methodological process did not support the high and low CX groups’ use of invariant models, which implies that the final models could not be compared (moderated). This surprising result suggests that high and low CX differ more fundamentally than previously assumed, as customers in the high-CX group do more than merely rate their CX higher than that of the customers in the low group. From a methodological perspective, the results suggest that the CX scale should include these two groups’ distinct experiences.

5. Future research directions and limitations

Despite the insights revealed by our findings, we encourage researchers to conduct more research on this topic and research

area to validate our findings and refine our results. Researchers could, for example, use different behavioral outcomes (rather than WoM) and switch behavior to explore the effects of CX on firm-based performance measures, such as share-of-category or share-of-wallet. We also advise scholars to explore these and related dynamics in different research settings, such as online/ offline, tangible goods/services and different industry contexts. Researchers should also examine Silvestro et al.’s (1992) service types or other service typologies to establish their individual differences across service contexts.

As we focus on CX’s generic role in services, this paper does not analyze the different CX dimensions. Further research should therefore examine low and high CX groups in different contexts and identify their underlying evaluation dimensions. For example, it is essential to know – if indeed the case – in what context and why a specific dimension or a combination of the three EXQ dimensions drives consumer behavior more than any other dimension or combination of dimensions. Additionally, we encourage researchers to expand our research by exploring other settings than the setting we investigated. For example, it would be advantageous to investigate whether and, if the case, how a more hedonic setting, such as tourism or a luxury setting, confirms our findings. The same applies if researchers were to explore online settings and/or extend their research scope to omnichannel experiences. We also encourage researchers to investigate if a brand’s nature and orientation in terms of value (social, hedonic and utilitarian or any combination thereof) determine CX perception and what exactly drives their consumer behavior.
