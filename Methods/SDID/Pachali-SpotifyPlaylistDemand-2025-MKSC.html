What Drives Demand for Playlists on Spotify?

Max J. Pachali,a,* Hannes Dattaa

1. Introduction Digital platforms have increasingly replaced traditional revenue streams across various industries, including music, where streaming platforms now account for 84% of total revenues in the United States (Friedlander and Bass 2023). Users seeking to consume music can either search for particular songs or albums or browse thou sands of playlists—that is, curated lists of songs centered around a specific genre, mood, activity, event, or artist. Playlists have contributed to a significant expansion in music consumption on streaming services (Datta et al. 2018), accounting for about 30% share of listening time on Spotify. Another 30% of music consumption is from tracks saved in users’ music libraries (Spotify 2018), which often get discovered by consumers via playlists. Many of the most popular playlists are designed and professionally managed by Spotify. With 15,000 play lists, Spotify attracts 63% of platform-wide playlist fol lowers (Aguiar and Waldfogel 2021). Other playlists on the platform are either curated by users or professional

editors—including major and independent music labels. Finally, Spotify features some playlists across various categories on the Spotify app’s Search Page. This chang ing directory of lists features mostly playlists designed internally by Spotify.

Despite the growing importance of playlists to music consumption, not much is known about why users pre fer some playlists over others. For example, users may follow playlists because they are prominently featured on the platform, giving them a shortcut to finding music they want to listen to from Spotify’s vast assortment. Alternatively, users may follow playlists because they contain tracks by famous artists such as Taylor Swift (Universal), Adele (Sony), or Ed Sheeran (Warner). In our empirical analysis, we compare how adding tracks from popular major label artists to a playlist causally affects daily playlist followers in the short term (i.e., using variation within a month) versus the impact of the playlist being featured on the Search Page of the Spotify app. Before our work, little was known about how

We use two complementary methods to estimate these effects: a two-way fixed effects model (Shapiro et al. 2021) and a synthetic difference in difference model (Arkhangelsky et al. 2021). Our analysis builds on a unique data set collected from Chartmetric.com that tracked more than 30,000 playlists across 66 music cate gories between October 2019 and March 2020. The data include the number of daily aggregate playlist followers, as well as various characteristics such as whether play lists add tracks from famous artists of the three major record labels (Universal, Sony, and Warner), the share of content on the playlist owned by these labels, the dura tion of the playlist, and the acoustic attributes of tracks (Boughanmi and Ansari 2021). Our data set also records which playlists were featured prominently on the plat form’s Search Page on a given day, obtained through live scraping from everynoise.com/worldbrowser.cgi (Boegershausen et al. 2022).

Our identification strategy relies on the synthetic dif ference in difference (SDID) method to estimate treat ment effects (e.g., the effect of featuring playlists on the Search Page) relative to control playlists that closely mimic treated playlists but that have not received the treatment (Berman and Israeli 2022). For this purpose, the SDID method reweighs the large pool of control playlists to match pre-exposure trends between treated and control observations, reducing the reliance on paral lel trend assumptions as in conventional difference in difference approaches (Arkhangelsky et al. 2021). We also infer treatment effects in a flexible two-way fixed effects (2W-FE) model accounting for date and playlist- month fixed effects (Shapiro et al. 2021). We find that the treatment effects are consistent between the SDID and 2W-FE models, demonstrating robustness with regard to different model specifications and assumptions.

Our SDID estimates reveal that users strongly value playlists featured in the Spotify app, implying that daily followers go up by 0.95% if a playlist is included on the Search Page. For large playlists like “Reggaeton Classic” (which has 1.9 million followers), being prominently fea tured on the Spotify app can yield 18,050 additional daily followers. This finding indicates that Spotify is effective in guiding consumer searches of its vast playlist assortment through what it features on its Search Page. In comparison, we find that users are about half as sensi tive to the inclusion in a playlist of tracks by popular major label artists—adding at least one track by a major label superstar artist increases daily followers by 0.45%.

Our analysis of playlist demand has two main implica tions. First, our estimates show that playlist curators attract followers twice as easily when their list is featured on the Search Page as when they add a track by a major label superstar. Historically, the major music labels controlled content distribution in the music industry and

exerted influence over how music was advertised and sold offline (du Gay and Negus 1994, Prey 2020). Today, however, our findings corroborate researchers’ observa tions that music streaming platforms have higher in fluence over users’ listening behavior compared with labels—such as by promoting songs via playlists to the advantage of some producers over others (Aguiar and Waldfogel 2021).

Second, our results imply that superstar artists remain a relevant asset for major music labels. This insight is of interest to record labels and music streaming platforms, who are disputing royalty rates in the United States.2

Our findings could aid judges in assessing the consumer value of major label content on music streaming plat forms: the inclusion of major label superstar tracks in playlists draws significant attention, as reflected by its positive (short-term) effect on followers.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the data. Section 3 discusses causal identifica tion challenges and presents two model specifications that address these challenges: the two-way fixed effects model and the synthetic difference in difference model. Section 4 presents the treatment effect estimates and dis cusses robustness checks. We offer concluding thoughts and discuss the implications of our results in Section 5.

2. Data 2.1. Data Set Construction Our data set covers 34,483 playlists on Spotify from vari ous professional curators, including the platform, and major and independent music labels. We created this data set using the API of Chartmetric.com, a platform that tracks the composition (i.e., which songs are avail able on a list at any point in time) and performance (e.g., daily followers) of playlists available on Spotify.

We initially obtained a list of all 1.2 million playlists tracked by Chartmetric in April 2020. This cross- sectional data set includes playlist IDs and names (e.g., “Happy Tunes”), curator names (e.g., “Spotify”), tag words describing a playlist’s content, and followers at the time of data retrieval. We group playlist curators as per the classification in Online Appendix A. In addition to the cross-sectional characteristics, we obtained daily data on a playlist’s number of followers from October 2019 to March 2020, along with information on which song was available on a list and when.3

To understand which type of songs are included on playlists, we parse comprehensive track-level meta- information of about 2 million tracks. For each track, we observe its duration (in minutes), release date, and record label. We further classify whether each track is (1) distributed by one of the major record labels (Uni versal, Sony, or Warner)4 and (2) released by popular “superstar” artists, based on historical top 10 place ments in the Billboard Charts (Top 100; 1999–2019) and

Collectively, the major music labels account for most of the superstar content on the plat form (6,000+ tracks). Tracks by these popular artists are also added to more playlists compared with the remain ing artists on the platform. Our data contain numeric measures characterizing the tracks’ acoustic attributes (as in Boughanmi and Ansari (2021), i.e., danceability, energy, speechiness, acousticness, instrumentalness, live ness, valence, tempo, loudness). We aggregate this infor mation to the playlist level by computing a weighted average of the attributes at every date.6

We next augment the data with daily information about which playlists are featured on the Search Page of the Spotify app. Figure 1 illustrates featured playlists in the “Focus” category. Everynoise.com makes the playlists featured on Spotify’s Search Page available in real-time across up to 50 categories and 49 countries. To capture daily fluctuations in featured playlists, especially considering our data’s global coverage, we scraped this website at three-hour intervals between October 2019 and March 2020 (Boegershausen et al. 2022). Our raw data record a timestamp, the name of the category, the country name, and a list of playlist IDs featured in a category and country at the time of data retrieval. Most categories on the Search Page per tain to established genres (e.g., “pop,” “rock,” “jazz,” “EDM”). Other categories are relatively new, such as activity categories (e.g., “sleep,” “workout”) or mood categories (e.g., “inspirational”). Categories are largely the same across countries, but featured playlists may differ based on users’ location. Playlists can therefore be featured in multiple categories and countries simultaneously.

As Spotify does not provide a categorization for all of its playlists, we developed our own categorization based

on association rule mining (Hastie et al. 2009), which aligns with the categories observed on Spotify’s Search Page. Tracks are added to or removed from playlists, and the character of a playlist—and consequently, its competitors—can change. To account for the dynamic nature of a playlist’s content, we allow for weekly cate gory changes. The largest category is “pop” (3,979 play lists with combined followers of 336 million), followed by “hiphop+pop,” a hybrid category consisting of both “hiphop” and “pop” tracks (281 million followers). We provide an overview of the top 20 categories and details on the categorization algorithm in Online Appendix C.

The follower distribution of playlists is highly skewed: 1,105 playlists account for 50% of total followers across the 1.2 million playlists (see Table B2 in Online Web Appendix B). To avoid including extremely unpopular playlists, we select the 95% most-followed playlists for subsequent analyses. Some playlists are less frequently tracked than others, and we require playlist followers to be consecutively observed for at least four weeks. In addition, we impose that at most two intermediate (daily) follower observations can be linearly interpolated and require playlist characteristics to be consecutively available for at least 100days. Our final selection encom passes 34,483 playlists that fulfill the aforementioned conditions, accounting for 90% of platform followers at the time of data retrieval. We summarize the final data set in Table B3 in Online Appendix B.

3. Empirical Strategy and Modeling Approaches The main goal of our study is to estimate the causal effect of featuring playlists on Spotify’s Search Page and compare it to the effect of adding songs by exceptionally popular major label artists to playlists. We first describe the identification challenges associated with these two primary goals and then present two modeling ap proaches that address these challenges—the two-way fixed effects model and the staggered synthetic differ ence in difference model. All our estimates reflect short- term treatment effects as we are relying on the variation in featuring on the Search Page or adding tracks by major label superstars across weeks within a month. Estimates of the long-term effects on playlist followers of these demand factors goes beyond our data coverage.

untreated in each period, and we do not observe the counterfactual outcome in the unobserved condition. (ii) The decision of whether to feature a playlist or add pop ular tracks may be endogenous in the sense that it may be correlated with Spotify’s or the focal curators’ expec tations about changes in demand for the playlist. The two-way fixed effects model assumes that condi tional on the rich fixed effects and control variables, the residual variation in Figure D1 is quasi-random—that is, not correlated with unobserved demand factors. To jus tify this assumption, one might argue that user demand for a playlist does not drastically change within a month. Hence, variations in the composition of the playlist’s songs and the Search Page are quasi-random within weeks of a month. However, we cannot observe the sta bility of users’ preferences for playlists, and Spotify may update its playlists/Search Page in response to demand shocks within a month. In other words, the main limita tion of this model pertains to the identification challenge (ii) as there might be endogenous selection of remaining playlists into the treatment and control groups, even conditional on including playlist-month fixed effects. To address this concern, we would ideally want to create a control group of playlists that closely resemble treated playlists in the days before the event but do not get trea ted in our data.12 Another potential issue with the two- way fixed effects model is that it does not adequately account for the different timing of treatment effects (challenge iii), potentially biasing estimation results if treatment effects can change over time (“dynamic treat ment effects”) due to a “bad comparisons” problem (Baker et al. 2022). To mitigate these limitations, we pro pose an alternative model.

The SDID’s crucial “exclusion restriction” assumption is that the synthetic control playlists precisely match the follower evolution of the treated playlists in the times before the event.13 Treating a playlist can then be regarded as quasi-random compared with its synthetic control unit. To justify this assumption, we present the mean squared percentage error (MSPE) between the treated playlist’s followers and the synthetic control playlists over the 15days prior to the event, detailed in Table 4. The small MSPE highlights a strong fit, facili tated by leveraging thousands of never-treated donor units to generate synthetic “clones” for treated playlists. There are two potential challenges (iv and v) to this exclusion restriction assumption that we address as fol lows. First, Spotify or other curators may have concur rently implemented other changes after treatment (identification challenge iv). We checked the stability in playlists’ characteristics after treatment in Table E1 in Online Appendix E. We found that curators changed almost none of the covariates after their playlists got fea tured on the Search Page or tracks by exceptionally pop ular major label artists were added. Second, we use only donor playlists from categories other than the treated units to ensure that the stable unit treatment value assumption is not violated (Goldfarb et al. 2022).

3.3.1. Playlist Featuring. We use data on all playlists we observed being featured for the first time. To have sufficient pretreatment data, we require playlists to have been observed not featured for at least two weeks. We retain observations 15days before and 15days after treatment and require that playlists remain treated in the after-period for at least 7days. Choosing such a narrow analysis window mitigates the impact of confounding variables (Orhun et al. 2016). These steps led to a sample of 113 treated playlists on 23 unique dates.

Featured observations include more popular playlists such as “Reggaeton Classics” (1.9 million followers), as well as less popular playlists such as “Tech House Chargers” (4.5k followers). Most of the featured playlists were curated by Spotify, indicating that Spotify tends to prefer selecting its own playlists for inclusion on the Search Page.14 Figure D2 in Online Appendix D illustrates the variation in treatment dates across treated playlists. In line with Figure 2, we see that playlists were included on many different dates during our observation period.

3.3.2. Addition of Tracks from Popular Major Label Artists. We define the treatment as adding at least one track to a playlist by a major label “superstar” artist

included in the Spotify Top 10 between December 2016 and July 2019, or the U.S. Billboard Top 10 between 1999 and 2019. Artists include established superstars such as Alicia Keys, Jennifer Lopez, and Ed Sheeran, as well as more recent superstars such as Shawn Mendes, Billie Eil ish, and Lizzo. Again, we keep treated playlists that are observed at least 15days before and 15days after treat ment, with the requirement that newly added tracks be listed for at least 7days after the event. It is possible that playlists can be “treated” multiple times; in these few cases, we select only the first treated event of a playlist (under the restriction that the second treatment did not happen in the 15days after the first treatment). These steps lead to a sample of 2,248 treated playlists on 107days. Figure D3 in Online Appendix D shows the treatment variation plot for the selected events.

4.2. Robustness Checks We performed additional analyses to provide robustness of our empirical estimates. First, we have dropped the “2019” category celebrating hits of the past year for esti mating the effect of being featured on the Search Page. However, variations in other categories might also be implemented by Spotify in response to seasonality in demand (such as playlists being featured in the “Exercise” or “Yoga” categories at the beginning of the year when users make New Year’s resolutions). We removed these seasonal categories from the data and re-estimated all models. As shown in Table G1 in Online appendix G, the elasticities for the SDID specification are almost identical. The 2W-FE estimate drops after remov ing potentially seasonal categories but is not statistically different from the overall 2W-FE estimate in Table 4.

Second, we show the robustness of our results with regard to the choice of the stabilization constant (k  1). We re-estimated the models using the inverse hyperbolic sine transformation to deal with the problem of zeros in the logarithmic specifications. As can be seen in Table G2 in Online Appendix G, the results are very close to the main results in Table 4.

5. Summary and Discussion We collected a diverse panel data set covering 30,000+ popular playlists on Spotify to estimate the causal treat ment effect of featuring playlists on Spotify’s Search Page and compare the impact of being featured on this high- traffic page with the effect of adding tracks by popular major label superstar artists to playlists. We find that users are responsive to the featuring of playlists, and our estimates suggest that inclusion on Spotify’s Search Page raises daily followers by 0.95%. For large playlists like “Reggaeton Classic” (with 1.9 million followers), this implies an increase of 18,050 additional daily followers after being featured in Spotify’s app. By comparison, we find the effect of including tracks by popular major label superstars in a playlist to be 0.45%. Our analysis reveals that the average treatment effect of a playlist being fea tured is about two times greater than the effect of adding a track by a “superstar” major label artist to the list.

Our work has important implications for policy makers and music industry professionals. First, as major music labels have watched their market share on music streaming services erode in recent years, some music executives have started to wonder about the value of their “superstar” artists.18 However, our research sug gests that “superstars” continue to create value on music streaming services: consumers respond to adding major label superstars with an estimated increase in daily fol lowers of 0.45% in the 15days after the event. This find ing is of interest to record labels who are disputing royalty rates with streaming platforms in the United States.19 Our findings can also aid court judges in asses sing the consumer value of major label content on music streaming platforms like Spotify.

Second, we measure the short-term treatment effect of including playlists on the Search Page, a gateway to the app’s content where a select number of playlists— typically Spotify-curated playlists—are brought to the user’s attention. Our finding that followers increase by 0.95% implies that Spotify is effective in guiding user attention by selectively highlighting playlists on the plat form (Goldfarb and Tucker 2019). Importantly, the fact that the featuring effect is about twice the size of the superstar effect suggests the success of Spotify playlists is much less dependent on the availability of artist- specific (superstar) tracks than it is to being prominently featured on the platform. We believe this difference in effect may in the future drive record labels to pay Spotify to be prominently featured.20

To foster competition among playlist curators, policy makers could legally impose a minimum fraction of non-Spotify playlists that need to be featured on the plat form’s Search Page on a given day. Spotify already fea tures its own curated playlists in almost 90% of cases (Table 3). A policy requiring Spotify to feature a mini mum percentage of non–Spotify-curated playlists would be analogous to recently implemented EU legislation requiring streaming services like Apple TV+ and Netflix to feature at least 30% European content or face a European Union customer block.21

Our work opens several avenues for inquiry about the economics of streaming. First, our data does not incorpo rate information on all potential factors influencing users’ demand for playlists, such as “announcements” to adver tise playlists or concerts on Spotify. Second, we study playlist followers but lack sufficient data on a playlist’s number of listeners. Third, the results extend to the set of 30,000+ most popular playlists on the platform. How ever, a larger sample of playlists would allow for a better understanding of how Spotify can exert power over music labels through algorithmic design. Fourth, our data primarily allows us to identify short-term treatment effects. Yet, for music labels, understanding the long-term dynamics of a playlist’s follower evolution is important to develop successful playlist brands. For policymakers, in turn, long-term estimates would enable a more nuanced understanding of the power dynamics between digital platforms and their suppliers to devise effective regulatory policies.
