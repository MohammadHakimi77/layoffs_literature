The Power of Star Creators: Evidence from the Live Streaming Industry

Kun Qian and Ying Xie

Abstract In this article, the authors examine the impact of star creator exits on digital content markets, leveraging exogenous events on Twitch. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences approach, they estimate the causal effects of star creators’ exits from the live streaming platform. Their findings reveal that, at the live streaming category level, the exit of a star creator leads to a sizable decrease in both content provision and consumption within the star creator’s focal category. At the individual creator level, the effects are heterogeneous, varying by the creator’s audience size and contractual status with the platform. Major creators reduce their content provision, while micro creators maintain their streaming activity, though both groups experience a decline in viewership. Additionally, contracted creators tend to sustain their content provision levels following a star creator’s exit, whereas noncontracted creators exhibit a significant decline. Lastly, the authors find that the impact of a star creator’s departure persists for several months, indicating a lasting effect at both the category and individual levels.

Keywords market exit, stardom, content creation, live streaming, synthetic difference-in-differences

The proliferation of digital content platforms has transformed content creation, enabling millions of users worldwide to generate and distribute content. As of December 2024, YouTube, the leading video-sharing platform, hosted over 114 million active channels with 500 hours of new video uploaded every minute (Hayes 2024). The sustainability of these platforms hinges on a continuous provision of high-quality content in large volume and variety. To stimulate content creation, platforms employ various incentives, including monetary rewards (in the form of revenue sharing and consumer donations), creator tools (e.g., YouTube Studio editor), and creator recruitment or retention programs (e.g., YouTube Partner Program).

Despite these efforts, success among content creators remains highly concentrated. Only an estimated .06% of YouTube creators have surpassed the 1 million subscriber milestone, a threshold often associated with financial viability (Kumar 2025). This concentration is particularly pronounced in digital entertainment, where a small number of star creators dominate consumer attention. For instance, PewDiePie, a prominent YouTuber, amassed 110 million subscribers and 29 billion total views by the end of 2024. This heavy reliance on a few star creators raises concerns about market stability and diversity. While these creators drive engagement and revenue, their dominance may also pose barriers for emerging creators seeking visibility and audience growth.

Given the skewed nature of digital content markets, understanding the role of star creators in shaping content provision and consumption is crucial for effectively managing these complex markets. Although the rise of star creators has received increased media attention, many fundamental questions remain unanswered. The implications of star creators for the content provision incentives of nonstar creators are particularly ambiguous. On the one hand, star creators can enhance the platform’s ecosystem by attracting a larger audience, potentially benefiting all creators. On the other hand, their dominance may intensify competition, making it harder for nonstar creators to gain visibility and sustain content production. Moreover, these effects may vary significantly depending on a creator’s audience size, platform engagement, and contractual agreements. The coexistence of multiple mechanisms and the high degree of heterogeneity in creators underscore the complexity of star creators’ market influence and the need for empirical investigation.

Drawing from extant literature on prominent firms’ market entry and exit, stardom, and content creation incentives, we develop and test hypotheses predicting that: (1) a star creator’s market exit will reduce both content consumption and provision in the affected category, and (2) at the individual level, the response of nonstar creators to a star’s departure will vary based on their audience size and contractual status. Specifically, we expect that major creators (those with a substantial following) will experience a greater negative impact on both content provision and viewership compared with micro creators (those catering to niche audiences). Additionally, we predict that noncontracted creators will be more adversely affected than contracted creators.

To empirically test these hypotheses, we conduct a quasi-experimental study by leveraging unexpected exits of star creators from Twitch (Twitch.tv). The study centers on Ninja (Richard Tyler Blevins), who exited from Twitch in August 2019. Given Ninja’s extensive engagement with the online video game Fortnite (FN), this game’s live streaming category experienced a significant disruption after his departure, which we use as the treated category of our analysis. We select five streaming categories that housed other star creators throughout the study period to construct a synthetic control unit. At the individual level, we consider nonstar creators who actively streamed FN-related content as our treatment group, and active nonstar creators in the five control categories as our control group. Following Arkhangelsky et al. (2021), we develop a synthetic difference-in-differences (SDiD) model to estimate the causal effects of the star creator’s exit on both the provision and consumption of live streaming in FN. Our results indicate a marked 18% decrease in daily FN content provision and a 26% decrease in daily content consumption after Ninja’s exit. At the individual level, we find that the incumbent major creators reduce their daily FN streaming duration by approximately 15 minutes on average, an 8% decline relative to their baseline before the star exit, while the effect on the micro creators is statistically insignificant. Meanwhile, both the major and micro creators incurred losses in their streaming viewership following the exit despite the major creators losing more in percentage terms (22% vs. 7%). Our investigation into the heterogeneous treatment effect across contracted versus noncontracted creators further shows that committed content production, constrained by the contract, mitigates the negative effect caused by a star creator’s exit: While the noncontracted creators significantly reduced their daily streaming duration after Ninja’s exit, the contracted creators maintained their content provision effort. In addition, our analysis reveals that the influence of a star creator’s departure extends over several months, indicating a lasting effect at both category level and individual level. Lastly, to validate the robustness of our findings, we replicate the analysis using a second star creator exit —Shroud (Michael Grzesiek) in October 2019.

Our article makes several key contributions. First, our study explores the market power of prominent players in a new empirical context. The literature suggests that the entry or exit of a prominent firm can reshape market competition and influence

incumbent firms’ pricing behaviors in the retail and hospitality industries. We provide empirical evidence on the market power of star creators, the prominent players in digital content markets, demonstrating that their departure significantly reduces the quantity of content production. Second, we contribute to the stardom literature by investigating the influence of star creators in the market of live streaming, a new yet very popular digital entertainment form. Our study is one of the first to examine how the presence of a star player affects the supply of creative efforts in the entertainment industry. Our results suggest that the departure of star creators, in addition to the disruption to consumers, has a significant and lasting impact on nonstar creators, complementing existing research that exclusively focuses on the effect of star power on consumer demand. Third, we advance the rapidly growing literature on digital content creation incentives by showing that noncontracted creators are more vulnerable to market shocks, whereas commitment to content production due to contractual obligations mitigates the negative effects. Our research bridges these three separate streams of literature and provides empirical evidence to enrich our understanding of the market power of star creators.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. First, we develop our hypotheses based on the extant literature. Then, we conduct an empirical analysis and present our findings with several robustness checks. Lastly, we conclude with our findings, provide managerial implications, summarize the limitations of our article, and discuss the potential for future research.

Theoretical Background

In this section, we develop a theoretical framework to analyze how a star creator’s exit from a content market affects content consumption and provision at both the market level and the individual creator level. We formulate hypotheses by integrating insights from the literature on (1) market entry and exit of prominent firms, (2) the stardom effect, and (3) empirical studies on live streaming and content creation incentives.

The Effects of Star Creator’s Exit at the Market Level

The influence of star creators in content markets resembles the impact of prominent firms in traditional industries. To understand how the exit of a star creator can impact the content market, we first draw from empirical research on market entry and exit of prominent firms. Many empirical studies on market entry and exit use observed exogenous shocks to examine the impact of prominent firms using a quasi-experimental method. The main purpose is to identify the change in market outcomes before and after the shock rather than to discover the underlying demand or supply function. Taking this approach, prior research shows that the entry of prominent firms, such as Walmart in retail or Airbnb in hospitality, diminishes the market performance of incumbent firms (e.g., Ailawadi et al. 2010; Gielens et al. 2008; Jia 2008; Seamans and Zhu 2014; Singh, Hansen, and

Beyond the literature on market entry and exit of prominent firms, stardom literature similarly emphasizes that star performers substantially enhance audience demand across industries like film, theater, and sports (e.g., Basuroy, Chatterjee, and Ravid 2003; Elberse 2007; Han and Ravid 2020). These studies highlight the significant consumer appeal of stars, attributing their influence to superior talent, reputation, or brand equity. Based on these two literature streams, we argue that the absence of star players can decrease the overall consumption of the entertainment product or service. Such a demand-dampening effect may be exacerbated when a star creator leaves one platform for another, considering star creators’ ability to command millions of followers. The loyalty of followers is typically rooted in the star creator’s appeal rather than the platform itself, making it likely that they will migrate to a competing platform. Furthermore, the loss of a star creator could lower the overall content quality of the platform, further diminishing the market size. Thus, we propose that a star creator’s exit can lead to a decline in overall content consumption in the content market(s) associated with the star creator.

Regarding supply-side effects, prior literature suggests that the entry of a prominent firm can intensify competition, harm incumbents’ sales, and compel them to lower prices (e.g., Ailawadi et al. 2010). Conversely, the exit of a prominent firm may soften competition and enable incumbents to raise prices. For instance, Ozturk, Venkataraman, and Chintagunta (2016) find that the exit of the Chrysler dealership led to a 1% price increase among incumbent automobile dealerships, suggesting that reduced competition allows incumbents to soften pricing strategies. In digital content markets, star creators function similarly to prominent firms within their specialized markets; therefore, their market exit is expected to soften competition among incumbent creators. Unlike traditional industries, such as retail and hospitality, where firms primarily compete on price, content creators compete by deciding how much effort to invest in producing content (Jain and Qian 2021). Consequently, the exit of a star creator may reduce competitive intensity, prompting incumbent creators to lower their content production efforts. Moreover, anticipating a decline in consumer demand, creators may further scale back their content provision. With both market competition softening and anticipated declines in content consumption, we expect an overall reduction in content provision at the market level following a star creator’s exit.

Furthermore, the anticipated reduction in content production efforts may exacerbate declining content consumption, resulting in a further decline in content consumption at the market level after a star creator’s exit. Formally, we state the following hypothesis to summarize the net effects of a star creator’s exit on content consumption and provision:

H1: A star creator’s exit has a negative net effect on the content consumption and content provision of the market(s) associated with the star creator.

The Heterogeneous Effects of a Star Creator’s Exit on Incumbent Creators

The impact of a star creator’s market exit on incumbent creators is unlikely to be uniform. Prior studies have shown heterogeneous effects of a prominent firm’s market entry or exit on incumbents (e.g., Ailawadi et al. 2010; Goli and Chintagunta 2021; Li and Srinivasan 2019; Orhun, Venkataraman, and Chintagunta 2016; Zervas, Proserpio, and Byers 2017). For example, Orhun, Venkataraman, and Chintagunta (2016) indicate that a theater’s market entry affects the quality provision of co-owned theaters more severely than independent competitors. Zervas, Proserpio, and Byers (2017) and Li and Srinivasan (2019) document that the impact of Airbnb’s entry varies by hotel type, with low-end hotels suffering greater sales losses than high-end hotels.

In digital content markets, creators vary significantly in audience size and their contractual relationship with platforms, leading to different responses to changes in market conditions. Previous studies on influencer marketing (e.g., Gu, Zhang, and Kannan 2024; Tian, Dew, and Iyengar 2024) classify creators as mega or micro creators based on audience size. Mega creators attract broad audiences and compete directly with other mega creators, while micro creators appeal to small but niche groups with less overlap. In this study, we define a star creator as the most prominent mega creator in a specialized content market. To distinguish nonstar mega creators, we refer to them as “major creators.” Due to their broad appeal and shared target audiences, major creators resemble star creators more closely than micro creators do. Prior research on prominent firms’ entry and exit suggests that firms with similar characteristics to a departing prominent firm experience greater disruptions than those with dissimilar characteristics. Applied to digital content markets, this suggests that major creators will be more affected by a star creator’s exit than micro creators. Two key mechanisms underlie this expectation. First, the migration of loyal followers to another platform disproportionately affects major creators, as they share a larger portion of their audience with the departing star than micro creators. Second, since major creators are direct competitors to the star creator, they are more likely to adjust their content production efforts in response to reduced competition, potentially decreasing their output more than micro creators. In summary, we posit the following hypothesis:

H2: The negative effects of a star creator’s exit on incumbent creators are more pronounced among major creators than micro creators.

H3: The negative effects of a star creator’s exit on incumbent creators are more pronounced for noncontracted creators than for contracted creators.

Empirical Analysis

Our empirical analysis centers on Twitch, an ideal platform for studying the power of star creators for two reasons. First, it is the world’s largest live streaming platform, with 140 million monthly active users (Dean 2023), and attracts top streamers to create content. Second, Twitch features over 20,000 streaming categories that cater to diverse content markets, each attracting streamers who specialize in producing content within that category. The exit of a star streamer who primarily streams in one or a few categories is, therefore, unlikely to affect streamers and viewers of unrelated categories, allowing us to identify eligible control conditions for quasi-experimental studies.

We proceed by outlining our data collection and research design, followed by conducting two sets of SDiD analyses to test our hypotheses: (1) a category-level analysis to examine how total content consumption and provision are affected by the star creator’s exit, and (2) an individual-level analysis to examine how the exit of a star creator affects different types of incumbent streamers. Lastly, we assess the robustness of our findings by analyzing a second star creator’s exit and extending the observation window.

Data and Descriptives

We obtain data from SullyGnome (sullygnome.com), a thirdparty website that records the broadcasting activities and performance of Twitch streamers. The website archives all Twitch stream sessions using the Twitch application programming interface. From this source, we first collect data at the category level, where categories are defined by Twitch. This includes a wide array of gaming categories named after specific video games (e.g., League of Legends) and several nongaming categories (e.g., “Just Chatting”). For each streaming category, the website tracks the daily average number of concurrent channels and concurrent viewers. In 2019, the most streamed categories were primarily gaming-related. FN had the highest daily

average, with 8,212 concurrent channels, followed by League of Legends (2,792) and Apex Legends (2,503). The three most watched categories, in terms of daily average concurrent viewers, were League of Legends (127,689), FN (120,348), and Just Chatting (86,737).

To study how the exit of a star creator affects incumbent creators, we also collect information on the streaming activities of individual streamers from the same source. In total, SullyGnome recorded 177,763,457 stream sessions from 10,632,084 Twitch streamers across 20,775 categories in 2019, totaling 433 million hours of streaming content, or an average of 40.7 hours per streamer. We collect individual-level data, including each streamer’s unique channel ID, primary streaming language, channel creation date, participation in mature content streaming, and status in the Twitch Partner Program. We also record the start and end times of each stream session, allowing us to calculate the duration of each session in minutes (duration) and track the average concurrent viewers during each live streaming session (viewers). Furthermore, we distinguish duration and viewers within each session by category, recognizing that a streamer may broadcast content across multiple categories in a single session.

Research Design

In our empirical context of the live streaming market, star creators possess two distinctive characteristics: (1) they are market leaders with a broad audience and a significant number of followers, and (2) they are major competitors and role models for many nonstar creators. With these criteria in mind, we employ the following approach to identify star creators on Twitch. First, we search Google using the keywords “best Twitch streamers” and “top Twitch streamers,” reviewing the first ten pages of organic search results from January 1, 2019, to June 30, 2019. Second, we pinpoint the Twitch IDs of the streamers mentioned in these search results. Third, we identify streamers whose Twitch ID appears at least twice in the search results as star streamers. The number of mentions in these sources reflects not only the popularity of these streamers among consumers but also their recognition by media outlets and peers. Using this approach, we identify 26 individual star streamers on Twitch. Figure W1 in the Web Appendix illustrates the number of mentions for these star streamers. The top four streamers with the highest number of mentions are Ninja (20 mentions), Shroud (16 mentions), DrDisrespect, and TimTheTatman (each with 9 mentions). Figures W2 and W3 present the number of followers and concurrent viewers of these star streamers on Twitch as of December 31, 2019, respectively. Both metrics support our identification strategy by demonstrating the wide popularity these star streamers had on the platform. In addition to these individual streamers, some Twitch channels are operated by organizations, including esports associations and video game publishers, or by nonstreamer celebrities with a large following. We classify these as organizational channels and designate them as star creators if they are the most popular channels in their categories. For

Given this evidence, we believe it is safe to conclude that both exit events were unexpected to the Twitch and Mixer communities. This sudden shift supports the unpredictability of these exits, aligning with the assumptions necessary for our quasi-experimental design.

The departure of star creators from Twitch to Mixer raises questions about the causal impacts of a star creator’s exit, which could be confounded by Mixer’s concurrent recruiting and promotional efforts targeting both streamers and consumers. To address this, we collect additional data and conduct two analyses. First, we obtain data from Social Blade (socialblade.com) on the 1,000 most-followed Mixer channels throughout 2019, tracking channel IDs, follower counts, and total views at the daily level. A comparison of all English-speaking Twitch streamers and the top 1,000 Mixer channels reveals that most streamers exclusively broadcast on one platform, and the departures of Ninja and Shroud did not affect Twitch streamers’ platform choices around the time of these two events.3 In Figure W5, we plot the daily total views for the top 1,000 Mixer channels during our study period,

Second, we collect data from Google Trends and plot the weekly search volume for Mixer in 2019 in Figure W6 of the Web Appendix. The search volume remained stable throughout the year and showed no significant change around the time of Ninja’s or Shroud’s exits. Furthermore, a news report revealed that Mixer’s lucrative contracts were a key factor in these moves (Kim 2019). This evidence supports our assumption that the exits of these star creators from Twitch influenced content consumption and provision on the platform solely through their exit decisions.

We focus our primary analysis on Ninja’s exit, using Shroud’s exit as a robustness check. To balance data availability while minimizing the influence of concurrent events, we select two 60-day observation windows surrounding each event. Specifically, for Ninja’s exit, we define June 2 to July 31 as the pretreatment period and August 1 to September 29 as the posttreatment period.

Treatment and Control Categories for Category-Level Analysis

For the category-level analysis, we select FN as our treatment unit, as it is the primary category for Ninja’s streaming activities. In the two months before his exit in August 2019, Ninja devoted approximately 98% of his total streaming time to the FN category. This suggests that his exit is likely to influence content provision and consumption within FN while having minimal effects on unrelated categories. We use channelskt, the average number of concurrent English-speaking channels in category k on day t, to measure the daily total volume of content provision in that category. Similarly, we use viewerskt, the average number of concurrent viewers watching English-speaking channels in category k on day t, to measure the daily content consumption in that category. We focus exclusively on English-speaking streaming sessions because English was the primary language used by Ninja.

For the control group selection, we focus on streaming categories that maintain star creators throughout our study period. These categories exhibited a similar presence of star creators to the treatment unit before Ninja’s exit but diverged after his departure, making them ideal for comparison. Among the top 20 most-watched gaming categories, only FN (our treatment unit) experienced a star creator exit event during the observation window. Treatment and Control Streamers for Individual-Level Analysis

For the individual-level analysis, we consider nonstar streamers who actively streamed FN-related content as our treatment group. In the two months before Ninja’s exit, 17,006

English-speaking streamers had broadcast at least 1,000 minutes of FN-related content. These 17,006 active FN streamers accounted for 99% of FN’s total views on Twitch, while the remaining 1% came from 49,216 streamers who were inactive in FN broadcasting during that period. The live streaming market is a long-tail market where a small number of major streamers command most of the market share. The FN category is no exception: Among the 17,006 active FN streamers, the top 3% accounted for 80% of total views and 90% of the follower base within the category. To investigate the heterogeneous effects of a star creator’s exit on major creators versus micro creators, we construct two samples from the 17,006 active FN streamers. First, we construct a sample of major FN creators by collecting data from all top 3% FN streamers who are ranked by their viewership in the pre-exit period. This sample consists of 508 streamers. Second, we draw a stratified random sample of 516 streamers from the remaining 16,498 micro streamers. This involves ranking the streamers by total hours watched, dividing them into 516 subgroups based on rank, and randomly selecting one streamer from each subgroup. Given the similarities among streamers within each stratum, this stratification ensures the sample is representative of micro streamers while keeping the estimation tractable. In sum, our treatment group sample includes 1,024 FN streamers.

For the control group, we apply the same selection process to nonstar streamers across the five previously identified control categories. For each category, we construct two samples: the first consists of all major streamers, and the second is a stratified random sample of the micro streamers. In total, the control group includes 945 streamers: 404 major streamers and 541 micro streamers.

However, our treatment category, FN, substantially exceeds all control categories in terms of concurrent channels and viewers. As a result, implementing SCM would place a disproportionately large weight on a single control category which is the closest in magnitude but does not necessarily exhibit a pretreatment parallel pattern with FN.

Doudchenko and Imbens (2016) propose the modified synthetic control (MSC) method to relax SCM’s assumptions but do not offer clear guidance on which assumption(s) should be relaxed. To address this, Li and Shankar (2024) introduce the two-step synthetic control (TSC) method, which helps determine when to use SCM versus MSC based on the performance of pretreatment parallel trend tests. Following Li and Shankar (2024), we apply TSC tests to determine the synthetic control specification before conducting causal inference estimation. We construct separate synthetic controls for each of the four outcome variables (i.e., channelskt and viewerskt for categorylevel analysis, and durationit and viewersit for individual-level analysis).6

After determining the synthetic control specification, we implement SDiD, proposed by Arkhangelsky et al. (2021), to estimate causal effects at both the category level and individual level. Both DiD and SCM are nested within SDiD and can be considered special cases of SDiD. In our case, SDiD offers advantages over SCM, as it allows for the inclusion of two-way fixed effects and mitigates the estimation bias caused by control unit heterogeneity and time dynamics (Lambrecht, Tucker, and Zhang 2024). Additionally, SDiD

enables us to apply a consistent causal inference approach across different levels of data aggregation.

Conclusion and Implications

In this article, we examine the role of star creators in digital content markets, focusing on how their exit influences both content provision and consumption. Using a quasi-experimental approach, we analyze the effects of Ninja and Shroud’s exits from Twitch and uncover several key insights:

1. The exit of a star creator shrinks content consumption. Our empirical research indicates that a star creator’s exit results in a substantial decline in content consumption in the market(s) associated with the star creator, as evidenced by fewer concurrent viewers in the affected content categories. This suggests that star creators function as key drivers of audience engagement, attracting and sustaining a significant portion of the consumer base. Our findings resonate with previous research on the demand-enhancing impact of star performers (e.g., Elberse 2007) and the research on the traffic loss due to a big supplier’s departure from the aggregator website (e.g., Chiou and Tucker 2017). Our findings also suggest that the loss of a star creator not only affects their direct followers but also diminishes the overall appeal of the content category, ultimately leading to a contraction in content consumption. 2. Content provision does not expand to compensate for a star creator’s absence; instead, it contracts. We find that overall content provision decreases following a star creator’s exit. This result may seem counterintuitive, as one might expect incumbent creators to increase content production to fill the gap and capture market share. However, two key factors could drive the opposite outcome. First, reduced audience size discourages content creators from investing effort in content

As content creation becomes an increasingly popular profession, digital content markets are experiencing an oversupply of content creators. However, audience attention tends to concentrate on a small group of top creators. To adapt to this highly concentrated content preference, platforms often allocate disproportionately high exposure to a select number of star creators, further widening the gap between star and nonstar creators. Our research emphasizes the central role of star creators in this ecosystem, shedding light on how their market exit reshapes the digital content ecosystem.

Our research also provides valuable insights for digital content platforms navigating strategic investments in star creators, particularly regarding retention and acquisition. Our quasi-experiment demonstrates that the exit of Ninja, Twitch’s most prominent FN streamer, led to a substantial decline in content provision and consumption for FN-related content. Armstrong and Wright (2007) propose that e-commerce platforms sign exclusive contracts with sellers to prevent multihoming and mitigate platform competition on the supply side. Similarly, we have observed that platforms sign exclusive contracts with stringent penalty terms to deter star streamers from leaving. This approach has become more prevalent after Twitch’s loss of Ninja and Shroud (Goslin 2020). We recommend that platforms fully consider a star creator’s impact on the supply side when determining penalty terms in exclusive contracts. Platforms can refer to our study when assessing their investments in star creators.

The findings regarding heterogeneous effects support the idea that a market shock might not impact creators equally. Noncontracted creators, often smaller in scale and reach and less committed to producing content within the platform, face greater vulnerability to such shocks. Their content production and audience engagement often hinge on star creators’ vast followings. In contrast, contracted creators, armed with loyal fan bases and a strong commitment to content creation, are more insulated from these market shocks. Platforms should consider strategies to convert high-potential creators into contracted creators. Providing incentives, mentorship, and financial support for emerging streamers can help maintain content diversity and resilience in the ecosystem.

Our article is not without limitations, which we hope future research will address. First, we focus on the effect of a star creator’s exit on specialized content markets. Future research could extend our research to a platform-wide perspective. Second, our study focuses on the causal estimation of the effect of a star creator’s exit. Future work could try to decompose the underlying demand and supply shifts in response to a star creator’s entry or exit and separately identify the various pathways discussed in this article. Third, we do not have access to individual-level consumer data. The availability of such data would allow us to conduct additional analysis to further strengthen our validation of the SUTVA assumptions. Moreover, with individuallevel consumer data, future studies could explore whether the observed decline in content consumption is primarily driven by the departure of the star creator’s loyal audience. Fourth, our empirical analysis focuses on the live streaming market.

Future research could examine whether our findings generalize to other digital content markets, such as short videos or online novels. Lastly, we use streaming duration as the primary measure of creator effort. Future research could develop a broader set of metrics to better capture different dimensions of content creation effort.
