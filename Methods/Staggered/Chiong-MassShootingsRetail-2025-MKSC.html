1. Introduction Mass shootings in the United States have become more frequent in recent years. Between 2016 and 2019, the country averaged 371 mass shootings per year. This number rose to 650 annually between 2020 and 2023.1

The growing prevalence of mass shootings presents challenges for retail businesses. First, retail locations such as shopping malls are common targets due to their high foot traffic,2 making them the most common setting, followed by restaurants and bars.3 Second, nearly two-thirds of retailers have identified mass vio lence or active assailant events as a growing concern (National Retail Federation 2023). In response to the recent uptick in mass shootings, some public compa nies have started assessing and disclosing the potential financial impact of these events in their annual reports.4 Although the human toll of these events is evident, their economic impact on retail businesses is not known. We present the first systematic investiga tions into the economic cost of mass shootings to retail businesses and how they impact consumers.

We combine U.S. mass shooting incident data with store-level credit and debit card transaction data that are matched to the location of the incident. Because mass shootings are salient and plausibly random events, we implement a difference-in-differences (DID) strategy around the timing and location of mass shootings to esti mate their causal impact on consumer shopping behav ior. Specifically, we compare the performance of stores that encountered a mass shooting (i.e., treatment group) to that of stores that did not (i.e., control group).

We examine the record of all mass shootings in the United States that occurred at or near a retail location between January 2022 and June 2023, totaling 486 inci dents. By tracking the performance of 16,431 individual retail businesses over a 30-month period, our DID esti mates show that, on average, affected stores experi enced a 12.9% reduction in transactions and a 4.8% decrease in order size, leading to an overall 18.7% decrease in revenue. These findings are corroborated by foot traffic data, which show significant reductions in both foot traffic and dwell time at the affected stores

We also estimate how the impact of mass shootings varies with distance from the incident. We show that mass shootings impact not only directly affected stores but also businesses within a 1.25-mile radius. The impact, however, diminishes with distance: stores closest to the incident (within 0.1 miles) face an average revenue decline of 24.6%, whereas those further away (between 1 and 1.25 miles) see a smaller decline of 8.7%. Beyond 1.25 miles, the effect is not statistically significant.

Accounting for store closures and using DID esti mates based on the proximity of stores to incidents, we estimate the nationwide economic cost of mass shoot ings at $27 billion annually in forgone revenue for affected retailers, equivalently erasing 0.5% of the U.S. retail gross domestic product (GDP) each year.

The decrease in foot traffic and dwell times at impacted stores following an incident suggests that heightened consumer fear and safety concerns may lead consumers to avoid or curtail time spent there. Further supporting this, we observe heterogeneous effects by business type, with nonessential stores more affected than essential ones, evidence of channel substi tution from in-person to online shopping, and modera tion by local exposure to gun-related violence, where stores in areas with higher firearm mortality experience smaller impacts.

We conduct a battery of additional analyses to check the validity and robustness of our results. First, we use recent advances in staggered DID (Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021) to account for the variation in the treatment timing of mass shootings. We also find con sistent results across various functional form assump tions and sample selection criteria.

We offer several managerial and policy implications. First, this study is the first to document and quantify the economic cost of mass shootings on retail businesses, providing benchmarks for understanding these risks. Second, our findings show that the impact of mass shoot ings extends beyond directly targeted stores, affecting businesses within a 1.25-mile radius. With the increasing number of mass shootings, our findings offer a novel per spective on the national discourse by highlighting the geographically widespread economic impacts on busi nesses and underscoring the need for attention from pol icymakers to address it. Finally, we demonstrate the use of online channels in bolstering store resilience against adverse shocks such as mass shootings.

2. Related Literature Our study adds to three streams of literature. First, our study contributes to the literature examining the effect

of mass shootings on consumer psychology. Hughes et al. (2011), Soni and Tekin (2023), and Lagerborg et al. (2023) highlight mass shootings’ prolonged negative impact on the community and individual emotional well-being, as well as consumer confidence levels. Studies have also shown that fear of mass shootings reduces leisure activities (Clark and Stancanelli 2017) and discourages consumers from attending public events or going to the mall (American Psychological Association 2019). However, these studies primarily rely on surveys. In contrast, we employ detailed data on customer visits and spending at retail stores to investigate the impact of mass shootings on businesses.

Second, our study builds on literature examining the financial and economic consequences of mass shoot ings. Brodeur and Yousaf (2025) and Lagerborg et al. (2023) report the adverse effect of mass shootings on local economies, including increased unemployment rates and decreased industrial production. Other stud ies, like Gopal and Greenwood (2017), Karan (2019), and Sakariyahu et al. (2023), focus on repercussions for financial markets. Shaik et al. (2023) study the eco nomic impact of fatal school shootings on grocery purchases and home values. Although these studies primarily consider broader macroeconomic indicators at the county or national levels, our study takes a dif ferent approach by examining the performance metrics of individual retail stores using location-based credit and debit card transaction data.

Finally, our study contributes to policy discussions on gun violence and the availability of firearms. Luca et al. (2020) find that mass shootings have an outsized influ ence on policy responses, leading to a 15% increase in firearm bills introduced in a state in the following year. Recent research, such as Balakrishna and Wilbur (2022), Kim and Wilbur (2022), and Moshary et al. (2023), inves tigates the firearm market by analyzing sales and con sumer demand for firearms. Our study adds to this dialogue by quantifying the economic cost of mass shootings.

3. Data We utilize three main data sets: (1) mass shooting data from the Gun Violence Archive (GVA), (2) debit and credit card transactions data from SafeGraph Spend, and (3) foot traffic data from Advan Patterns.

3.2. Location-Based Retail Spending Data: SafeGraph Spend SafeGraph Spend collects anonymized debit and credit card transactions from a panel of around 10.6 million consumers, spanning more than 1.1 million business locations in the United States.6 Each transaction is geo- coded and linked to a brick-and-mortar business loca tion. The data provide a spatially granular view into American commerce, allowing us to see how much customers spend at each place and month.7 Online Appendix C.1 offers additional background informa tion on SafeGraph Spend and highlights its representa tiveness by comparing it to U.S. Census statistics, including the number of customers and income levels.

We focus on three performance mea sures at the store-month level: (1) Total Revenue, repre senting the total dollar amount spent by customers; (2) Number of Transactions, indicating the total number of credit and debit card transactions; and (3) Order Size, measured as the median dollar amount spent per transaction.8

3.3. Estimation Sample Construction This section outlines how the GVA and SafeGraph Spend are merged to identify stores affected by mass shootings (i.e., treated stores), followed by details on finding comparable stores (i.e., control stores) via a matching process.

3.3.1. Sample of Mass Shootings. From the GVA, we obtained all mass shooting incidents in the United States between January 2022 and June 2023 that occurred within 0.25 miles of a retail store in SafeGraph Spend.9

This resulted in a total of 486 incidents, which we term retail mass shootings: incidents occurring within walk ing distance of a retail location. Figure 1 depicts the geo graphical distribution of these incidents, indicating that mass shootings are frequent in population centers. In terms of the site of mass shootings in our sample, retail/commercial was the most frequent incident loca tion (41.7%), followed by residential (25.1%) and mixed- use commercial/residential (23.4%). Our sample also includes other incidents, such as school shootings, drive- by shootings, shootouts, robberies, and domestic vio lence. Table C.3 in the Online Appendix provides a more detailed characterization of these incidents.

Our sample covers several high-profile retail mass shootings, such as the shooting at a Tops Friendly Supermarket in Buffalo, NY, that killed 10 and injured three; the shooting at the Allen Premium Outlets that killed nine and injured seven; and the shooting at Greenwood Park Mall in suburban Indianapolis that killed four and injured two.

3.3.2. Treated Stores and Descriptive Evidence. Based on our sample of mass shootings, 486 incidents occur ring within walking distance of 0.25 miles from a retail store, we then identified 3,323 affected (treated) stores within 0.25 miles of these incidents. We identify these stores by calculating the distances between the stores and the locations of mass shooting incidents, using the coordinates provided in the two data sets.

Our SafeGraph Spend data cover the period from July 2021 to December 2023. By examining mass shoot ing incidents between January 2022 and June 2023, we ensure that each treated store has at least six months of pre- and posttreatment observations.

Table 1 provides the summary statistics of the trea ted stores’ retail performances. By comparing the aver age values before and after mass shooting incidents, we observe that the total revenue decreases by 13% after the incident. Similarly, the average number of transactions and the order size decreases by 9.6% and 6.8%, respectively. Figure A.1 in the Online Appendix, which plots the trend of average weekly revenues before and after a mass shooting incident, also exhibits a noticeable decrease after an incident.

3.3.3. Comparable Control Stores via Matching. To facilitate a DID estimation of the causal impact of mass shootings, we identify unaffected comparable control stores using the following steps.

First, we define the pool of eligible control units to be stores located between 10 and 94.7 miles from the inci dent location. The minimum distance of 10 miles is to ensure that the control stores are not too close to risk spillover effects from the treated stores,10 whereas the

maximum distance of 94.7 miles is to ensure similarity in the underlying macroeconomic conditions for each control and treated pair. Second, for each treated store, we employ exact matching to find the set of control stores of the same business type (based on NAICS). For instance, a treated pharmacy is matched with other pharmacies as its control group. Third, for each treated store, we apply Mahalanobis distance matching to find five control stores that are most similar in terms of per formance measures preshooting.

The final sample is a balanced panel data of 16,431 retail stores (3,323 treated and 13,108 control stores) between July 2021 and December 2023, totaling 492,930 store-month observations. Online Appendix B provides further details and justification of the matching proce dure and demonstrates a covariate balance between treated and control stores. Online Appendix D.2 dis cusses the robustness of our results with respect to alter native matching methods.

3.4. Foot Traffic Data We augment our stores’ transaction data with foot traf fic data from Advan Patterns.11 These data provide monthly visit counts to places and points of interest based on a panel of mobile devices, including visit fre quency and duration. In particular, it shares the same store identifier with SafeGraph Spend, allowing us to examine the same set of treated stores over the same time period (balanced panel). We focus on the following store-month outcomes: (i) Visits, representing the total number of visits to the location, and (ii) Dwell Time, indicating the median duration of visits (in minutes).

4. Empirical Analyses We separately analyze the effect of mass shootings on two types of stores: (1) operating stores and (2) closed stores. We consider a store as closed if we observe three or more consecutive months of closure after the inci dent and operating otherwise. Of the 3,323 treated stores, 554 (16.7%) are closed, with the remainder being operating stores.

4.1. Main Effect Our main empirical specification is a two-way fixed- effect (TWFE) DID regression:

Yit  β · PostTreatit + δi + τt + ɛit: (4.1)

The dependent variable Yit represents the outcome measure of store i in month t, specifically the logarith mic functions of total revenue, the number of transac tions, order size, foot traffic, and median dwell time. The variable PostTreatit equals one if store i is affected by a mass shooting incident and if t is after the incident; otherwise, it is zero. The coefficient β is our parameter of interest. We include fixed effects for stores, δi, and time periods, τt, to account for store-specific and macro economic factors that may affect the outcome. Standard errors are clustered at the incident level to account for correlations among the error terms of different stores affected by the same mass shooting incident.

The standard TWFE DID framework assumes that treatment occurs at the same time for all treated units. However, mass shootings occur at different times, resulting in staggered treatment timing. As an alterna tive model, we implement the staggered DID estimator of Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) (CS).

Both the TWFE and the CS estimates show that mass shootings have a significant negative effect on revenue, number of transactions, and order size (Panel A in Table 2). In particular, affected stores on average expe rience a 19% reduction in revenue, 13% lower transac tions, and 5% smaller order size, compared with the

control stores. The magnitudes of coefficients from both TWFE and CS are comparable.

In Panel B of Table 2, we examine the effect of mass shootings on consumer visits and their median dwell time. We find that mass shootings significantly decrease foot traffic to affected stores by 23% (column 7 of Table 2). Additionally, the median dwell time of visitors at these stores decreases by 18%. The reduced dwell time explains the negative impact of mass shootings on both intensive and extensive margins: with less time spent in stores, indi viduals purchase fewer items per visit, leading to the smaller order sizes observed in Table 2.12

We now examine how the impact of mass shootings varies over time, relative to the month of the incident. We conduct an event study using a dynamic TWFE regression and plot the parameter estimates in Figure 2. The preincident coefficients indicate no violation of the parallel trends assumption. Meanwhile, the negative postincident coefficients demonstrate the long-lasting adverse effect of mass shootings on store revenues. In Figure A.2 in the Online Appendix, we further extend the event study to different store types, considering fac tors such as store closures and proximity to the incident location and assessing the recovery dynamics of differ ent types of stores.

Lastly, we conduct extensive robustness checks to val idate our estimates. 4.2. Store Closure Following our analysis of the impact on operating stores, we now turn our attention to closed stores. We examine the factors influencing the likelihood and duration of store closure among the treated stores using two dependent variables: (1) Closedi, indicating whether store i is observed to be closed for three or more consecutive months following a mass shooting, and (2) ClosedMonthsi, representing the total months of closure (which may be nonconsecutive).

We find that branded stores and stores with a larger revenue are less likely to experience closure (Table A.1 in the Online Appendix). For example, being part of a national franchise or major brand13 reduces the proba bility of closure by 0.102 and shortens closure duration by 1.3 months. This resilience may be due to higher brand equity and better resources to handle shocks like mass shootings. Branded stores also perform better in terms of revenue, number of transactions, and order size postincident (Table A.5 in the Online Appendix). These findings are consistent with franchising litera ture, which reports higher survival rates for franchised stores compared with independent ones (Lafontaine et al. 2019).

On the other hand, Nested stores and those with higher order sizes are associated with a greater likelihood of

store closure. Nested stores, defined as those located within or forming part of larger structures such as shop ping complexes, are often enclosed spaces with limited escape routes, which may be perceived as less safe during mass shootings. Supporting this, foot traffic to Nested stores declined more sharply after mass shootings (Table A.6 in the Online Appendix). Similarly, stores with higher order sizes, often nonessential retailers such as jewelry, luggage, shoe, and electronics stores in our sample, are also more prone to closure.14

4.3. Effect over Distances Our main sample consists of stores located within 0.25 miles of an incident, corresponding to the immediate vicinity of mass shootings, where we observe an overall negative impact. Splitting the treated stores into three equal-sized distance groups further reveals diminishing effects with distance: Stores within 0.11 miles experi ence the largest impact at –25%, followed by a –16.4% impact for stores 0.11–0.19 miles away, and a –15% impact for those 0.19–0.25 miles away (see column 1 of Table A.2 in the Online Appendix).

To further assess the spatial extent of the impact, we expand our sample to include stores within 2 miles of an incident, matching control stores in the same way as in our main sample. Figure 3 plots the estimates of the group-specific treatment effect. We find that the spatial impact of mass shootings diminishes over distance, becoming statistically insignificant beyond a 1.25-mile radius. This pattern also justifies our choice of the con trol group, consisting of stores located at least 10 miles away from any incident, which avoids spillovers from treated to control units. We report the full results in Table A.2 in the Online Appendix.

For closed stores (Equation (4.3)), losses are calculated based on their preperiod revenues.15 We then scale this in-sample loss with SafeGraph’s sam pling rate and store coverage ratio (see Online Appen dix A.3 for details).

The estimated economic loss is $27 billion annually in foregone revenue to the affected retailers. To put this number in context, the retail industry’s total GDP con tribution was $5.3 trillion, accounting for 20.4% of the U.S. GDP in 2022.16 Hence, the loss from mass shoot ings amounts to approximately 0.5% of the U.S. retail GDP annually.

This estimate represents the loss from mass shoot ings occurring within a single year, based on the

incident rate observed during our sample period from January 2022 to June 2023. If incident rates were to increase (or decrease), the corresponding annual loss would rise (or fall) accordingly. Furthermore, this figure reflects only counterfactual revenue losses and does not take into account direct costs such as facility repairs and medical expenses associated with shooting incidents.

5. Consumer Fear and Safety Concerns: Evidence Because our effect is long-lasting, temporary disrup tions such as police investigations, media presence, or scene clean-up and repair are unlikely to explain our findings. A more plausible mechanism is heightened fear and safety concerns, which prompt consumers to avoid or curtail time spent in affected stores (American Psychological Association 2019). This section presents evidence showing that fear and safety concerns drive consumers to avoid visiting or spending time at physi cal retail locations impacted by mass shootings.

5.1. Heterogeneous Effects by Business Types In Section 4, we saw that mass shootings negatively impact foot traffic and dwell time at affected locations. Additionally, if fear and safety concerns were to deter prolonged stays at affected locations after mass shootings, businesses that rely on longer customer dwell time may experience relatively greater impacts. To investigate this, we examine two retail categories with long and short dwell times: sit-down versus fast-food establishments. Sit-down establishments, including bars, full-service res taurants, and coffee shops, encourage consumers to stay longer. Conversely, fast-food establishments, such as fast- food chains and fast-casual restaurants, are designed for shorter visit durations.17 In column 1 of Table 3, we show that sit-down venues are more adversely affected than fast-food establishments.

Consumer responses to mass shootings may also vary based on the nature of the products sold in a store—specifically, whether the store offers essential products meeting basic needs or nonessential products with discretionary demand that can be postponed or avoided. We examine this by looking at retail stores and categorizing them into either essential or nones sential. For instance, essential stores are groceries, pharmacies, gasoline stations, and convenience stores, whereas nonessential stores sell clothing, shoes, elec tronics, jewelry, and sporting goods. This analysis excludes food-service establishments, such as restau rants and bars, which are analyzed separately in the previous section.18

5.2. Online Channel Substitution Following mass shooting incidents, consumers may perceive affected locations as unsafe. As a result, con sumers may shift from in-person shopping to online ordering to minimize the perceived risks associated with physical visits.

To examine this shift, we focus on restaurants, defined as Full-Service Restaurants (722,511) and Limited- Service Restaurants (722,513) in the NAICS, for two rea sons. First, restaurants are frequent targets in mass shootings, making them the most common retail loca tion type in our sample, comprising 43% of all treated units.19 Second, unlike other businesses, our data pro vide comprehensive insights into online channel orders for restaurants.20

We find that restaurants affected by mass shootings experience a significant increase in online revenue (+9:3%), as we show in column 3 of Table 3. However, because mass shootings negatively impact in-person revenue to a larger extent (column 4), the overall total revenue still decreases (column 5). Table A.7 in the Online Appendix further shows positive effects on online order size and the number of transactions, indicating substitution from offline to online channels along both the intensive and extensive margins.

Overall, our findings that consumers are shifting from offline to online channels support the notion that consumers seek to curtail the time spent at the affected locations. From a managerial perspective, the presence of an online channel can help businesses mitigate losses, thus making them more resilient to negative shocks.

5.3. Desensitization and Normalization to Gun Violence Consumer fear and safety concerns from these incidents may be influenced by local conditions and experiences. Previous studies (Li et al. 2017, Goldstein and Prater 2023) reported evidence of desensitization and normali zation to recurrent acts of violence, suggesting that con sumers in areas with higher baseline firearm death rates may exhibit smaller changes in shopping behavior following mass shootings. We find the impact of mass shootings is significantly moderated in counties with higher firearm death rates, suggesting some desensitiza tion to gun-related violence in these areas (see Table A3 in Online Appendix A.4).

5.4. Supply-Side Factors In addition to consumer fear, supply-side factors may also contribute to the decline in store performance following mass shootings. Staffing issues after such events may lead to store closures or reduced operating hours. Moreover, heightened fear and lowered morale among employees may result in declining service qual ity toward customers, further impacting consumer demand and revenue. Although these supply-side fac tors could alter the interpretation of our findings, they should not change our estimate of the economic cost.

Finally, stores might increase promotions to attract consumers or implement enhanced security measures to alleviate consumer fears after mass shootings. As these efforts are likely designed to boost revenue, fail ing to account for these factors could lead to an under estimation of the true impact. In other words, our estimate would likely be a conservative measure.

We provide empirical evidence that mass shootings lead to significant and measurable reductions in retail performance and foot traffic. Mass shootings nega tively affect not only the directly impacted focal stores but also stores within a 1.25-mile radius, with the effect diminishing as the distance from the incident increases. The economic cost of mass shootings is estimated to be $27 billion annually in forgone revenue to retail busi nesses or 0.5% of the U.S. annual retail GDP.

We conclude by discussing several implications. First, although the issue of gun violence and mass shootings has been widely studied, we bring a novel perspective by examining how consumers and individual busi nesses are affected by mass shootings in retail settings. Our findings suggest that the impacts on retail busi nesses should be included as part of the policy discus sion surrounding gun violence and mass shootings.

Second, we provide insights into the mechanism driving the decline in consumer spending following mass shootings. By analyzing foot traffic data, we find decreases in both customer visits and dwell time at affected retail locations, implying the role of con sumer fear and safety concerns. We also find that establishments selling nonessential goods and those requiring longer customer dwell time experience more pronounced adverse effects. Furthermore, possi bly as a result of desensitization, businesses in areas perceived as less prone to gun violence experience greater disruptions from mass shooting incidents.

Lastly, online channels can serve as a tool for busi nesses in the aftermath of social disruptions, such as mass shootings. We find a substitution from offline to online channels after incidents among restaurants, which we attribute to consumers avoiding the affected physical locations. From a managerial perspective, hav ing an online channel may allow businesses to recover some of their losses, making them more resilient to negative shocks.
