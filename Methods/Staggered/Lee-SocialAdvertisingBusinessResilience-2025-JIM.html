Social Media Advertising’s Role in Small Business Resilience Amid Lockdowns

Shun-Yang Lee , Julian Runge , Daniel Yoo , Yakov Bart , Anett Gyurak , and J.W. Schneider

Abstract Research has investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on business performance and survival, indicating particularly adverse effects for small and midsize businesses (SMBs). Yet only limited work has examined whether and how advertising may have helped shape these outcomes, particularly for SMBs. By constructing and analyzing a novel dataset of more than 79,000 businesses across 49 countries, the authors explore this question and examine the impact of pandemic-induced government stay-at-home orders on business survival. Using staggered difference-in-differences estimators, this research produces directional evidence that government lockdowns increased the likelihood of SMB closure and that social media advertising attenuated this adverse effect. These results are robust across different model specifications and measures of advertising activities. The findings additionally show heterogeneity across business sizes and industries, enriching the understanding of business survival during severe demand crises from a global perspective. The current research suggests that advertising may play an important role in building SMB resilience in light of severe demand shocks and mobility restrictions.

Keywords small business, social media advertising, COVID-19, lockdown, stay-at-home, business closure

Submitted November 18, 2024

The COVID-19 pandemic posed a serious threat to the operation of businesses around the world. As the pandemic unfolded, businesses were faced with the dire consequences of falling demand, declining revenues, and liquidity constraints. Indeed, as the pandemic intensified, businesses closed at an alarming rate (Apedo-Amah et al. 2020).1 While the pandemic has increased researchers’ interest in understanding the extent and moderators of these closures (Bartik et al. 2020a; Bartlett and Morse 2020; Gourinchas et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2020), few insights exist into how advertising may have shaped the performance and operation of businesses around the world.

One reason for the lack of insight on the interaction of COVID-19 demand shocks, business operation, and advertising is the absence of a guiding theoretical framework for understanding whether/how advertising could help businesses operate in times of unexpected demand crises. While prior studies have examined how business outcomes, such as revenues (Bloom, Fletcher, and

Yeh 2021; Chetty et al. 2024), layoffs (Bartik et al. 2020a; Khamis et al. 2021), and business closures (Apedo-Amah et al. 2020; Crane et al. 2022), were affected during the pandemic, few studies have examined the extent to which advertising shaped these outcomes. Furthermore, advertising technology such as digital marketing channels can be particularly important for small and midsize businesses (SMBs) during such crises, as SMBs often mainly rely on digital channels for advertising; however, the literature is lacking in understanding whether/how such advertising technologies can affect SMB operations during demand crises.

Another reason for the lack of research is the paucity of data that observe both the advertising and operation of businesses

Although there has been a critical need to understand how businesses were impacted by the pandemic, the sudden and rapid onset of COVID-19 has made it difficult to obtain timely and detailed data, especially outside a small group of regions and countries. In addition, recent efforts have focused on collecting data on a basic set of financial and employment indicators (Apedo-Amah et al. 2020; Bartik et al. 2020b; Chetty et al. 2024). Thus, the availability of advertising data has been limited by comparison, and, to the authors’ best knowledge, no studies have closely examined the role of advertising in business survival during the pandemic.

Therefore, in this research, we examine whether advertising impacted SMBs’ operations during the COVID-19 pandemic. Consisting of more than 79,000 businesses across 49 countries, our dataset combines firm-level time series of advertising activities on the Facebook platform with information on business operation and data on government lockdowns. We first investigate how governments’ stay-at-home orders affected the survival of businesses. We then explore whether Facebook advertising moderated the impact of stay-at-home orders on business operation and potential heterogeneity in the moderating effect. Our approach is exploratory in nature and focuses on providing directional evidence of advertising’s potential positive effects on SMBs during severe demand downturns.

Our empirical analyses utilize staggered difference-indifferences (DID) estimators. We find that governmentlockdowns during the pandemic led to an increase in business closures, but that social media advertising attenuated the impact of these lockdowns. These results are robust across different model specifications and measures of advertising activities. Our findings provide several important implications. First, we confirm that stay-at-home orders have a negative effect on business operation. Second, we find that advertising played an important role in supporting SMBs in navigating the hardships induced by the pandemic and related lockdowns. Third, we uncover effect heterogeneity across business sizes and industries using a global sample. While prior studies on the impact of stay-at-home orders have largely focused on a single country or region (Alexander and Karger 2023; Aum, Lee, and Shin 2021; Bartlett andMorse2020;GoolsbeeandSyverson2021),ourfindingshighlight the important role that advertising can play in building small business resilience using a global sample.

Literature Review

The COVID-19 pandemic has led to a growing body of research examining how businesses have been affected around the world. Notably, the role of government lockdowns and their impact on the performance and survival of businesses have been a major topic of interest. Alexander and Karger (2023) and Gourinchas et al. (2025) find that stay-at-home orders caused considerable reductions in consumer spending and led to increased failures among small businesses during the pandemic, respectively. Apedo-Amah et al. (2020) also find that business closures around the world were at their highest in the weeks following the most stringent periods of lockdown.

While research on business outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic has grown rapidly since its onset, several important questions remain unanswered. First, how did advertising influence businesses during the pandemic? Second, to what extent are the results heterogeneous across different types of business? We expect advertising to have played a critical role in the performance and survival of businesses during the pandemic for both theoretical and empirical reasons. In particular, marketing theory posits that advertising activities have a long-term effect (Breuer and Brettel 2012; Reimer, Rutz, and Pauwels 2014) and that they can have a positive effect on future cash flows by supporting the development of market-based intangible assets (McAlister et al. 2016; Srivastava, Shervani, and Fahey 1998). Studies have also shown that advertising has an important influence on business survival, with past advertising both reducing the risk of bankruptcy (Jindal and McAlister 2015) and increasing the likelihood of surviving the bankruptcy process (Jindal 2020). In addition, studies have shown that personalized online advertising can enhance market-based intangible assets, including brand equity, by enhancing brand identification (Gordon, Moakler, and Zettelmeyer 2023; Tran et al. 2020). Personalized online advertising also has a positive impact on brand-specific page views (Rutz and Bucklin 2012), active and passive searches on a business’s web page (Ghose and Todri-Adamopoulos 2016), and both revenues and repeat purchase probabilities for businesses (Lewis and Reiley 2014; Manchanda et al. 2006). Moreover, Alekseev et al. (2023) provide initial evidence that Facebook advertising may have mitigated the adverse effect of the pandemic on businesses.2 Given its ability to target and provide personalized content, we expect social media advertising to be effective in helping businesses accumulate market-based assets that support their financial performance during lockdowns, thereby increasing their likelihood of survival.

Therefore, this article aims to address these questions and offer new insights into the role of advertising for businesses during the pandemic, using a global sample.

Data and Measures Measuring Business Survival

We obtained data on business survival during the COVID-19 pandemic from the Future of Business Survey (FOBS). The FOBS is a joint data collection project conducted by the World Bank, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, and Facebook that collects survey data on the challenges, opportunities, and needs of businesses around the world using the Facebook platform. We use the FOBS data to construct an outcome variable of business operation by calendar month. Specifically, we construct panel data consisting of businesses and their monthly (binary) closure decision from December 2019 to May 2020. Measuring Government Responses to the COVID-19 Pandemic

We obtain data on government responses to the COVID-19 pandemic from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker, a data collection project that gathered information on the policies and interventions that governments around the world adopted in response to the pandemic. Our primary government response variable is the implementation of stay-at-home orders, which we treat as a binary measure. This variable captures the effects of lockdowns and movement restrictions. Operationally, we consolidate daily records of stay-at-home orders into monthly observations. We classify a month as having a stay-at-home order

in effect if the order was implemented for at least one day during the focal month.

Method

We use the collected data to examine how SMBs responded to governments’ stay-at-home orders during the COVID-19 pandemic and whether this relationship is moderated by their Facebook advertising activities. Since business closure is a binary outcome (i.e., open or close), we use a logit model for our analyses. Moreover, since some countries never issued any stay-at-home orders, we could use a DID approach to estimate the effect of the stay-at-home order on business closure, where weconsidergovernments’stay-at-homeordersasshocksandevaluate business closure as the outcome. However, since different countries issued the stay-at-home orders at different times, this means that the orders were enforced in a staggered fashion across countries. We therefore adopt a staggered DID model for our analysis. Specifically, we adopt an extended two-way fixed effect (ETWFE) approach (Berman and Israeli 2022; Wooldridge 2021, 2023) to model the staggered policy implementation. Intuitively, the ETWFE approach extends the two-way fixed effect model by adding additional interaction terms, including those between treatment and periods as well as between treatment and cohorts, where each cohort includes countries that adopted the treatment in the same period. The advantage of ETWFE is that it addresses the staggered adoption concern and can be applied to nonlinear models, and therefore we use ETWFE as our main modeling approach. As a robustness check, we also implement and present results from using the Callaway and Sant’Anna DID estimator (Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021). Additionally, we use our models to examine whether advertising activities on Facebook moderate the impact of governments’ stay-at-home orders. Operationally, we estimate the staggered DID models separately for advertisers and nonadvertisers, enabling us to compare their respective treatment effect estimates.

Model-Free Evidence

We begin by providing model-free evidence of the effect of stay-at-home orders on business closure. We visualize the proportion of businesses that closed separately for advertisers and nonadvertisers in Figure 1. We consider two ways of measuring time: calendar month and number of months since the implementation of stay-at-home orders (i.e., a relative time scale). We find that with both time measurements, advertisers exhibited lower closing proportions than nonadvertisers. Specifically, the relative time plot shows that the proportion of businesses that closed increased drastically as soon as stay-at-home orders took effect (i.e., when Months Since Treatment = 0), suggesting that stay-at-home orders are positively correlated with business closure. We also observe that advertisers exhibited a lower closing rate compared with nonadvertisers across different industries, as shown in Figure 2, and for small businesses (i.e., fewer than 500 employees), as shown in Figure 3. Overall, these

findings suggest that advertising might have moderated the effect of stay-at-home orders. We next present results from staggered DID analyses.

Advertising’s Moderating Effects

To investigate advertising’s moderating effects, we examine the ATT separately for advertisers and nonadvertisers. We measure a business’s Facebook advertiser status using two different measures because advertising is known to have a lagged effect (Beaver, McNichols, and Rhie 2005; Jindal and McAlister 2015; Shumway 2001). The first measure represents a longer time frame, that is, using a business’s advertising activities in 2019, whereas the second measure represents a shorter time frame, that is, using the advertising activities two months prior to a given period. The results using both measures are reported in Table 4. We also visualize the ATT estimates for the 2019 data in Figure 4 and those for the two-month-lead data in Figure 5. We find that, while the ATT estimates are positive for both advertisers and nonadvertisers across all periods, the advertisers’ estimates are consistently lower than those of nonadvertisers. This provides

evidence that advertising might have attenuated the negative effect of stay-at-home orders.3,4

Results by Business Size and Industry

We explore the heterogeneous effect of stay-at-home orders and advertising’s moderating effect in this section. Figure 9 displays the ATT estimates broken down by business size (indicated by the number of employees). We find that advertising appears particularly effective for small businesses compared with large businesses. Note that these results are directional as the estimates are imprecise.

We also break down the ATT estimates by industry sectors, as shown in Figure 10. We again see variations in both the ATT estimates and advertising’s moderating effects across industries. For example, retail and construction industries

appear to have benefited more from Facebook advertising, while advertising barely made any difference in the transportation industry and even had a negative effect on the agriculture industry. Nevertheless, directionally, Facebook advertisers in all industries (except for agriculture, which accounts for around 2% of the data) have lower ATT estimates compared with their nonadvertiser counterparts.

In summary, our analyses show that, while stay-at-home orders increased business closure overall, the adverse effects were less severe for social media advertisers than for nonadvertisers. The results are robust across different model specifications and measures of advertising activities. We observed similar trends across businesses of different sizes and industries.

Our study has several limitations, mostly originating from the correlational nature of our findings. First, in addition to stay-at-home orders, governments around the world also implemented different economic and health policies during the pandemic, which we did not take into account in the current research. Future research should examine the effect of different policies on business operations. Second, we used Facebook advertising as the sole source of online advertising data. While most SMBs are likely to rely on prominent digital advertising platforms such as Facebook as their main advertising channels, we cannot rule out the possibility that there might have been other unobserved advertising activities. Third, we did not observe and thus could not control for key business characteristics such as financial health and age. Furthermore, businesses’ advertising decisions are inherently endogenous and

depend on their own performance as well as that of their competitors, leading our analyses to be correlational in nature. We encourage future research to carefully address the endogeneity concern.

Nevertheless, we believe our research raises important questions that have potential to enrich the scientific discourse (Golder et al. 2023). The first question is whether our findings apply to other modes of online advertising. The predictions provided by our analysis are not limited to the use of Facebook advertising, and we expect that different modes of online advertising, such as banner ads and search advertising, might also have supported the performance and survival of businesses during the pandemic. The second question is whether online advertising technology was especially relevant during the pandemic in light of the increase in internet usage and e-commerce expansion. More fully understanding how different modes of advertising interacted with the increased internet usage during the pandemic is important. While our study focuses on Facebook advertising, gaining insights into SMBs’ overall advertising spending and allocation across different media and channels would shed further light on the role of online
