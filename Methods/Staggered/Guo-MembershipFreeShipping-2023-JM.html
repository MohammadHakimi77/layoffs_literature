The Effectiveness of Membership-Based Free Shipping: An Empirical Investigation of Consumers’ Purchase Behaviors and Revenue Contribution

Fangfei Guo and Yan Liu

Abstract Membership-based free shipping (MFS) has been increasingly adopted by online retailers. However, its effectiveness is understudied. This study leverages a consumer transaction data set provided by an online retailer and uses a stacked difference-in-differences approach to quantify the enrollment effect of MFS on consumers’ purchase behaviors and their net revenue contribution to the retailer. Interestingly, MFS enrollment does not lift average consumers’ spending at the beginning of the enrollment, as they break large orders into smaller ones. Retailers do not gain incremental net revenue due to the increased shipping costs during this period. However, the free shipping benefit may build a switching barrier that motivates consumers to purchase more often with larger order sizes over time. It eventually leads to increased spending and revenue contribution. MFS also increases members’ purchase variety and impulse purchases. In addition, the authors find that there is a greater increase in net revenue contribution after enrollment for light buyers, variety seekers, and those who are willing to pay shipping fees before enrollment. Surprisingly, MFS has a negative impact on the revenue contribution of heavy buyers, the conventional best-value consumers. Moreover, MFS can effectively strengthen consumer–retailer relationships by reducing customer churn.

With the proliferation of e-commerce, one of the challenges retailers face is recovering the constantly increased shipping costs. As Amazon’s annual report shows, its shipping costs reached $83.5 billion in 2022, an increase of about 200% over the previous five years (Amazon 2022). With the hope of satisfying consumers’ expectations of free shipping while recovering high shipping costs, online retailers have shown a growing interest in membership-based free shipping (MFS), in which consumers pay an upfront membership fee in exchange for unlimited free shipping. Nearly 40% of the world’s top retailers have adopted MFS or advertised it as a premium service in their membership programs (Freedman 2022).

Whereas some retailers, such as Walmart, ASOS, and Sephora, introduced MFS with few added perks other than free shipping, other retailers have integrated MFS into their subscription programs (Table 1). For example, Best Buy provides members with tech support, and Bed Bath & Beyond offers member price discounts, along with MFS. Recent literature

has examined the effects of such an integrated subscription program on incremental sales and profits (Iyengar, Park, and Yu 2022). However, Iyengar, Park, and Yu (2022) suggest that the free shipping benefit is not likely the driver for sales change because other perks (e.g., member price discounts) are more important for members of these subscription programs. On the contrary, industry experts claim that MFS is quite successful and that unlimited free shipping is the main reason that drives consumers to join a subscription program such as Amazon Prime (Freedman 2022). In the current study, we intend to fill this research gap and investigate a fee-based membership program that offers only one benefit to its members, unlimited free shipping. We first ask to what extent MFS enrollment could increase consumers’ spending, one of the most crucial success indicators of any membership program (Lal and Bell 2003). On the one hand, when consumers pay membership fees, they are inspired to gain a greater return on their initial investment by purchasing more (Ailawadi, Ma, and Grewal 2018). Thus, MFS enrollment may motivate consumers to consolidate their purchases with the retailer, leading to more sales revenue. On the other hand, consumers may take advantage of unlimited free shipping by placing frequent and small orders without spending more. Therefore, an important question remains unanswered: how MFS enrollment changes consumers’ purchase behaviors related to how much to buy, including total spending, purchase frequency, and average order size.

When shopping online, consumers can easily access millions of products, and shipping policies could change consumers’ purchase intentions in different product categories (Shehu, Papies, and Neslin 2020). One interesting fact about top retailers that have adopted MFS is that they also offer contingent free shipping (CFS) to consumers who choose not to enroll in MFS (Freedman 2022). In CFS, consumers get free shipping with a minimum spending requirement, which may limit their purchases within certain product categories that can easily meet the requirement (Chen and Ngwe 2017). When the free shipping requirement is removed, MFS members may explore more product categories with different price levels. In addition, free shipping might stimulate them to make impulsive purchases to add unplanned products to their baskets (Dawson and Kim 2009). Thus, we investigate how MFS changes consumers’ purchase behaviors related to what to buy, including purchase variety and purchase of impulse products (referred to as impulse purchases).

The free shipping benefits of MFS bring enormous shipping costs to retailers, especially when consumers have their orders shipped in a myriad of small packages (Sun, Cavusoglu, and Raghunathan 2022). Retailers hope membership fees can recoup shipping costs. However, the value implications of membership programs are inconclusive in the literature (Fang et al. 2021; Sun, Cavusoglu, and Raghunathan 2022) and in real-world business. For example, Costco’s annual membership fees account for 80% of Costco’s gross margin (Lewis 2016), while an estimate for Amazon Prime shows that it lost at least $11 per member after accounting for the membership fee (Tuttle 2013). We thus further investigate the net customer revenue (NCR), defined as the revenue generated from consumer transactions and membership fees minus the shipping cost paid by the retailer, to shed light on the financial outcome of MFS.

Taking these factors together, we examine the effect of MFS enrollment on consumers’ purchase behaviors related to how much to buy (i.e., total spending, purchase frequency, and average order size), what to buy (i.e., purchase variety and

impulse purchases), and NCR with an order-level transaction data set. The data set is provided by a top online retailer in Asia, which launched MFS during the sample period. Consumers pay an annual membership fee to enroll in MFS and get the sole membership benefit, unlimited free shipping on all orders. The data set spans 22 months before and 14 months after the launch of MFS. To estimate the MFS enrollment effect and its trend over time, we compare the purchase behaviors and revenue contributions of members with those of nonmembers before and after their enrollment date by using a quasi-experimental design with a difference-in-differences (DiD) approach and propensity score matching (PSM). We employ a time-varying instrumental variable with a control function approach to control for the endogeneity of enrollment timing. Given that members’ enrollment occurs at different times, we adopt a stacked DiD approach that circumvents the problem of staggered treatment timing (Baker, Larcker, and Wang 2022; Cengiz et al. 2019). Moreover, we take a further step to identify varying responsiveness among customer segments. In additional analyses, we examine whether MFS enhances customer retention to shed light on the effectiveness of MFS in the long run.

Our research primarily contributes to the literature on shipping policies and offers important managerial implications for practitioners in the following ways. First, using extensive consumer transaction data, we are among the first to examine the effect of an emerging free shipping policy—MFS—on consumers’ purchase behaviors. We find that MFS is surprisingly ineffective in increasing overall spending for average consumers during the membership period, as members take advantage of free shipping benefits to break large orders into smaller ones. Moreover, MFS changes consumers’ purchase variety and the component of shopping baskets; that is, members purchase from broader product categories and enjoy impulsive purchases.

Second, we expand the scope of the membership program literature from free-to-join programs to paid programs. We provide empirical evidence on the importance of membership fees. A membership fee serves as more than just a steady revenue source for retailers. Membership fees might be perceived as sunk costs and switching costs in consumers’ mental accounts, effectively preventing them from switching to alternatives and enhancing customer retention. Our findings on the dynamic effect of MFS (i.e., that members’ spending and revenue contribution gradually increase over time) might be due to the positive effect of switching costs. Moreover, we find that light buyers, who are the most willing to pay shipping fees or purchase from the broadest product categories before enrollment, are the most responsive customer segment, having the highest percentage change of revenue contribution after enrollment. In contrast, heavy buyers, normally regarded as the best-value segment, have no significant increase in their revenue contribution after enrollment as they are most likely to exploit free shipping. The MFS program even loses money from heavy buyers who are unwilling to pay the shipping fee or purchase from limited product categories before enrollment. These findings provide guidance for retailers to conduct segment-based targeting.

Institutional and Conceptual Background Institutional Background

Shipping fee is an essential component of the marketing mix (Lewis, Singh, and Fay 2006). It is also one of the leading reasons that drive consumers to abandon their shopping carts in the United States (Statista 2022). In this situation, free shipping has become a necessary incentive to stimulate consumers’ online purchases (Lewis 2006). Among top retailers that offer free shipping, only 17.5% offer free shipping on all orders (Freedman 2022). In comparison, over 65.4% of them offer free shipping under certain conditions, such as CFS and MFS, to retain consumers and recover shipping costs (Freedman 2022).

CFS is one of the most popular free shipping policies. It can lead to greater sales revenue since consumers need to buy more to get free shipping. According to a recent survey, 58% of consumers have added items to their shopping carts to qualify for free shipping (Comscore 2014). However, CFS may reduce store traffic (Lewis, Singh, and Fay 2006) when consumers purchase extra items and use them in the future. To compensate for CFS’s disadvantage while increasing retailers’ competitive advantage, retailers have begun to roll out MFS on top of CFS. Some retailers offer MFS with few added perks other than free shipping (Table 1). Other retailers integrate MFS into their subscription programs with additional benefits, such as streaming service with Amazon Prime. The retailer we study belongs to the former group, and the only member benefit is unlimited free shipping.

Literature Review

Free shipping promotion and CFS. Prior research has shown that shipping costs greatly influence consumers’ purchase frequency and order size. Using transaction data from an online grocer and drugstore, Lewis, Singh, and Fay (2006) examine the impact of different shipping fee schedules on consumers’ order incidence and order size. They find that free shipping promotion and CFS effectively boost sales, but the loss of shipping revenue and high shipping costs render these shipping structures unprofitable to retailers.

Similarly, Lewis (2006) finds that free shipping promotion leads to the highest order incidence with the smallest order size, and CFS always outperforms free shipping promotion in acquiring consumers and increasing retailers’ profits. Yang, Essegaier, and Bell (2005) also conclude that CFS influences consumers’ purchase behavior more effectively than free shipping promotions. Chen and Ngwe (2017) reveal that retailers should modify prices according to different product categories

when changing free shipping thresholds. More recently, Shehu, Papies, and Neslin (2020) find that free shipping promotions are unprofitable, as they may lead to increased product returns.

Membership-based free shipping. A limited number of gametheoretical studies have started to examine the performance of MFS. Sun, Cavusoglu, and Raghunathan (2022) study how MFS affects a single shipping platform (e.g., eBay) and its thirdparty sellers’ profits. They find that MFS can induce high demand and product price, benefiting both sellers and the platform. In another theoretical work, Fang et al. (2021) compare three shipping policies, including paid shipping, CFS, and MFS, in a duopolistic setting and focus on retailers’ optimal pricing schemes under different shipping policies. They conclude that the costs of free shipping cannot be recovered in general. Our study differs from these previous studies by quantifying the MFS enrollment effect on consumers’ actual purchase behaviors from multiple dimensions with real-world data.

Subscription program. Our study also relates to research on feebased subscription programs. Wagner, Pinto, and Amorim (2021) examine a subscription-based free delivery program in a grocery store setting and suggest that it hurts grocery stores’ profits because of picking costs, delivery costs, and cannibalization of in-store sales. Since consumers’ daily needs for groceries from local stores are less likely to change with free delivery, their findings offer limited implications for understanding consumers’ purchase behavior when shopping online for large varieties of products (e.g., electronics, clothing, personal care). Moreover, the cost structure of local grocery delivery differs from the shipping costs of online purchases, leading to different financial outcomes for retailers. Another related study is by Iyengar, Park, and Yu (2022). They study a subscription program for an online retailer and conclude that it can generate incremental sales and profits. Our study differs from that of Iyengar, Park, and Yu in two ways. First, Iyengar, Park, and Yu examine a subscription program offering many other benefits beyond free shipping to members, including gift cards and member discounts. More importantly, they find that the free shipping benefit “is not likely the driver of the observed effects” (Iyengar, Park, and Yu 2022, p. 1111), while other benefits are the reasons for the change in sales. This is not surprising, as free shipping is a less important benefit in their research context. For this retailer, which specializes in personal care products (e.g., skin care), the large majority of the orders satisfied free shipping requirements, regardless of being purchased by members or nonmembers. Due to the nature of their empirical context, Iyengar, Park, and Yu cannot offer insight into the focal research question of this study, that is, how MFS influences consumers’ purchase behaviors and revenue contribution. The MFS program we study offers the sole benefit of free shipping and thus allows us to isolate its effect. Secondly, the subscription program studied by Iyengar, Park, and Yu is almost free to members as they receive a no-restriction gift card that equals the amount of the annual subscription fee upon activation

Effects of MFS Enrollment on Consumers’ Purchase Behaviors

In this study, we focus on the following consumer purchase behaviors: how much to buy (i.e., total spending, purchase frequency, and average order size) and what to buy (i.e., purchase variety and impulse purchases). Prior literature on membership programs suggests that program-related factors, such as requirements for participation and reward design, affect consumers’ purchase behaviors (e.g., Liu and Yang 2009). The two salient and unique characteristics of MFS—an upfront membership fee and unlimited free shipping—may be linked to three behavioral theories, including switching cost effect, sunk cost effect, and no-minimum free shipping effect, and help us predict the impact of MFS on consumers’ purchase behaviors (Figure 1). Please note that our goal is to use these theories to predict the effect of MFS, not to test any specific theory.

Switching cost effect. Prior literature suggests that membership programs can lock in consumers by increasing switching costs (Henderson, Beck, and Palmatier 2011). In the context of MFS, consumers face both high economic and noneconomic switching costs. The economic switching costs are constituted by the initial investment (i.e., membership fees) and the savings from free shipping benefits (Leenheer et al. 2007). The noneconomic switching costs include learning cost (i.e., learning about another retailer), search cost (i.e., searching for equivalent free shipping promotions and price discounts), and psychological cost (i.e., giving up satisfaction and trust with the retailer) (Dick and Basu 1994). A well-established finding from previous literature is that switching costs increase consumer repurchase attention and enhance consumer engagement. Switching costs drive repeat purchases, leading to an increased

wallet share (Bolton, Kannan, and Bramlett 2000). They could also moderate the relationship between important determinants, including consumer-perceived value (e.g., Yang and Peterson 2004) and satisfaction. Therefore, switching costs also have a profound explanatory effect on consumers’ attitudes toward the retailer. Because of the increased purchase intention and enhanced positive attitude toward the retailer, we expect that consumers’ spending may increase after MFS enrollment. Moreover, as satisfied consumers’ goodwill and trust increase their intention to expand their purchase scope, they may purchase categories previously not bought from the retailer, resulting in an increased purchase variety and purchase of impulse products.

Furthermore, since it takes time for consumers to develop positive perceptions and attitudes toward the retailer (Dick and Lord 1998), the effects of switching costs might intensify over time. Thus, the increase in consumers’ spending, purchase frequency, purchase variety, and impulsive purchases over time could be explained by the increasing effects of switching costs.

Sunk cost effect. The upfront membership fee is an irreversible cost in consumers’ mental accounts. It represents a sunk cost, which has been shown to be very effective in encouraging consumers to commit to their prior choices (Dick and Lord 1998). Once the membership fee has been paid, consumers have a greater tendency to continue utilizing their membership benefits to maximize their investment (Ailawadi, Ma, and Grewal 2018). Moreover, to justify the money spent on membership fees, consumers will form a positive attitude toward their chosen retailer (Ashley, Gillespie, and Noble 2016), affecting their long-term commitment (Dick and Lord 1998). Therefore, we expect that the sunk cost of membership fees should have similar effects on consumers’ purchases as the switching cost effect. That is, an increase in members’ spending, purchase frequency, purchase variety, and purchase of impulse products might be partly explained by the sunk cost effect.

However, different from switching costs, sunk costs may become less salient over time (Iyengar, Park, and Yu 2022). Although consumers tend to immediately escalate commitment

No-minimum free shipping effect. Consumers are sensitive to shipping fees and thus very responsive to free shipping promotions (Lewis, Singh, and Fay 2006). Recall that, in our empirical setting, the retailer offers CFS to nonmembers. Compared with nonmembers, MFS members could enjoy the no-minimum free shipping and thus have no incentive to place large orders (Lewis 2006; Lewis, Singh, and Fay 2006). As a result, consumers may purchase more frequently with smaller order sizes after enrollment. Therefore, consumers’ total spending is expected not to change.

No-minimum free shipping may also affect consumers’ decisions on what to buy. Consumers often concentrate their purchases within limited product categories to meet the CFS threshold or minimize shipping fees (Chen and Ngwe 2017). With MFS, consumers are more likely to explore various product categories with different price levels to fit their true preferences (Rothschild 1974). We thus expect MFS members to increase their purchase variety. No-minimum free shipping also reduces consumers’ transaction costs. Such monetary benefit can effectively stimulate consumers’ impulse purchases as it alleviates their guilt about buying unplanned items (Khan and Dhar 2010). However, it may not directly affect consumers’ purchase of nonimpulse products, as these are necessities that consumers will buy regardless of the shipping fee. We thus expect an increased share of impulse products.

Overall, we expect a positive impact of MFS on consumers’ spending, purchase frequency, purchase variety, and purchase of impulse products because of the switching cost effect, sunk cost effect, and no-minimum free shipping effect. We expect that the no-minimum free shipping effect negatively impacts members’ order size as unlimited free shipping is the main reason consumers enroll in MFS, leading to a smaller order size. Moreover, we are not sure how the effects of MFS on consumers’ purchase behaviors change over time because of the opposite time trends of the switching cost effect and the sunk cost effect. We thus leave it to the empirics to determine the directions of these effects (Golder et al. 2023). In addition, we expect heterogeneous effects of MFS on consumers’ purchase behaviors and revenue contribution. Therefore, the effects discussed previously could be moderated by consumer characteristics, such as preenrollment spending, purchase variety, and willingness to pay shipping fees.

Data Overview

We obtained consumer order-level transaction data from a major online retailer in Asia, one of the top ten online business-to-consumer platforms in terms of market share. It carries various products, including apparel, electronics, beauty products, home goods, and books. The retailer provided us with 36 months of consumer transaction data. A crucial feature of this

data set is that the retailer launched MFS in the 22nd month of the observation period. To enroll in MFS, consumers pay an annual membership fee in exchange for unlimited free standard shipping on all orders, the only benefit provided by the program. Before the launch of MFS, the retailer offered CFS to all consumers. The CFS still applies to consumers who choose not to enroll in MFS. The CFS threshold was kept the same during the observation period. The launch of MFS was announced via multiple marketing channels, such as the retailer’s website, opt-in email, and media news. After that, the retailer did not engage in targeted promotions for members during the observation period, and there was no price discrimination between members and nonmembers. Other than the shipping policy, no significant business changes occurred during our observation period.

For data privacy and security reasons, the retailer randomly drew consumers from its database. Our data set contains consumer transaction information, including consumer ID, order ID, order type, purchase date and time, delivery method, shipping fee, product category, brand, quantity, transaction amount, promotion indicator, and zip code. To ensure the data are valid for estimating the effects of MFS enrollment, we apply the following criteria for consumer inclusion. First, we only include consumers who made at least one purchase before and after the MFS launch. This restriction ensures that we observe consumers’ purchase behavior before their MFS enrollment and that the estimations are not biased because of early churn. Second, we only include members who enrolled in MFS at least six months before the end of the sample period, which is necessary to analyze the dynamic impact of MFS enrollment. Third, we exclude outliers, who are possible third-party buyers and have made over 300 orders per year from a single product category, and consumers who have exclusively purchased digital items such as electronic gift cards and e-books, to which shipping policies are not applicable. Only .78% (20 members and matched nonmembers) of the sample are third-party buyers. They often make very large and frequent orders that satisfy free shipping regardless of their membership status. We thus expect MFS to have minimal effects on them. A robustness check including them in the sample confirms our expectation (Table W10 of Web Appendix A). After the filtering process, the final sample contains 5,354 consumers (2,225 members and 3,129 nonmembers) who made 176,602 orders during our data period. The order-level transaction data include a prelaunch period of 22 months and a postlaunch period of 14 months.

How much to buy. We first measure a consumer’s total spending (defined as the purchase amount in a given month). As total spending is determined by purchase frequency and order size, which may change with different shipping policies (Lewis, Singh, and Fay 2006), we thus include purchase frequency (defined as the number of orders in a month) and average order size (defined as average purchase amount in each order) as two other measures.

What to buy. We investigate the purchase variety and types of products affected by MFS enrollment. Purchase variety is measured as the number of distinct product categories a consumer purchases. Moreover, we follow previous literature and group products into impulse and nonimpulse products. Impulse products are the product categories that are more likely to trigger consumers’ spontaneous and unreflective purchases. These purchases are often made “on a whim” or when the urge is high (Narasimhan, Neslin, and Sen 1996, p. 22; Rook and Fisher 1995). In contrast, nonimpulse products are the product categories that consumers normally purchase or are necessities on their current shopping list (Rook and Hoch 1985). Specifically, we followed Narasimhan, Neslin, and Sen (1996) and Srinivasan et al. (2004) and asked consumers to rate each product category on a set of impulse scale items in a survey. We report the survey design in Web Appendix B. The categories with an impulse rating above 4 (a total of seven scales) are classified as impulse products. Specifically, impulse products include apparel, shoes, food, cosmetics/beauty/luxury beauty products, jewelry, and mobile/video games/DVDs/music, and nonimpulse products include musical instruments, automotive products, cameras, laptops and computers, electronics, bags/ luggage, wine, home/office/kitchen appliances, personal care appliances, watches, books, pet products, kid/baby products, sports equipment, outdoor items, and garden appliances and tools.1 Our classification is similar to that of Xu et al. (2017), confirming the validity of the survey. We then define impulse purchases as the percentage of spending on impulse products.

Research Design and Methodology Identification Strategy

Our research goal is to estimate the treatment effect of MFS enrollment on consumers’ purchase behaviors and revenue

contribution to the retailer. However, the observational nature of our data brings us two challenges for identifying the treatment effects. First, the decision of whether and when to enroll is endogenous, as customers may enroll in MFS when the perceived value of membership benefits exceeds the cost of enrollment. For example, frequent buyers are more likely to enroll in MFS to avoid shipping fees. The estimation may be upward biased without controlling for self-selection bias. Second, member enrollment occurs at different times, suggesting a staggered treatment pattern. Applying the classic DiD approach in a staggered treatment setting may lead to biased estimation (Goodman-Bacon 2021).

To this end, we first adopt a quasi-experimental design with PSM to address endogenous enrollment due to observed factors. The PSM aims to pair members (i.e., treated group) with similar nonmembers (i.e., control group) according to a rich set of observed characteristics (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). Next, we employ a stacked DiD approach that is suitable for the staggered treatment setting (Goodman-Bacon 2021). Furthermore, in case the time-varying enrollment decision is correlated with unobserved factors, we use a control function approach with an instrumental variable.

Control for Selection on Observables with PSM

PSM is the most widely used statistical technique for addressing selection bias in observational studies (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). The fundamental idea of PSM is to match the treated group to similar subjects in the control group according to the observed covariates and propensity to be treated. Then, the matched control group resembles the treated group in every observable aspect except for not being treated (Rosenbaum

and Rubin 1983). We pair each member with a comparable nonmember who has the same probability of MFS enrollment but “randomly” did not do so during the observation period.

We take the following two steps to implement PSM. First, we calculate each consumer’s propensity score (i.e., the probability of MFS enrollment) using a binomial logit model with the preenrollment variables (Xi) discussed previously. We have

Pr(Enrolli = 1|Xi) = Pr(β0 + βXi + ηi > 0), (3)

where ηi is a Type I extreme value distributed error term. The direction of the estimates shows face validity: consumers with higher spending and purchase variety are more likely to enroll in MFS (Table W1 of Web Appendix A). However, consumers who always purchase with large order sizes are less likely to enroll. This is because they can easily get free shipping under CFS. Consumers who live farther from the distribution center are less likely to enroll because standard free shipping offered by MFS is less valuable to them. Consumers who regularly use scheduled delivery prefer to receive products at their convenience. As a result, they are more likely to accumulate items and purchase with large orders beyond the CFS threshold. Therefore, waiving free shipping is less likely to be an attractive benefit for them to enroll in MFS.

We next match nonmembers to similar members according to the estimated propensity scores. The baseline matching algorithm takes the 1:1 matching with a caliper (.01 times the standard deviation of the propensity scores) and replacement. This matching algorithm can generate the closest matched pairs without dropping a large proportion of members who do not have ideal nonmembers to match. We also apply other matching algorithms as robustness checks to assess the results’ stability and discuss them subsequently. As a result, 1,957 members are matched with similar nonmembers.5

To check the quality of PSM, we first compare the distributions of propensity scores between members and nonmembers before and after matching. Figure 2 shows that the distributions of propensity scores of the two groups are visually indistinguishable after matching. Next, we check the mean value of covariates (Xi) between members and matched nonmembers (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983) and find that these two groups do not significantly differ on any observed covariate (Table W2 and Figure W2 of Web Appendix A).

Control for Selection on Unobservables with Instrumental Variable

The inclusion of fixed effects in DiD regression addresses the endogeneity of MFS enrollment by controlling for individualspecific or time-specific unobservables that influence consumers’ enrollment decisions and purchase decisions. However, there are unobserved factors that might vary across both time and individuals. One example is the ever-changing customer satisfaction with the retailer, influencing their decisions to enroll and to purchase. We use a time-varying instrumental variable combined with the control function approach to address such endogeneity (Heckman 1979; Wooldridge 2010). In the first stage, we regress a consumer’s enrollment decision on an instrumental variable, along with cohort fixed effects and time fixed effects, using a probit regression.6 Specifically, we use the percentage of members who reside in the same zip code as the focal consumer in a given month as the instrumental variable. The consumers in the data reside in 256 different zip codes across the country. This instrumental variable is provided by the retailer, not calculated from our sample. In the second stage, we use the generalized residual obtained from the firststage estimation as an additional covariate in estimating the outcome equations (Equations 2a and 2b).7

The instrumental variable is correlated with the consumer enrollment decision because people around a consumer may form peer effects that influence a consumer’s decision to join a membership program (Bapna and Umyarov 2015). The more people around a consumer enroll in MFS, the higher the probability the consumer will enroll. Table W4 of Web Appendix A shows that the percentage of members is a significant predictor of whether a consumer enrolls in MFS. We test the strength of the instrumental variable using the incremental F-statistic in the first stage of regression.8

More importantly, the instrumental variable should satisfy the exclusion restrictions; that is, it has no direct effect on consumers’ purchase outcomes, and the only way it affects the outcomes is by affecting their enrollment decision (Nunn and Wantchekon 2009). It is reasonable to assume that neighbors’ enrollments

do not directly impact a consumer’s purchase outcomes. Undeniably, the percentage of members in an area may be correlated with geographic and socioeconomic features, which have been controlled with the customer-level fixed effects.

To further confirm that the exclusion restriction is satisfied, we follow previous literature and perform a falsification exercise (e.g., Ailawadi, Ma, and Grewal 2018; Nunn and Wantchekon 2009). We examine the reduced-form relationship between the percentage of members in a consumer’s zip code (the instrument) and the consumer’s purchase outcomes using a sample of consumers who purchase exclusively digital products (e.g., e-cards and e-books). Because no shipping or delivery is necessary for digital products, these consumers are not subject to any shipping policies, including MFS. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that MFS does not apply to this sample. Suppose the instrumental variable only affects purchase outcome through MFS enrollment. In that case, we expect no relationship between the percentage of members in an area and consumer purchase outcomes for this digital-purchase-only sample after controlling for other observed variables. Indeed, Table W5 of Web Appendix A shows that the coefficient of the percentage of members is not significant for any purchase outcome variables. Therefore, we have theoretical and empirical justification for the exogeneity of our instrument variable. We conducted a series of robustness checks to test the stability of our results, described in the “Robustness Checks” section.

Heterogeneity in Enrollment Effects

Understanding the heterogeneous effect of MFS enrollment is strategically important for retailers to identify and engage their best-value consumers with MFS. We next conduct a heterogeneity analysis to reveal whether and how MFS enrollment effects vary across customer segments. Prior studies have shown that the effect of membership programs might be moderated by consumers’ initial usage levels (Liu 2007), variety seeking (Zhang, Krishna, and Dhar 2000), and sensitivity to transaction costs (Kopalle et al. 2012). We thus examine the moderating effect of these consumers’ characteristics, which are measured correspondingly by their preenrollment spending (spendingi), purchase variety (purchase_varietyi), and willingness to pay shipping fees (%_orders_paying_shipping_feei), on the treatment effect of MFS. For each characteristic, we subsequently develop the expectation of its impact on spending and NCR.

Moderating effect of preenrollment spending. We posit that the treatment effects of MFS on spending and NCR might be smaller for buyers who previously spent more (i.e., heavy buyers with higher total spendingi) because of a ceiling effect. Heavy buyers might already reach their consumption limit and have less potential to increase their subsequent consumption than light buyers (Liu 2007). In contrast, light buyers are still well under their consumption “ceilings.” Once they commit to the retailer as a paid member, they are more likely to consolidate their spending with the retailer, leading to more

purchases. In addition, heavy buyers are more likely to enroll (Table W1 of Web Appendix A) to exploit free shipping benefits, resulting in less net revenue than others. Thus, we expect the treatment effect of MFS on both spending and NCR to be smaller for heavy buyers.

Moderating effect of preenrollment purchase variety. We posit that the treatment effect of MFS on spending might be larger for consumers who previously purchased from diverse product categories (i.e., those with a higher value of purchase_varietyi). The diversity of purchased product categories reflects consumers’ familiarity with the retailer, making them more likely to increase their purchases after enrollment. Moreover, consumers often have a limited budget for each product category (Heath and Soll 1996). The total budget consumers are willing to spend at the retailer could be higher for variety seekers. As a result, these consumers are more likely to respond to unlimited free shipping and spend more with the retailer.

Moderating effect of willingness to pay shipping fees. We posit that the treatment effect of MFS on spending might be stronger for consumers who are more willing to pay shipping fees (i.e., those with a higher %_orders_paying_shipping_feei). Consumers who are unwilling to pay shipping fees are more likely to accumulate large orders to avoid paying those fees under CFS. After enrollment, they may take advantage of the unlimited free shipping and break large orders into smaller ones without spending more. This purchase behavior change increases the shipping cost absorbed by the retailer and thus hurts its profit. On the contrary, consumers who are willing to pay shipping fees might purchase whenever they need and thus pay more shipping fees before enrollment. Joining the MFS could benefit them more than others because of the reduced transaction costs, leading to an increase in spending and revenue contribution.

Robustness Checks

Alternative explanation on renewal effect. Consumers may increase their purchases over time because they want to get the most out of their last months of membership. If that is the case, we would observe a decrease in their purchases after the 12-month membership period, especially for those who choose not to renew their membership. We estimate Equation 2a with data extended to the months after the membership ends and find no significant difference between members’ monthly spending in the last quarter of the membership period and the first quarter after the membership expires (Table W9 of Web Appendix A). More important, there is no significant difference in the first-quarter spending after the membership expires between members who renewed and those who did not renew. These findings suggest that the effect of MFS goes beyond the membership period, ruling out the alternative explanation.

Conclusions and Implications

An increasing number of retailers have adopted MFS with an expectation to balance the free shipping required by consumers and the need to recover shipping costs. However, the effectiveness of MFS is still under debate. In collaboration with a top online retailer that experimented with MFS, our study takes the first step to systematically examine the effects of MFS on consumers’ purchase behaviors, retailers’ financial outcomes, and customer retention.

Theoretical Implications

Our study advances three major themes in the marketing literature. First, we add to the shipping policy literature by investigating an emerging shipping policy, MFS. Lewis (2006) and Lewis, Singh, and Fay (2006) demonstrate that temporary free shipping promotions can successfully increase order incidence but lead to smaller order sizes. Our results support this finding in the context in which retailers offer unlimited free shipping for members and CFS for nonmembers. While Shehu, Papies, and Neslin (2020) show that consumers spend more with temporary free shipping promotions, we find that an average MFS member may not initially purchase more but does so over time. Besides the expenditure, Lewis (2006) and Shehu, Papies, and Neslin (2020) find that free shipping might hurt retailers’ financial outcomes as the increased sales cannot offset the lost shipping revenue and other associated costs. However, our findings suggest that in the context of MFS, the upfront membership fee is an important revenue source and can compensate for the shipping costs. The profitability of MFS may vary across customer segments due to their different behavior change. Although heavy buyers have long been treated as the most valuable customers for relationship management

(Liu 2007), we find that those customers may not be as profitable for the MFS program as expected. This is because when heavy buyers exploit the unlimited free shipping benefit, they do not spend more but significantly increase the shipping costs absorbed by retailers. In contrast, MFS effectively enhances light and moderate buyers’ purchase and revenue contribution. These findings also reconcile prior inconclusive results on the profitability of MFS from theoretical studies, which suggest that the adoption of MFS could make retailers either gain (Sun, Cavusoglu, and Raghunathan 2022) or lose (Fang et al. 2021). Furthermore, our work adds to this stream of literature by examining the effect of free shipping on purchased product types. Shehu, Papies, and Neslin suggest that free shipping would encourage purchases from high-risk product categories. Our study focuses on other aspects of product types and reveals that free shipping leads to purchases from more diverse product categories, especially impulse products.

Second, our research contributes to the literature on membership programs (e.g., loyalty and subscription programs) by showing the dynamic changes in consumers’ purchase behaviors over the membership period. According to prior studies, the sunk cost effect of initial investment (e.g., Ailawadi, Ma, and Grewal 2018; Iyengar, Park, and Yu 2022) and the switching cost effect of cumulated rewards (e.g., Lewis 2004; Zhang and Breugelmans 2012) might be the drivers of consumers’ subsequent repurchase decisions. Moreover, these effects may change over time. For example, Iyengar, Park, and Yu (2022) study a subscription program with member-only price discounts and gift cards being the most important benefits and find that the increase in consumer spending is the strongest in the first month after enrollment and decreases over time. This is because the effect of sunk cost on a consumer’s mental account wears off after the initially escalated commitment. On the contrary, we show that the treatment effect of MFS increases over time. Our findings suggest that the switching cost caused by free shipping benefits and membership fees might become more salient over time. It might dominate the wearing out of the sunk cost effect, as the members enjoy the ease of shopping and gradually become more engaged and committed to the retailer.

Finally, we add to the customer retention literature (e.g., Bolton, Kannan, and Bramlett 2000; Lewis 2004). Prior research investigates the effectiveness of various marketing tactics on customer retention, such as customer relationship management (e.g., Bolton, Kannan, and Bramlett 2000; Verhoef 2003), targeted marketing campaigns (Ascarza 2018), and price discounts (Lewis 2006). We extend this work by empirically revealing the positive effect of MFS on customer retention regarding the length of the relationship. Moreover, we find that MFS is especially effective in preventing churn in consumers who would like to shop in diverse product categories.

Managerial Implications

From a managerial perspective, our study demonstrates that MFS is an effective shipping policy to solve retailers’ free shipping dilemma. MFS can enhance retailers’ revenue as the

membership fees could recoup the shipping costs. Moreover, our study suggests that consumers are more likely to exploit their unlimited free shipping benefits at the beginning of enrollment. In this period, consumers break large orders into smaller ones without increasing their total spending. Retailers may not gain incremental revenue due to the reduced profit margin and increased shipping costs. However, the free shipping benefit builds a switching barrier that motivates consumers to purchase more frequently with larger order sizes over time. It eventually leads to increased spending and revenue contribution. Such dynamic changes in purchase behaviors suggest that the MFS program may help consumers form a positive perception of the retailer and transform consumers’ occasional purchases into repeat patronage behaviors.

Next, MFS might have different, even opposite, effects on revenue across customer segments. Managers should pay attention to the diverse responses from various customer segments to ensure the overall profitability of MFS. Conventionally, retailers treat heavy buyers who spent the most before enrollment as the best customer segment. In contrast to this view, our study supports the argument of the heavy buyer fallacy that these consumers are less likely to contribute additional revenue due to their consumption ceiling (Lal and Bell 2003). Heavy buyers can even bring negative revenue to the MFS program if they are unwilling to pay shipping fees or have low purchase variety during the preenrollment period. These consumers often take advantage of the unlimited free shipping without spending more, resulting in greatly increased shipping costs to the retailer. Instead, light buyers, especially those willing to pay shipping fees or variety seekers, are the groups with greater potential to increase their revenue contribution after enrollment. Free shipping motivates them to consolidate their spending. When promoting the MFS program, managers should reallocate the marketing resources to target and encourage the more responsive customer segments to enroll.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study could be investigated in future studies. First, our study investigates how free shipping as the only paid membership benefit changes consumers’ purchase behaviors and the ultimate revenue. However, some retailers tend to add additional benefits to the MFS program subsequently, such as streaming service on its own or partner platforms and premium customer service. Consumers’ perceived value of MFS may differ for these subscription programs with added major perks. Therefore, our findings may not be generalized to retailers that offer other major perks on top of MFS. Second, the effect size of MFS on consumers’ purchases may change with membership fees and the CFS threshold. For example, a lower free shipping threshold may result in smaller effects of MFS as the unlimited free shipping benefit becomes less attractive. A higher membership fee may prompt members to purchase more to make their investment worthwhile. The ratio of the CFS threshold to membership fees offered by the retailer in this study is about 1:3. It is comparable

with its local competitors and giant retailers in the United States, such as Walmart. Our empirical results can be generalized to retailers with a similar ratio of these fees. Future studies could investigate to what extent the changes in such a ratio may lead to different findings. Lastly, we focus on the effect of MFS on customer retention, but factors that impact customers’ membership renewal and cancellation decisions also have significant managerial implications. We leave these analyses as a fruitful topic for future research.
