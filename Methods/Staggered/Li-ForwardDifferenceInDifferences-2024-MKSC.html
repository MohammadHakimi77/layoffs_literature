1. Introduction Marketing researchers are interested in establishing causal effects in a variety of contexts (Busse et al. 2006, Goldfarb and Tucker 2011, Puranam et al. 2017, Guo et al. 2020, Berman and Israeli 2022). The difference-in- differences (DID) method is the most popular method to estimate treatment effects, such as the average treatment effect (ATE) and the average treatment effect on the trea ted (ATT), when using quasiexperimental data. DID is simple, easy to implement, and widely applicable. In fact, it even applies to data with only two time periods (one pretreatment period and one posttreatment period) and any number of treatment and control units. Market ing scholars have used DID to study how television advertising influences online shopping (Liaukonyte et al. 2015), how showroom openings affect sales (Bell et al. 2018), and the impact of a tax on sweetened beverages (Seiler et al. 2021) among many others (Mantin and Rubin 2016, Calzada and Gil 2020, Johnson et al. 2020, Todri 2022, Wang et al. 2022, Li and Van den Bulte 2023).

However, DID has a restrictive identifying assump tion that may not be met in many data settings. The DID parallel trends assumption states that the

treatment unit’s outcomes would have been parallel to the average of the control units’ outcomes during the posttreatment periods in the absence of treatment. Because this assumption is a statement about a counter factual outcome of what the treatment outcomes would have been without treatment, it cannot be checked empirically. Therefore, a common practice is to check whether the parallel trends assumption holds during the pretreatment periods (which we call parallel pretrends assumption). Having parallel pretrends together with the assumption that the correlation between the treat ment and control units’ outcomes is stable during the posttreatment periods in the absence of treatment identi fies the treatment effect (e.g., ATT). Therefore, selecting the most relevant control units so that the DID parallel pretrends assumption is likely to hold is of utmost importance. When selection into treatment is based on some observables, various covariate-matching methods, such as propensity score or K-nearest-neighbor match ing, can be used to select the control units. When selec tion is based on unobservables, the extant literature does not offer a satisfying solution for how to select control units for the DID method.

However, there have been many recent developments expanding the causal inference tool kit beyond DID for marketers to include more flexible estimators, although they require long time series. Perhaps the most popular alternative approach is the synthetic control (SC) method proposed by Abadie and Gardeazabal (2003) and Abadie et al. (2010). The SC method relaxes the DID equal weight assumption and uses a weighted average of con trols to estimate the treatment unit’s counterfactual out comes. Allowing for flexible weights can better handle heterogeneity between treatment and control units. The SC method has been used to estimate the effect of TV advertising on online chatter (Tirunillai and Tellis 2017) and the effect of banning foreign pharmacies from spon sored search (Chesnes et al. 2017). Another method pro posed by Hsiao et al. (2012), which we will refer to as the HCW method, is an even more flexible method that imposes no constraints on the weights for controls units. However, these flexible methods also have limitations. First, the inference theory for the synthetic control method and HCW methods do not exist under certain data settings (e.g., nonstationary data where the struc ture of nonstationary is unknown). Second, the HCW method is not feasible when the number of control units is greater than the number of pretreatment time periods and likely suffers from overfitting.

Recently, Shi and Huang (2023) proposed the For ward HCW (FHCW) method, which uses a forward selection algorithm to select an appropriate subset of control units and then, applies the HCW method. For ward selection is an iterative algorithm that chooses a relevant subset of control units. It proceeds by first choosing the best subset of size 1. Then, it chooses the best subset of size 2 that includes the first subset. The algorithm continues to choose greater subsets that have one more control unit than the previous subset in the same way. Forward HCW can be applied even when HCW breaks down but has several limitations. First, similar to the synthetic control and HCW methods, there is no inference theory for nonstationary data of unknown structure, which is a key drawback as mar keting data often fall into this category. Second, For ward HCW may still suffer from overfitting if it selects a large number of control units, and it is infeasible when the number of selected control units is larger than the number of pretreatment time periods.

We propose a simple forward difference-in-differences (Forward DID) method that takes advantage of the for ward selection algorithm to select a relevant subset of control units and avoids the overfitting problem of the Forward HCW method. Forward DID flexibly selects the appropriate control units and therefore, is more likely able to accurately estimate the counterfactual and ATT than the DID method. Forward DID has several advan tages. First, as a flexible alternative to DID, Forward DID can be widely applied and suitable even when DID is too

restrictive. Second, Forward DID can accommodate any number of control units. Third, there are no overfitting concerns because Forward DID only needs to estimate one parameter after identifying a subset of control units using the forward selection algorithm. Fourth, by using a greedy algorithm that is a myopic approximation of an optimal algorithm, the Forward DID has computational advantages over algorithms that consider all possible subsets of control units. Fifth, we develop the inference theory, which is applicable to both stationary and non stationary data. Finally, we establish the consistency of the Forward DID method. We compare the performance of the Forward DID method with alternative methods using simulations and an empirical application to store openings.1

2.2. Forward Difference-in-Differences Algorithm When using DID to estimate the treatment effect (e.g., ATT), researchers need to decide which control units to choose from the potential control units. Deciding how to select relevant control units is a challenging and impor tant problem. When selection is based on observables, various covariate-matching methods have been pro posed in the literature. See Abadie (2021) for a survey of

various matching methods. The assumption behind the covariate-matching method is that when covariates of a control unit and a treatment unit are close to each other, then it is likely that their outcomes are parallel in the absence of treatment. However, this assumption can fail in practice. The similarity of outcomes may depend on some unobserved latent factors, and therefore, matching based on observed covariates may not be enough to ensure that DID can be used.

We propose a simple Forward DID method that first uses a forward selection algorithm to select a relevant subset of control units and then applies the DID method. Forward DID flexibly selects the appropriate control units and therefore, is more likely able to accu rately estimate the counterfactual and ATT than the DID method. Next, we outline the steps of the Forward DID method.

Step 1. Select one control unit. Without loss of gener ality, choose the ith unit from the control pool. We then estimate ATT using Equations (2.3)–(2.7) but replace yN co,t with yit (Nco1 in this initial step). Do this for each of the N control units (i  1,:::,N) to create N one- control-unit DID models. Choose the model with the highest R2

The first one has one control unit, the second one has two control units, … , and the last one has N con trol units. Choose the model with the highest R2 value from these N models. This is the model that the For ward DID method uses to estimate the ATT. We use c N co to denote the subset of controls selected by the For ward DID method.

Note that there is no concern of overfitting no matter how large Nco is as all models only contain a single unknown parameter α. Unlike a conventional regression model, all these submodels contain only one parameter α, and therefore, R2 is identical to the adjusted R2. The forward selection algorithm is a myopic approximation of the algorithm that would optimally sequence control units. However, allowing some degree of suboptimally buys us computational efficiency. This is especially important when the number of control units, N, is large and the optimal algorithm becomes intractable. Forward

3. Store Opening on Sales Changing consumer needs have made it imperative for online retailers to identify factors that are critical for its growth. We study an online-first retailer that sells men’s clothing and accessories. When the retailer launched in 2007, it started completely online and later opened brick-and-mortar stores in certain cities so that custo mers could touch and try the products before deciding to purchase. However, given the high cost of opening a physical store, it is important to accurately assess the benefit or the additional increase in sales because of the store opening.

We estimate the causal effect of opening a store in a city on the retailer’s sales in the city. We use the For ward DID method and compare it with four alterna tive methods: (1) DID, (2) SC, (3) synthetic control with an intercept (SCI), and (4) Forward HCW. We cannot consider the HCW method because it is not feasible in these empirical applications, where the number of con trol units (60) is larger than the number of pretreat ment time periods (23 or 24). We have data from January 2014 to December 2016. During this time period, the retailer opened a store in Atlanta, Georgia in December 2014 and opened stores in San Diego, Cal ifornia and San Jose, California in January 2015. These were the first stores to open in their respective cities of Atlanta, San Diego, and San Jose. Therefore, these 3 cit ies are the treatment units, and 60 cities without stores serve as the potential control units. The DID method uses all 60 control units, whereas the other methods (SC, SCI, Forward HCW, and Forward DID) generally use a subset of the control units.

One major advantage of Forward DID over other flexible alternative estimators to DID is inference the ory that is widely applicable. We develop the inference theory for the Forward DID method, which is applica ble to both stationary and nonstationary data of known and unknown structure. Like DID, Forward DID uses a simple (unweighted) average of control units, which avoids having to estimate a large number of parameters and therefore, leads to simple inference. By contrast, other alternative methods to DID, such as the synthetic control methods (SC and SCI) that use a weighted aver aged of control units and the HCW and Forward HCW methods that use a linear combination of control units, do not have inference theory when data are nonstation ary of unknown structure.

We now compute the CIs for the Forward DID and alternative methods (DID, SC, SCI, and Forward HCW). Although we compute the confidence intervals for the SC, SCI, and Forward HCW methods for the sake of completeness, the confidence intervals for these methods may not be reliable for the following reasons. For the SC and SCI methods, there is no inference the ory when the data are nonstationary of unknown structure, which is the setting of this empirical applica tion. To compute the CIs for SC and SCI, we apply the subsampling procedure proposed by Li (2020), whose validity requires that the number of pretreatment time periods is much larger than the number of control units and that the data are stationary data or nonsta tionary data of known structure. The Forward HCW method as discussed previously suffers from overfit ting in-sample, which means that the ATT is unreliable.

By contrast, SCI is preferable to Forward DID when For ward DID is not applicable, but SCI is still applicable. This can occur because SCI is more general than For ward DID as SCI allows weights on different control units to be different.

4. Conclusion The difference-in-differences method is the most popu lar quasiexperimental approach for estimating causal effects. It is simple, easy to implement, and applicable for any number of treatment and control units and for any number of pretreatment and posttreatment time periods. However, its identifying parallel trends assumption may be violated in quasiexperimental data where the treat ment and control units are heterogeneous. There have been many recent developments expanding the causal inference tool kit to include more flexible estimators, such as the synthetic control method (with and without an intercept), the HCW method, and the Forward HCW method. However, these flexible methods also have limitations. First, their inference theory does not exist for nonstationary data of unknown structure. Second, the (Forward) HCW is not feasible when the number of (selected) control units is greater than the number of pretreatment time periods and likely suffers from overfitting.

We propose a simple Forward DID method that takes advantage of the forward selection algorithm to flexibly select a relevant subset of control units. The Forward DID has several advantages. First, it can be widely applied and suitable even when DID is too restrictive. Second, Forward DID can accommodate any number of control units. Third, there are no overfitting concerns because Forward DID only needs to estimate one param eter after identifying a relevant subset of control units. Fourth, Forward DID has computational advantages over

algorithms that consider all possible subsets of control units. Fifth, we develop the inference theory that is appli cable to both stationary and nonstationary data of known and unknown structures. Finally, we establish the consis tency of the Forward DID method. The inference theory and the consistency results are the main theoretical results of the paper. To facilitate ease of use, we provide a practitioner’s guide outlining steps of how to use For ward DID to estimate the ATT and conduct inference in the appendix. Although we only consider estimation of ATT based on treatment and control units’ outcome vari ables, future research could consider adding relevant covariates to the Forward DID method.

We calculate the ATT and compare the out-ofsample performance of the Forward DID method with four alternative methods (DID, SC, SCI, and Forward HCW) using simulations (presented in Online Appen dix E) and an empirical application to store openings. In simulations, DID and SC methods perform best for DGPs where DID is applicable, which is expected, although Forward DID still performs well. For DGPs where DID is not applicable, Forward DID and the syn thetic control method with an intercept perform best, with the Forward DID outperforming the synthetic control method with an intercept when the pre- and posttreatment periods are shorter. In the empirical applications, the Forward DID method performs well in all cases and outperforms alternative methods in the out-of-sample prediction. Forward DID and SCI are the only methods (of those considered) that are applicable in all three cities, and although they perform very simi larly in terms of in-sample fit and ATT estimates, For ward DID is preferable to SCI because inference theory is more widely available, which leads to more reliable confidence intervals.

As with any methodology, it is also important to understand its boundary conditions. The Forward DID method should not be used when its identifying paral lel trends assumption is violated. An example of this scenario would be when the intercept-adjusted treat ment is outside the range of the control units (e.g., the treatment unit’s outcome has an upward trend that is steeper than that of all the control units’ outcomes). In such a case, it is not possible to find a subset of control units whose average traces the treatment unit well in the pretreatment period.

Fortunately, because of the flexibility of the forward selection algorithm, there are many empirical marketing and business settings in which the Forward DID is appli cable. The Forward DID belongs to a class of flexible alternatives to the DID method. These flexible alterna tive methods should be used when DID is not applicable because of its identifying parallel trends assumption being violated. Compared with other flexible alterna tives to the DID method (e.g., SC with or without an intercept, HCW, and Forward HCW), the Forward DID
