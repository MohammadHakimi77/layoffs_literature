The Effects of Downsizing on

Organizational Buying Behavior: An Empirical Investigation

Jeffrey E. Lewin

Western Carolina University

To survive in today's highly competitive markets, many firms are initiating fundamental changes in organizational form and practice. These restructuring efforts are having

significant effects on the organization and management of work within customer firms. However, these important changes have been largely ignored in the extant marketing literature. The research presented in this article first de- scribes a general theory of the effects of organizational downsizing. Then, it examines the potential effects of downsizing on buying center structure and purchase par- ticipant characteristics. Findings support several of the hypotheses related to the proposed effects of organiza- tional downsizing on the outcome variables of interest.

The pace of organizational change has accelerated, and most organizations now operate within much more com- plex environments (Watkins 1994). In the past, many orga- nizations focused on vertical integration as a means of increasing control in uncertain environments (cf. William- son 1975). Today, however, organizations are finding it less beneficial to own and operate a large number of facto- des or to employ a large number of people (cf. Cascio and Young 1997; Werhane 1999). To survive, many firms are looking for alternatives to hierarchical structures and are altering their form and practice to adapt to rapid change and increased competition. These developments have collec- tively precipitated a shift in the corporate paradigm toward smaller, flexible organizations that can better accommodate novelty, innovation, and change (Bahrami 1992). As a result of these changes, the practice of corporate downsizing has

become pervasive. Even as economies expand, downsizing continues, and experts believe that the downsizing trend is far from over (cf. Challenger 1999; Porter 1998)--since much of the change seems to represent permanent shifts in economic and organizational structures (McKinley, Sanchez, and Schick 1995).

Purchasing deparunents have not escaped these trends (Giunipcro, Dawley, and Anthony 1999). Furthermore, the extensiveness of downsizing in procurement has many deeply concerned (Morgan 1997). For example, a recent survey found that 41 percent of purchasers had experi- enced significant downsizing in their departments in recent years (Fitzgerald 1999). In a separate survey, pur- chasers indicated that downsizing had left them woefully shorthanded, leaving existing staff to juggle too many projects, too many deadlines, and too many work hours (Porter 1999). As Michaels, Kumar, and Samu (1995) observe, due to a variety of factors, including downsizing, "purchasing decisions are currently being made under conditions of greater uncertainty than ever before" (p. 11).

For savvy marketers, however, these conditions are creating important opportunities. These opportunities lie in first understanding the changing needs of today's down- sized purchasers and then carefully developing the prod- uct-service combination that best satisfies these needs. Therefore, one major objective of this research is to exam- ine these important issues to provide marketers with insights into how they can refine their offering to best sat- isfy downsized customers' needs. This topic is discussed in greater detail in the Managerial Implications section.

To that end, drawing on threat-rigidity thesis (Staw, Sandelands, and Dutton 1981), classic administration the- try (Blau 1970; Child 1973), and the more recent work of Sutton and D'Aunno (1989), this study investigates the effects of organizational downsizing on buying center struc- ture and purchase participant characteristics. It should be noted, however, that the proposed theory and related out- comes of downsizing studied in this research may be appli- cable to other forms of organizational redesign. ~

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: first, the theoretical underpinnings are reviewed. Then, applying this theoretical grounding, the proposed model and associated hypotheses are developed. Next, the study's methods and results are presented, and finally, the managerial implications of the findings are discussed.

THEORETICAL RELATIONSHIPS

Threat-rigidity thesis (Staw et al. 1981) posits that decreases in organizational resources (e.g., workforce size) are threatening to organizational members. The anxi- ety generated by such threats is proposed to lead to mecha- nistic structures throughout the organization, as managers seek to consolidate their control. Thus, to the extent that decreasing size is threatening, departments and deci- sion-making groups within downsizing organizations are expected to shift toward more mechanistic structures as manifested through (1) increased centralization of author- ity, (2) increased emphasis on formal rules and procedures, and (3) decreased participative decision making. In con- trast, classic administrative theory suggests that as organi- zations decrease in size, problems of coordination and

control are reduced and can be handled by more organic means, including decentralization of authority, decreased emphasis on formal rules and procedures, and increases in participative decision making (cf. Sutton and D'Aunno

1989). Attempting to reconcile this seemingly contradictory set of expected outcomes, Sutton and D'Aunno (1989) argue that a significant reduction in workforce size evokes both (1) mechanistic shifts in organizational structure due to the increased anxiety among surviving organizational members and (2) at the same time, a decrease in the organi- zation's need for coordination and control brought about by the smaller workforce. These competing forces occur simultaneously, but initially the anxiety produced by workforce reduction will prevail, and the expected mecha- nistic shifts will likely occur. However, once the anxiety provoked by workforce reduction wanes, many of these mechanistic means of coordination and control will be gradually eliminated.

Furthermore, Sutton and D'Aunno (1989) identify three key downsizing characteristics that significantly influence the expected effects: (1) the degree ofdownsiz- ing, (2) the severity of implementation tactics used to downsize, and (3) members' negativity in outcome expec- tations as to the results of downsizing (discussed more fully below). Recent empirical studies have found some support for these downsizing characteristics (cf. Robbins

1999; Wager 1997, 1998). Sutton and D'Aunno (1989) also propose that downsiz- ing firms can be expected to move through two distinct stages. These stages likely will moderate the effects of these three downsizing characteristics. Specifically, dur- ing Stage 1 (Stage 1 firms are defined as firms that are either currently downsizing or have completed downsiz- ing within the past 2 years), the stress and anxiety brought about by greater degrees of downsizing, more severe implementation tactics, and greater negativity in outcome expectations are amplified and lead to increased mecha- nistic structures. However, in the longer term, when down- sizing initiatives are completed and fears of further down- sizing initiatives disappear, the threat abates and organizations move into Stage 2 (Stage 2 firms are defined as firms that have previously completed downsizing, have not implemented downsizing initiatives for 2 or more years, and are not expected to downsize further in the fore- seeable future). This latter shift brings about a change in structure so that more organic means of coordination and control will be adopted throughout the organization. 2

Degree of Downsizing

The degree of reduction in workforce size may significantly influence the strength of the anticipated effects. For example, Staw et al. (1981) argue that the shift toward mechanistic structures depends on whether the reduction

represents a significant, fundamental shift in resources or a modest, temporary disruption in an essentially supportive environment. Similarly, Ford and Bacus (1987) observe that if managers perceive reductions in resources as minor and temporary, little or no rigidity may occur. In contrast, large losses in resources may cause managers to construe that they face a significant threat, and resulting rigidities in structures occur. Analogous arguments can be found in the works of Sutton and D'Aunno (1989; D' Aunno and Sutton

1992). In addition, Sutton and Kahn (1987) argue that as the magnitude and duration of downsizing initiatives increase, survivors' perceptions of threat and uncertainty increase as well. These growing stressors lead to greater levels of role conflict and risk aversion and lower levels of organizational commitment.

Severity of Implementation Tactics

The extent to which workforce reduction is threatening may depend on the implementation tactics through which it is accomplished. Natural attrition and voluntary rede- ployment are often viewed as the least severe tactics and layoff without assistance and abrupt termination the most severe. Sutton and D'Aunno (1989) argue that "layoffs (i.e., terminations) are clearly the most threatening form of work force reduction for all groups of surviving employ- ees" (p. 205). The increased threat comes from at least two sources. First, survivors feel guilty because they avoided the fate of their former coworkers; second, survivors worry they will be terminated next if further downsizing is expected (Brockner et al. 1986). In contrast, downsizing through natural attrition or voluntary redeployment is much less threatening since survivors have no reason to feel either guilty or anxious (Sutton and D'Aunno 1989). Thus, it is expected that the use of severe strategies will be more threatening to survivors and provoke more pro- nounced and longer lasting mechanistic shifts in structure. In addition, the use of severe strategies likely will increase levels of role conflict and risk aversion and decrease levels of organizational commitment felt by survivors. Support for these arguments can be found in the work of Brockner, DeWitt, Grover, and Reed (1990); Brockner et al. (1994); and Wager (1997 ).

Negativity of Outcome Expectations

Finally, organizational members' expectations of the outcomes related to downsizing can influence the pre- dicted effects. For example, members may perceive down- sizing as a "necessary evil" needed to ensure the firm's long-term health (Mishra and Spreitzer 1998). To the extent that such positive perceptions are collectively held, it is expected that downsizing will lead to less rigidity in structure and that the transition to more organic structures likely will occur more quickly (Sutton and D'Aunno

1989). In contrast, uncertainty surrounding downsizing is likely to increase when survivors believe that downsizing was avoidable, question the legitimacy of the decision to downsize, and begin to speculate that the organization will take additional questionable actions (Brockner et al.

1990). In these situations, as survivors develop negative outcome expectations--believing that downsizing will result in a weakened organization--greater rigidity is expected and will likely continue until these collective negative perceptions subside (Sutton and D'Aunno 1989). These negative perceptions also are expected to increase survivors' levels of role conflict and risk aversion and decrease levels of organizational commitment. Limited support for these propositions is found in the work of Brockner et al. (1990) and Wager (1997).

DOWNSIZING'S EFFECT ON ORGANIZATIONAL BUYING BEHAVIOR

Coupling Sutton and D'Aunno's (1989) propositions with the extant theoretical and empirical work found in the management and psychology literatures (discussed more fully below), the model shown in Figure 1 emerges. In sum, downsizing's effect on buying center structure and purchase participant characteristics is expected to be sig- nificantly influenced by the degree of downsizing that has taken place, the severity of implementation tactics used to accomplish downsizing objectives, and/or the negativity of outcome expectations held by surviving organizational members. More specifically, as each of these downsizing characteristics increases in intensity, buying center struc- ture is expected to exhibit greater levels of centralization of authority and greater emphasis on the use of formal rules and procedures but lower levels of participative deci- sion making. Also, purchase participants are expected to experience greater levels of role conflict and greater risk aversion but lower levels of organizational commitment.

Furthermore, these anticipated effects are expected to be moderated by the stage of downsizing the organization is currently in. That is, the influence of degree of downsiz- ing, severity of implementation tactics, and negativity of outcome expectations on buying center structure and pur- chase participant characteristics are expected to be greater within Stage 1 organizations as compared with Stage 2 organizations. The specific hypotheses related to downsiz- ing's effects on organizational buying behavior will now be discussed in more detail.

Downsizing and Purchase Participant Characteristics

Purchase participant characteristics also have been studied extensively in the marketing literature. Of the vari- ous characteristics previously studied, role conflict, risk aversion, and organizational commitment are examined here. 4

Participant Role Conflict

The importance of role conflict has been recognized within the marketing literature (e.g., Barclay 1991; Michaels, Day, and Joachimsthaler 1987). Because pur- chasers must interact frequently with people from other departments and organizations, as well as accommodate requests from a variety of internal and external constitu- ents, they experience role conflict due to often incongru- ous and/or vague expectations (of. Michaels and Dixon

1994; Michaels et al. 1995). According to Brockner, Grover, Reed, DeWitt, and O'Malley (1987), as firms downsize, there is great poten- tial for a type of role conflict known as role overload, 5 as the number of tasks to be accomplished remains constant, but fewer people are available to accomplish these tasks. Simultaneously, to enhance their job security, survivors often feel the need to demonstrate superior performance (Isabella 1989; Robbins 1999). As these expectations place insurmountable demands on surviving employees, they often experience role conflict via role overload. Pur- chase participants in downsizing organizations are partic- ularly susceptible to role overload as they must accom- plish a greater portion of departmental tasks, in addition to the tasks defined by their roles within the buying center.

Furthermore, the extent to which an organization's work activities are defined by formal rules and procedures has been linked to role conflict both conceptually and empirically (e.g., Michaels et al. 1987; Nicholson and Goh

1983). Therefore, if (as expected) there is an initial shift to more mechanistic structures, then the resulting emphasis on the use of formalized rules and procedures is likely to

increase role conflict among buying center participants. However, as Stage 2 organizations adopt more organic structures, role conflict should abate as purchase partici- pants begin to enjoy more job-related autonomy and flexi- bility in decision making.

Participant Risk Aversion

The role of risk and risk handling strategies as significant elements of industrial purchasing has been demon- strated in prior research (e.g., Henthorne, LaTour, and Williams 1993; Puto, Patton, and King 1985). The ten- dency toward risk aversion among buying center partici- pants is a consistent finding across these studies. More recently, Hyman (1995) reports that in downsized firms, purchasers are often pressured to increase productivity, meet restrictive deadlines, and mediate increased conflict between internal customers and external suppliers. As a result of these stressful situations, purchasers become increasingly risk averse. In addition, Wager (1998) found that survivors spend much of their time struggling under the weight of more work and avoiding anything that approaches risk taking. Instead, surviving purchasers develop a nearly compulsive concern with following rules and procedures, become obsessive about risk avoidance, and strive exorbitantly to accommodate all concerned (Hyman 1995; Robbins 1999). Similar findings are reported by Fombrun and Ginsberg (1990) and Hitt, Keats, Harback, and Nixon (1994). Therefore, it seems reason- able to expect that buying center participants involved in an important purchase decision and working in downsiz- ing organizations will become increasingly risk averse.

Participant Organizational Commitment

As a result of downsizing, purchasing decisions are now made under conditions of greater uncertainty and stress. In their study of 330 purchasing professionals, Michaels et al. (1995) found that increased job-related stress (due to factors including downsizing) was positively correlated with decreased organizational commitment among purchasers. These findings are similar to those of Brockner et al. (1987), who found that downsizing initia- tives result in decreased organizational commitment on the part of downsizing survivors. As a result, the organization becomes increasingly vulnerable to defections, further decreasing survivor commitment (Hitt et al. 1994; Porter

1999). Additional empirical support for these decreased levels of organizational commitment can be found in the research of Brockner et al. (1994), Wager (1998), and Luthans and Sommer (1999).

Moreover, motivation theorists posit that individuals work within firms to receive rewards. Several studies pro- vide support for this perspective and its impact on pur- chase participants (e.g., Chambers, Anderson, and Dunlap

1986; McQuiston and Dickson 1991). However, today's downsized firms provide fewer opportunities for extrinsic

rewards (i.e., promotion, advancemenO, and neither their mechanistic structures nor threatening climates are condu- cive to the realization of intrinsic rewards. Therefore, it is unlikely that survivors will be committed to the organiza- tion. The preceding discussions suggest the following:

METHOD

Measure Development and Purification

Seven multi-item measures (initially containing five to seven items each) were used to investigate the perceptual relationships of interest in this study (see the appendix). In addition, one two-item and two single-item measures were used to assess stage of downsizing, degree of downsizing, and implementation tactics used to downsize3 Of the seven multi-item measures, six were adapted from previ- ous empirical studies (i.e., centralization of authority, for- mal rules and procedures, participative decision making, role conflict, organizational commitment, and negative outcome expectations), and one was newly developed (to measure risk aversion). The single, newly developed scale was based on an extensive review of the relevant literature and interviews with academicians and purchasing professionals.

In addition, two pretests were performed to further refine the selected items. First, seven academicians were asked to review the proposed items for clarity and repre- sentativeness based on their knowledge and experience. Second, iterative personal interviews were conducted with 20 purchasing managers employed in a large metropolitan area. Each purchasing professional answered the ques- tionnaire and, as they read the questions and instructions, verbalized any thoughts that came to mind (e.g., ambigu- ity, inapplicability). All elements of the questionnaire's instructions, items, and format were revised based on each personal interview until no further changes were suggested.

Also, as is customary, introductory statements were included in key sections of the questionnaire. For example, the following statement appeared at the beginning of the section focusing on buying center structure: "When answering the following questions please consider a pur- chase decision (1) where the outcome is of considerable importance to your company and (2) that requires the input and involvement of people from several departments or functional units (a buying group) within the company." Similarly, the following statement appeared at the begin- ning of the section focusing on purchase participant char- acteristics: "We are interested in your perceptions or beliefs about the current work environment within your company. When answering the following questions please think of that part of the company for which your depart- ment has buying responsibility." Each of these introduc- tory statements was pretested using the methods discussed in the preceding paragraph.

Prior to hypotheses testing, unidimensionality, reliability, and convergent and discriminant validity were assessed for each individual measure, as well as each group of theoretically related measures (Gerbing and Anderson 1988; J/Sreskog and SiSrbom 1989). Without exception, the models representing the predicted number of constructs were superior to all other models estimated as indicated by their individual goodness-of-fit indices (X 2) and low cross- loadings between constructs (Moorman, Deshpand6, and Zaltman 1993). Procedure

The sampling frame consisted of all members of the National Association of Purchasing Management of Geor- gia (NAPM-GA). Included with their monthly newsletter, each member (513) of NAPM-GA was mailed a survey, along with a cover letter and a self-addressed, prepaid return envelope. A similar second mailing was included with the following month's newsletter. To foster response rates, respondents could choose to be included in a lottery drawing for 1 of 10 cash prizes ranging from $50 to $500. RESULTS

To test the hypotheses, it was necessary to estimate the main effects that the predictor variables degree of down- sizing, severity of implementation tactics used to down- size, and negativity in outcome expectations have on the six criterion variables associated with buying center struc- ture and purchase participant characteristics (e.g., central- ization of authority, role conflict). In addition, it was nee- essary to examine the moderating (interaction) effects between stage of downsizing and the three predictor vari- ables (e.g., degree, severity, negativity) on the six criterion variables.

An appropriate statistical method to estimate these effects is multiple regression analysis. As part of the initial regression analysis, several diagnostic tests, graphs, and scatterplots of the residuals were used to test the assump- tions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity. An examination of the results of these tests and plots suggests that these assumptions were appropriate. Furthermore, an examination of additional diagnostic tests indicated no serious multicollinearity problems.

Therefore, a series of six ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models were estimated (see Table 3). The

independent variables in each of the six regression models were (1) degree of downsizing, (2) severity of implemen- tation tactics, (3) negativity in outcome expectations, (4) stage of downsizing (coded Stage 1 = 1 and Stage 2 = 0), (5) degree of downsizing 9 stage of downsizing, (6) imple- mentation tactics 9 stage of downsizing, and (7) outcome expectations 9 stage of downsizing. The dependent vari- able in each equation was one of the buying group struc- ture or purchase participant characteristic variables (e.g., centralization of authority, role conflict). Findings: Moderating Effect of Stage of Downsizing

Findings related to the moderating effects of stage of downsizing indicate that in those Stage 1 organizations experiencing higher degrees of downsizing, sellers can expect to find increased levels of centralization of author- ity and decreased levels of participative decision making within buying centers. Also, greater centralization of authority within buying centers will be found in those Stage 1 organizations using more severe implementation tactics to accomplish downsizing. In addition, findings indicate that higher degrees of downsizing in Stage 1 orga- nizations also will result in decreased levels of organiza- tional commitment among purchase participants.

Findings: Main Effect of Downsizing Characteristics

Findings related to the main effect of downsizing characteristics (i.e., degree of downsizing, severity of imple- mentation tactics, and negativity of outcome expectations) indicate that both greater degrees of downsizing and greater negativity of outcome expectations will increase levels of role conflict among purchase participants in downsized organizations. Furthermore, greater negativity of outcome expectations also will increase levels of risk aversion and decrease levels of organizational commit- ment among purchase participants. In addition, findings indicate that greater negativity of outcome expectations will increase centralization of authority and decrease participative decision making within the buying centers of downsized organizations.

Managerial Implications

In today's environment, marketers increasingly search for ways to better satisfy customers' needs. To that end, some of the findings reported here may provide insights to help marketers refine their efforts. For example, increased levels of centralization of authority and decreased levels of participative decision making in the buying groups of Stage 1 downsizing firms (especially those experiencing high degrees of downsizing and/or using more severe tac- tics to downsize) suggest that marketing efforts should be directed toward upper-level managers within these buying organizations very early in the purchase process. That is not to say other buying group members should be bypassed. Rather, it emphasizes the importance of not relying solely on efforts to reach front-line buying group participants. In contrast, in Stage 2 firms characterized by less centralization of authority and more participative decision making, early direct targeting of upper-level managers may be inappropriate.

Additional findings providing insight into refining the marketing approach can be found in the increased levels of role conflict and risk aversion found in downsized firms---especially those experiencing greater degrees of downsizing and/or greater negative outcome expectations. First, when purchase professionals are experiencing role overload, key motivators often include the ability to save time and simplify purchasing and inventory management processes. Suppliers can help by offering supplier-man- aged inventory solutions, electronic information-sharing systems, and rapid response strategies to accommodate changing customer demand (e.g., through inventory buff- ers and flexible warehousing, distribution, transportation systems). However, a word of caution is warranted. If downsizing initiatives were cost driven only, marketers need to ensure that these types of added customer services

Second, as levels of role conflict increase, vendors should place greater emphasis on helping purchase partici- pants reduce conflict and reach consensus. One way this might be accomplished is by providing improved support materials to all buying group members (i.e., cost-benefit analyses, user-friendly product specifications, technical information sources, and product demonstrations). In addition, when purchase participants are exceptionally risk averse, as may be the case especially when survivors hold more negative outcome expectations, the importance of price diminishes in favor of risk-reducing features in the offering. Also, highly risk-averse buying group members likely will favor known vendors and proven products. In contrast, buying groups in Stage 2 firms, where intelligent risk taking is encouraged, may be more likely to be recep- tive to adopting new products or technologies.

Research Contributions and Limitations

One contribution of this research is that it provides the first major test of Sutton and D'Aunno's (1989) explana- tion for the divergent predictions and findings offered by the threat-rigidity thesis and classic administration theory. Another contribution of this research is that it builds on Brockner et al.'s (1987, 1994) examination of the effects of organizational downsizing and extends that work into the domain of marketing. The findings reported here regard- ing purchase participants' levels of role conflict, risk aver- sion, and organizational commitment provide additional support for these previous works in a different research context, thus potentially increasing the validity of their findings. This research also adds to the rich body of litera- ture and knowledge focusing on business-to-business mar- keting in general and organizational buying behavior in particular.

Among potential limitations, while the results of the tests used to assess reliability and validity of scales in this study provide some level of confidence, there is a need for further testing using different samples and multiple meth- ods for further verification. Also, the assumption is made that purchasing professionals working within respondent organizations are in a position to accurately assess and report the impact downsizing has had on buying group structure and purchase participant characteristics. The problems surrounding the use of key informant data have been discussed throughout the literature (cf. John and Reve 1982; Phillips 1981). Yet, reliance on key informant data continues to be a widely used methodology.

Finally, a limitation and a contribution of this work lie in the fact that some of the constructs investigated here have not been examined before in the context of organiza- tional buying behavior. Therefore, the results and implica- tions presented must be viewed with a certain amount of reservation.

2. It should be noted that in recent years, several theoretical models have been developed and/or applied to various aspects of organizational downsizing. For example, the concepts of "procedural and distributive justice" have been adapted from the psychology literature and used to

However, while these competing (or, perhaps, complementary) theoretical models provide useful explanatory contributions, none addresses the constructs of interest in this study (i.e., centralization, formalization, participation, role conflict, risk aversion, organizational commitment) as directly as Sutton and D'Aunno's work (Sutton and D'Aunno 1989; D'Aunno and Sutton 1992).

It is also interesting to note that some of these other theoretical models incorporate some of the same explanatory constructs discussed by Sutton and D'Aunno (1989) and examined empirically in the study pres- ent here. These shared constructs include role conflict (i.e., "role over- load"), outcome expectations (i.e., "trust in management"), and implementation tactics (i.e., "perceived justice") as described in Mishra and Spreitzer (1998), as well as implementation tactics (i.e., "organiza- tional caretaking"), organizational commitment (i.e., "organizational commitment"), and Stage 1 to Stage 2 transition (i.e., "likelihood of fu- ture layoffs") as described in Broekner et al. 4. Role conflict--the degree of congruency or compatibility in the requirements of job-related roles relative to a set of standards or conditions that impinge on role performance. Risk aversion---the degree to which purchase participants believe it is best to play it safe and avoid taking risks, especially if they may be penalized if a wrong decision is made. Or- ganizational commitment--the relative strength of an individual's identi- fication with and involvement in his or her organization.

5. Role overload--the extent to which various tasks exceed the amount of time and resources available for their accomplishment.

6. The one two-item and two single-item measures were used to investigate matters of fact rather than perceptual or conceptual constructs. These items included stage of dowusizing, degree of downsizing, and imple- mentation tactics used to downsize. Throughout the sequence of pretest interviews, it was clear that respondents were able to answer these three questions with little hesitation and with great confidence of accuracy.

First, the difference between Stage 1 and Stage 2 organizations was determined by asking respondents the following: (1) during what years did your company engage in the process of downsizing? and (2) do you believe your company will downsize further between now and the year 2000 (which represented a 2-plus year period at the time the surveys were administered)? Third, respondents were asked to identify the predominant method used by their company to accomplish its downsizing goals. ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Jeffrey E. Lewin (Ph.D., Georgia State University) is an assistant professor and Chair, Department of Marketing at Western Carolina University. His research interests include business- to-business marketing, relationship marketing, personal selling and sales management, and organizational buying behavior. His work has been published in the Journal of Business Research, the Journal of Business & Industrial Marketing, Advances in Busi- ness Marketing and Purchasing, and other publications. He serves on the editorial review boards of the Journal of Business Research and the Journal of Business & Industrial Marketing and is a reviewer for the Journal of Business-to-Business Mar- keting, the Journal of Personal Selling and Sales Management, and Industrial Marketing Management.
