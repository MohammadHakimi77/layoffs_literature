Collective Layoffs and Offshoring: A Social Contract Account

ARMIN GRANULO ANNE-MADELEINE KRANZBÜHLER CHRISTOPH FUCHS STEFANO PUNTONI

Collective layoffs can occur for many reasons, often related to a firm’s pursuit of greater efficiency and cost reduction, and they tend to trigger negative reactions among the public. Anecdotal evidence suggests that offshoring, one of the most con troversial and politicized aspects of globalization, evokes particularly negative reac tions. We propose a social contract account of consumer reactions to collective layoffs and demonstrate differential consumer responses to collective layoffs due to offshoring versus other reasons, such as automation. Layoffs due to offshoring are perceived as an especially egregious violation of the normative expectation that firms should support members of their society. Data from nine experimental studies (N¼ 5,568; seven reported in the main paper and two in the General Discussion section), public con sumer responses to layoffs in a large online community (N¼ 29,045), and layoff announcements in the European Union (N¼ 1,261) show that consumers react more negatively to collective layoffs due to offshoring compared to other reasons. Supporting our social contract account, the negative effect of offshoring is stronger when offshoring affects workers in the consumers’ home (vs. foreign) country, when the firm is domestic (vs. foreign), and when most customers are domestic (vs. foreign).

Keywords: layoffs, social contracts, social contract violation, offshoring, interna tional marketing

Despite the practical and societal importance of this phe nomenon, it remains unclear what drives consumer reac tions to collective layoffs and whether such reactions differ systematically as a function of the efficiency-enhancing strategy adopted by firms (e.g., offshoring or other forms such as automation). Prior research has thus far provided initial evidence that consumers react negatively to offshor ing (Grappi, Romani, and Bagozzi 2013; Thelen, Yoo, and Magnini 2011; Whitaker, Krishnan, and Fornell 2006). For example, prior research has investigated consumer percep tions of service quality when firms offshore versus do not offshore their customer service (Whitaker et al. 2006), and how consumers react when firms offshore versus do not offshore production (Grappi et al. 2013). However, these studies were not designed to disentangle negative consumer responses to offshoring from negative consumer responses to layoffs in general (for a systematic literature review, see table A1 in web appendix A). What makes moving domes tic work abroad psychologically different from other col lective layoff reasons, such as automating or outsourcing work, even if they involve comparable reductions of the domestic labor force? And if consumers indeed react more negatively to offshoring than to other layoff decisions, how do these differential reactions vary depending on firm and consumer characteristics?

We seek to address these gaps by (i) proposing and test ing a comprehensive theoretical account of psychological reactions to collective layoffs; (ii) documenting negative consumer reactions to layoffs resulting from offshoring versus other reasons, both in real-world settings and con trolled experiments holding constant the number of domes tic job losses; and (iii) demonstrating that our findings extend beyond, and are poorly explained by, previously proposed explanations, such as ethnocentrism, patriotism, or product quality perceptions. Specifically, we theorize that consumer reactions to collective layoffs can be under stood within a framework of social contracts. Social con tracts delineate implicit and reciprocal (“give-and-take”) relationships between firms and members of their society, such as consumers or workers (Donaldson 1982; Dunfee, Smith, and Ross 1999). We propose that consumers react more negatively to decisions about offshoring compared to other firm decisions that involve collective layoffs, such as automation. This is because offshoring uniquely breaches a key element of social contracts: the normative expectation that firms should support members of their society by pro moting and committing to local production. Our theory identifies important moderators of negative consumer reac tions to offshoring, including the location of (i) the collec tive layoffs, (ii) the firm’s headquarters (HQ), and (iii) the firm’s customer base. To test our predictions, we employ a

multi-method approach, incorporating both field and labo ratory data. Our studies compare the effect of offshoring against several other forms of collective layoffs, including automation, outsourcing, relocation, and declining demand.

Our research makes three main contributions. First, we contribute to the vast literature in marketing, management, finance, and other disciplines on the phenomenon of off shoring (Bretscher 2023; Farrell 2005; Thelen et al. 2011) by unraveling the psychology of offshoring and its conse quences for consumer attitudes and behavior. Our social contract framework provides a more comprehensive con ceptualization of consumer responses to collective layoffs than previously proposed accounts and provides novel insights particularly relevant to the international marketing literature. For example, our framework predicts that in the context of offshoring, consumers perceive domestic firms more negatively than foreign firms, which represents a reversal of the well-documented tendency for consumers to favor (products from) domestic over foreign firms (Sharma 2011; Verlegh 2007). Second, our research advances the literature on social contracts in marketing, management, and business ethics (Malhotra, Sahadev, and Purani 2017; Montgomery et al. 2018; Neiman 2013). We show that social contract theory can serve as a fruitful framework to explain consumer reac tions to firm actions that affect members of their society (e.g., workers). We also provide new insights into the for mation of social contracts, an understudied aspect in social contract theory (Thompson and Hart 2006) and in market ing research (Montgomery et al. 2018). These new insights may inform phenomena beyond offshoring; for instance, consumer reactions to brand transgressions may depend on antecedents on both the firm level (e.g., the location of the firm’s HQ) and the community level (e.g., the location of the firm’s customer base).

Third, our research contributes to the field of lay eco nomics, which studies how lay people think about the workings of the economy (Bhattacharjee, Dana, and Baron 2017; Boyer and Petersen 2017). Specifically, our work sheds light on how consumers perceive the causes of col lective layoffs, often related to a firm’s pursuit of greater efficiency and cost reduction. Understanding these percep tions is critical, as they shape political beliefs and policy responses (Boyer and Petersen 2017), yet this area remains underexplored in lay economics. For instance, two primary forces shaping this quest for efficiency are globalization and technology, of which offshoring and automation are the most visible manifestations. Our social contract account offers a deeper understanding of why consumers react dif ferently to layoffs caused by offshoring versus automation. Consumers tend to view offshoring as a more severe breach of the reciprocal relationship inherent in the social contract, perceiving it as less legitimate behavior than automation, even when the impact on local employment is similar. CONCEPTUAL DEVELOPMENT

Collective layoffs are a widespread and significant organizational practice (Datta et al. 2010). In response to intense competitive pressure, firms often rely on collective layoffs as a strategy to boost efficiency and cut costs. To achieve these goals, firms may adopt various strategies. For instance, firms can reduce their production costs by outsourcing labor to specialized firms within the same country (Goldschmidt and Schmieder 2017) or by replacing human labor with machines (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020). A major efficiency-enhancing strategy that has received considerable attention from both academics and practi tioners is offshoring (Contractor et al. 2010)—moving domestic labor abroad (Brown and Siegel 2004). A large body of research has documented positive effects of off shoring on individual firms; for example, offshoring can help firms to become more efficient (Farrell 2005), more innovative (Nieto and Rodrıguez 2011), and more globally competitive (Di Gregorio, Musteen, and Thomas 2009). Moreover, offshoring can help mitigate global inequality by offering better earning opportunities to workers in developing countries (Rodrik 2018).

At the same time, research in services marketing sug gests that firms may encounter negative consumer reactions when they offshore their customer service to another coun try (Bharadwaj and Roggeveen 2008; Durvasula and Lysonski 2008; Thelen et al. 2011). For example, consum ers may perceive call centers less positively when they are offshored to a foreign country than when they are located domestically because they are concerned about a decline in service quality (Bharadwaj and Roggeveen 2008). Consumers may oppose service offshoring because of free trade resentment and foreign worker enmity (Thelen et al. 2011) or because they consider it unpatriotic (Durvasula and Lysonski 2008). Most relevant to the present investiga tion, Grappi et al. (2013) examined how firms’ decisions to offshore versus maintain corporate activities at home affect consumer attitudes toward firms. They find that laying off domestic workers to offshore jobs abroad makes firms be seen more negatively and as less moral (vs. firms that retain these functions in their home country). However, this line of research did not separate the effect of offshoring on firm perceptions from the general negativity associated with collective layoffs (St€abler et al. 2023; see table A1 in web appendix A). This separation is crucial because it remains uncertain whether consumer reactions would be equally negative in response to other reasons for collective layoffs, such as automation. To address this question, we introduce a new theoretical framework centering on social contract violations.

Social Contract Theory

Society operates not only on the basis of legal contracts but also of social contracts, which are implicit promises and agreements that parties are expected to adhere to (Donaldson 1982; Rawls 2009; Rousseau 1997). In contrast to legal contracts, social contracts are not enforceable or legally binding. Yet, this does not diminish their influence. In political philosophy, the concept of a social contract is a pivotal perspective for describing mutual expectations between people and authorities within a society (Hobbes 1998; Rawls 2009; Rousseau 1997). In a business context, the idea of social contracts has been applied to understand the reciprocal, normative expectations between firms and the society (Donaldson 1982; Dunfee et al. 1999). Social contracts can be construed at different hierarchical levels. At the broadest level, social contracts in a business setting may occur between business in an abstract, collective sense and the society in which they are embedded, encompassing the normative idea that firms in general have the right to operate in exchange for contributing positively to societal welfare (Donaldson 1982). At the industry level, social contracts may occur between specific industries and soci ety, with reciprocal expectations shaped by the industry’s role (Dunfee et al. 1999). For example, the healthcare industry faces unique ethical expectations due to its critical role in promoting public health (Leisinger 2005). Finally— and most relevant to our theorizing—social contracts may occur at the individual firm level. At this level, social con tracts describe reciprocal, normative obligations between individual firms and society. Specifically, we propose that two pillars of social contracts at the firm level—their soci etal dimension and their reciprocal dimension—provide a framework for understanding why and when consumers perceive collective layoffs due to offshoring (vs. other rea sons) to be a more severe social contract violation.

Reciprocity. Social contracts operate on the basis of reciprocity (Donaldson 1982; Dunfee et al. 1999)—sup porting others in return for the support received (Gouldner 1960; Molm 2010)—which is a cornerstone of human civi lization. Reciprocity influences how individuals interact with each other and sets expectations for them (Falk and Fischbacher 2006; Molm 2010; Trivers 1971), as well as how firms engage with their stakeholders. It is crucial in many economic settings, such as setting fair wages (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler 1986). In the context of social contracts, the principle of reciprocity implies an implicit and mutual give-and-take obligation between firms and members of a given society, such as consumers. Unlike norms, such as injunctive or descriptive social norms (Cialdini, Reno, and Kallgren 1990), social contracts not only specify normative expectations of what society should do for firms or what firms should do for society, but also specify give-and-take relationship dynamics. Society. While social contracts involve reciprocal rela tionships, these relationships are not without boundaries. As many scholars have noted, social contracts have a natu ral boundary: they are generally understood to operate within the bounds of a particular society. For example, Donaldson (1982) defined a social contract in a business context as an implicit contract between firms and members of a given society (vs. a supra-national entity). In the mod ern world, society is typically defined at the level of the nation-state (Donaldson 1982; Harari 2014; Hsieh 2015; Rawls 2009). And, indeed, consumers perceive social con tracts to exist within countries, that is, they tend to be national in scope (Mead et al. 2024). Thus, even though the concept of society can take many forms, such as linguistic or ethnic communities, it is often linked with a common nationality (Anderson 2006; Gellner 2008). Extensive research demonstrates that feelings of national attachment are deeply embedded in people’s belief systems (Dalton 2021), significantly shape people’s self-concept (Huddy and Khatib 2007), and influence consumer behavior (Verlegh 2007). Accordingly, people tend to be more attached to their country, such as France, than their broader supra-national entities, such as the European Union, even if they might feel devoted to both (Dalton 2021). In line with this, we argue that the reciprocal obligations emanating from the social contract are, to an important extent, valid within the national context.

The concept of society within social contracts can encompass different constituents (Donaldson 1982; Hsieh 2015). Society typically refers to “all of us citizens” but can also encompass specific subgroups, such as workers of a particular firm (Dunfee et al. 1999). In this research, we focus on how citizens (consumers) respond to firms’ col lective layoff decisions, which harm a specific constituent of society (workers). We argue that such decisions—partic ularly when driven by offshoring—can lead consumers, even when not directly affected by the layoffs, to perceive a violation of the social contract. For instance, when con sumers learn that a firm has laid off domestic workers to relocate production overseas, they may develop more nega tive attitudes toward the firm, even if they have no direct connection to the affected workers. This, in turn, can reduce buying intentions and amplify consumer boycott intentions (Klein, Smith, and John 2004).

Collective Layoffs and Social Contract Violations

To fulfill their reciprocal obligations emanating from the social contract, firms are expected to create and maintain employment opportunities for members of their society

(Donaldson 1982; Hsieh 2015). We argue that collective layoffs due to offshoring represent a particularly strong violation of this reciprocal obligation. All collective layoffs reduce domestic employment opportunities and thus repre sent a violation of the social contract. However, offshoring-related layoffs not only reduce domestic employment opportunities but also move these opportuni ties to a foreign country, replacing jobs for local workers with jobs for foreign workers (e.g., replacing American workers with non-American workers). By offshoring, firms thus not only fail to uphold their end of the give-and-take relationship with members of their domestic society (pro viding domestic employment opportunities) but also “give” employment opportunities to members of a foreign society. Extensive research in related domains documents that such decisions—that is, benefitting another group at the expense of one’s own group—can evoke intense negative emotional reactions (Esses et al. 2001; King, Knight, and Hebl 2010; Shackelford and Buss 1996; Stefaniak, Mallett, and Wohl 2020). For instance, research on interpersonal relationships shows that giving expected benefits to parties outside of the focal relationship can evoke intense feelings of betrayal (Shackelford and Buss 1996). Research has also shown that individuals react negatively when others fail to fulfill their reciprocal obligations to their societal group. For instance, people are willing to punish those who do not reciprocate toward their group, even at a personal cost and even if they have not been directly harmed (Falk and Fischbacher 2006; Molm 2010).

Against this background, we argue that a decision that harms members of the domestic society and, at the same time, benefits members of a foreign society should lead to strong perceptions that a firm betrayed the reciprocal rela tionship emanating from the social contract. In contrast, other forms of collective layoffs, such as outsourcing or automation, should not have the same effect. The benefit for overseas workers at the cost of domestic workers should lead consumers to perceive offshoring as a particularly egregious violation of acceptable corporate behavior, resulting in more negative reactions toward firms that off shore. For example, consumer attitudes toward firms, which are a critical predictor of consumer boycotts (Klein, Smith, and John 2004), sales (Fazio and Petty 2008), and stock returns (Aaker and Jacobson 2001), should decrease more sharply in response to offshoring compared to other types of collective layoffs. H1: Consumer reactions to collective layoff announcements are more negative in the case of offshoring, compared to other reasons for collective layoffs.

Below, we theorize three such factors affecting the intensity of negative reactions to offshoring to assess the explanatory power of our social contract account. These factors, which center around the concept of “location,” are of both theoretical and manage rial relevance.

Location of the Collective Layoffs. Given that social contracts are conceptualized at the societal level (Donaldson 1982; Mead et al. 2024; Nussbaum 2004; Rawls 2009), consumers should perceive a stronger social contract with members of their own society (e.g., domestic workers) than with those of a different one. Consumers should thus perceive layoffs affecting overseas workers as a weaker violation of their social contract. We, therefore, argue that the extent to which a firm’s decision is perceived as a social contract violation should depend on whether it affects workers who are members of the same society (e.g., domestic workers) versus of a foreign society (e.g., foreign workers). Formally,

H2: Consumer reactions to collective layoffs due to offshor ing are more negative when the layoff location is domestic versus foreign.

Location of the Firm’s HQ. A large body of research documents that a firm’s country of origin, typically deter mined by the location of its HQ (Johansson, Douglas, and Nonaka 1985; Samiee, Shimp, and Sharma 2005), has important economic effects. For example, consumers prefer domestic products over foreign ones (Verlegh 2007); investors favor domestic assets over foreign ones (Chan, Covrig, and Ng 2005); and the share of domestic versus international trade of goods and services is much larger than what normative trade models would predict (Wolf 2000). Based on our social contract account, however, we posit that this positive bias toward firms from consumers’ home country (Sharma 2011; Verlegh 2007) should reverse in the context of collective layoffs due to offshoring. The conceptualization of social contracts as implicit, reciprocal agreements and expectations between consumers and firms within a given society not only implies that both the recip rocal and societal dimensions are central to these con tracts—but also that these two aspects are interrelated.

Specifically, the strength of the give-and-take relation ship (reciprocal dimension) should depend on whether the individual firm is a member of the same society as the con sumers (societal dimension). For example, US consumers should be more likely to regard US firms, that is, firms with corporate HQ in the United States, as members of the same society than foreign firms, that is, firms with HQ out side the United States. Consumers should believe that a firm from their society (i.e., a domestic firm) has greater

reciprocal obligations than firms from other societies (i.e., a foreign firm). This reasoning aligns with extensive litera ture showing that people’s reciprocal expectations depend on whether they share a common identity with others (e.g., a shared national identity; Balliet, Wu, and De Dreu 2014; Yamagishi and Kiyonari 2000). As a result, we theorize that consumers hold domestic firms to a higher standard than foreign firms when they violate the reciprocal obliga tion to support members of their domestic society; that is, consumers should react particularly negatively when a domestic (vs. foreign) firm lays off workers and relocates production to another country. Formally,

H3: Consumer reactions to collective layoffs due to offshor ing are more negative when the location of the firm’s head quarters is domestic versus foreign.

Location of the Firm’s Customer Base. Social contracts set expectations about how firms and members of their society should interact. Firms should support domestic workers, for example, by creating domestic jobs. In exchange, local consumers should also support domestic firms. The most common and easiest way to support a firm is to buy its goods or services (Guo, Gruen, and Tang 2017). If members of a society fail to fulfill this expecta tion, consumers should perceive the resulting social con tract as weaker. We therefore argue that firms’ perceived obligations emanating from the social contract should depend on the location of their customer base. If the major ity of customers are located outside of the focal country, the firm’s perceived social contract responsibilities should be less stringent. As a result, such a situation should be seen as a weaker violation of the social contract’s idea of reciprocity (Rousseau 1989), and consumers should regard offshoring as a less egregious social contract violation. Formally,

H4: Consumer reactions to collective layoffs due to offshor ing are more negative when most customers are domestic versus foreign.

STUDY 1: CONSEQUENTIAL CHOICE

We start our empirical investigation with a series of studies examining whether consumer reactions are more negative when collective layoffs are related to offshoring as opposed to other causes (hypothesis 1). We first present evidence for this prediction from two controlled experi ments (studies 1 and 2). Subsequently, we offer comple mentary real-world evidence that aligns with our experimental findings (studies 3A and 3B), thus providing further suggestive evidence for the robustness and general izability of our main effect prediction.

Study 1 is a consequential choice experiment to test whether consumers choose a brand less when it lays off workers due to offshoring versus automation. Offshoring and automation, which both can replace domestic labor, are among the most important strategies for increasing opera tional efficiency (Rodrik 2018), can affect workers with similar skill sets, and are frequently in direct competition with one another (with automation becoming increasingly important; Frey 2019). The study was preregistered (https://aspredicted.org/c4kz-vhym.pdf).

Method

We recruited 807 US consumers (Mage ¼ 41 years, 54% females) via the online panel Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) who were randomly assigned to one of two condi tions using a consequential choice design (for similar designs, see Meyvis and Van Osselaer 2018). Participants were introduced to two US brands that manufacture and sell socks and were told that they could win a $25 voucher for the brand they preferred. Participants were exposed to both brands—Feat. Sock Co. and New Socks—side by side and were provided with exemplary pictures of products and information about each brand (the socks displayed were real products; web appendix B). We manipulated between participants whether one of the brands (i.e., Feat. Sock Co.) laid off workers due to offshoring versus automation, while the other brand (i.e., New Socks) served as a control brand.

In the offshoring condition, participants read that the tar get (i.e., manipulated) brand had recently decided to move its production from the United States and now relied on foreign workers. Participants in the automation condition read instead that the same brand had recently decided to keep its factory in the United States and now relied on machines. In both conditions, participants were informed that the brand had laid off 475 workers in their US plant. To describe the control brand, participants read that the brand recently decided to change its customer service and now offered self-service options to resolve customer requests. We randomized whether the offshoring (vs. auto mation) brand or the control brand was presented first. We then measured product choice for the offshoring (vs. auto mation) brand versus the control brand, which served as our dependent variable. Upon study completion, we con ducted a lottery, awarding an online shopping voucher to a randomly determined winner. After data collection was completed, the winning participant received the voucher, and all participants were debriefed.

Results

When the target brand’s layoffs were due to offshoring, only 11.7% of the participants chose it (88.3% chose the control brand). In contrast, when the layoffs were due to automation, 26.2% of the participants chose its product (73.8% chose the control brand; χ2(1) ¼ 27.89, p < .001). Thus, as preregistered, information that collective layoffs were due to offshoring (vs. automation) resulted in a signif icant 14.5% decrease in choice share (Z ¼ −5.28, p < .001). A logistic regression with choice as the dependent variable, experimental condition as the independent varia ble, and order of brand presentation, gender, age, and study completion time as covariates produced consistent results; that is, collective layoffs due to offshoring (vs. automation) significantly reduced the participants’ propensity to choose the brand (b ¼ −1.030, p < .001, CI95% [−1.41, −0.65]). To the best of our knowledge, study 1 thus provides the first (causal) evidence for the negative effect of offshoring on consumer behavior (hypothesis 1).

In sum, the results of study 2 provide con verging evidence for the distinct effect of offshoring on consumer attitudes.

STUDIES 3A AND 3B: COMPLEMENTARY

FIELD EVIDENCE

Studies 3A and 3B aim to complement studies 1 and 2 by offering corroborating field evidence for our prediction that collective layoffs elicit more negative responses when they are linked to offshoring (vs. other causes; hypothesis 1). In both studies, we rely on secondary (correlational) data from different sources—user-generated content and press releases—and analyze the sentiments in these data as a proxy variable for consumers’ reactions to offshoring using a sentiment analysis algorithm. This type of senti ment analysis method has been identified as a valid method to extract consumer attitudes from digital content (Schmitt 2019).

For study 3A, we collected data on public consumer responses to layoffs from Reddit, one of the world’s largest and most active online communities (Lebrous 2017). Study 3B

In study 3B, we tested the hypothesized negative effect of layoffs resulting from offshoring using a similar approach as in study 3A but a different data source. Consistent with studies in communications research, we use media coverage as a proxy for consumer sentiment (Erikson and Tedin 2015; Hopkins, Kim, and Kim 2017). Previous research has demonstrated that media coverage, to an important extent, reflects public opinion (Haller and Norpoth 1997; Soroka, Stecula, and Wlezien 2015). For instance, Hopkins et al. (2017) suggest that newspaper cov erage reflects consumer sentiment about the economy.

countries from 2002 to 2018, comprising a total of 23,189 announcements (collective layoffs, according to the EMCC, involve the elimination of a minimum of 100 jobs, or impacting 10% of the workforce in sites with more than 250 people). These textual summaries are generated by Eurofound correspondents and are based on the content from a broad range of daily press and online sources (for a concrete example, see web appendix D).

To obtain data on collective layoffs involving firms either relocating production to another country or retaining production within the same country, we selected cases from this dataset that were attributed to (i) offshoring (i.e., layoffs where production is relocated to another country; N ¼ 881), (ii) outsourcing (i.e., layoffs where production is subcontracted to another company but remains in the same country; N ¼ 102), and (iii) relocation (i.e., layoffs where production remains in the same company and the same country; N ¼ 278). The sentiment score indicates how negative or positive the sentiment of the media coverage of each lay off case was (ranging from 1 to þ1) and served as our dependent variable.

STUDY 4: LOCATION OF THE LAYOFFS

Study 4 tests the prediction that consumer reactions to offshoring are moderated by the layoff location (hypothesis 2). If the negative effect of offshoring is driven by social contract violations, then it should be less pronounced (or disappear) when perceptions of the social contract are weak (or when there is no social contract to violate). Based on our social contract account, we argue that when a firm is offshoring production from one foreign country to another, perceptions of social contract violations should be weaker, as the decision has little to no relevance or ramifi cations for members of one’s society. Our theory, there fore, predicts that the negative effect of offshoring should be diminished in this case.

This study further aims to rule out another explanation proposed by previous literature: product quality (see table A1 in web appendix A). According to this account, con sumers view firms that offshore more negatively because product quality suffers whenever production moves from a developed economy to a developing one, regardless of whether the developed economy is the participants’ home country. Thus, as long as products from the two countries are perceived to be of similar quality, a product quality explanation argues that consumers should evaluate a firm similarly regardless of whether it offshores (vs. automates) production in their home country or in a foreign country. In both cases, offshoring (vs. automation) should be associ ated with a stronger decrease in perceived product quality. However, if our theory is correct and reactions to offshor ing are primarily driven by perceived violations of a social contract between firms and members of their society, then consumers should hold a more negative view of a firm that decides to offshore (vs. automate) production in their home country (affecting members of their own society) rather than in a foreign country (affecting members of another society).

STUDY 6: LOCATION OF THE FIRM’S

CUSTOMER BASE

In study 6, we focus our attention on offshoring only and investigate whether consumers’ reactions to offshoring depend on the location of the customer base (hypothesis 4). Social contracts define implicit give-and-take relationships between firms and members of a given society, such as consumers. Consumers should therefore perceive firms to

violate the social contract more strongly when the majority of their customers is located in the firm’s home country rather than in a foreign country. This is because, in the for mer case, consumers “fulfill” their part of the give-and- take relationship. We again also test whether ethnocentrism and patriotism could serve as alternative explanations. The study was preregistered (https://aspredicted.org/p98g-y8p2. pdf).

Method

We recruited 763 UK consumers (Mage ¼ 41 years, 64% female; Prolific) and randomly assigned them to one of three conditions (customer location: foreign vs. domestic vs. control) of an experiment using a between-participants design. All participants were introduced to the same UK- based firm as in study 2. In all conditions, participants read that the firm had decided to offshore jobs abroad and lay off 4,000 workers. Moreover, we added a short description of the customer base of the firm, which was our manipula tion. In the foreign customer location condition, partici pants read that more than 90% of the firm’s customers were not from the United Kingdom (i.e., not from the home country of the participants and the firm described). In the domestic customer location condition, participants read that more than 90% of the firm’s customers were from the United Kingdom. In the control condition, participants instead received no information about the firm’s customer base. We then measured participants’ attitude toward the firm (α ¼ 0.97), the extent to which participants perceived the collective layoffs as a social contract violation (α ¼ 0.91), and participants’ ethnocentrism (α ¼ 0.83) and patriotism (α ¼ 0.83) as before.

GENERAL DISCUSSION

Collective layoffs are a widespread phenomenon in Western societies and are often the result of firms’ efforts to reduce costs and increase efficiency. Such events tend to receive prominent media coverage and can have serious negative repercussions on how individuals think and feel about companies (Landsman and Stremersch 2020). However, prior consumer research has not provided a com prehensive account of consumer reactions to collective lay off decisions. One widely used strategy to increase efficiency is offshoring—laying off local workers and moving the production abroad—a practice that has sparked heated and controversial debates in the media and political arenas. In this research, we propose a theory based on social contract violations and examine why consumers are seemingly reacting more negatively to offshoring com pared to other efficiency-enhancing strategies that entail collective layoffs.

Across seven experimental studies on consumer responses (N ¼ 4,761), analyses of user-generated content on Reddit (N ¼ 29,045), and media reports on European collective layoffs (N ¼ 1,261), we consistently observe more negative responses when firms laid off workers due to offshoring than due to other efficiency-driven reasons such as automation, aligning with our first hypothesis.

Our experimental studies provide evidence for the role of perceived social contract violations in explaining our findings, utilizing mediation, moderation, and moderated mediation approaches. As social contracts imply a recipro cal relationship between firms and members of their soci ety (e.g., consumers), we theorized that both firm-related and consumer-related factors should affect consumer per ceptions of social contract violations. In line with this rea soning, we find that the negative effect of offshoring is stronger when offshoring affects workers in the consumers’ home (vs. foreign) country (study 4; hypothesis 2), when the offshoring firm is domestic (vs. foreign; studies 5 and 5B; hypothesis 3), and when most customers are domestic (vs. foreign; studies 6 and 6B, hypothesis 4). Below, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the studies, as well as highlight limitations and future research opportunities.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

First, our study introduces a framework for understand ing consumer responses to offshoring—one of the most controversial and politicized aspects of globalization (Owen 2017). Our theory hinges on the idea that when a firm lays off workers and moves production to a foreign country, it breaches a fundamental element of the give- and-take relationship inherent in the social contract: the normative expectation that firms (should) support members of their society. Our account allows predictions that extend beyond existing theories; it also explains greater variance in consumer attitudes toward offshoring firms than other accounts based, for example, on ethnocentrism or product quality. As such, it provides a comprehensive theory to enhance our understanding of the factors affecting con sumer responses to collective layoffs and elucidates why and when consumers react more negatively to offshoring than to other efficiency-enhancing strategies. In addition, our research delineates the unique impact of offshoring, distinguishing it from the general effects of layoffs—a dis tinction not allowed by the design of previous research (Grappi et al. 2013).

Second, our research advances the literature on social contracts by shedding light on the formation and violation of social contracts between firms and consumers. In doing so, this work advances existing research in marketing (Malhotra et al. 2017; Montgomery et al. 2018), which has mainly focused on how consumers react to instances where their psychological contracts with brands are violated (e.g., when brands unexpectedly increase prices). Our research

suggests that the country of origin of a firm and of a firm’s customer base are important elements in the formation of social contracts, an understudied aspect in social contract theory (Thompson and Hart 2006). These new insights may in turn inform phenomena beyond offshoring. For instance, consumer responses to brand transgressions may depend on firm-level factors that strengthen social contract percep tions, such as a firm’s historical ties to a particular society.

Third, our research contributes to the field of lay eco nomics (Bhattacharjee et al. 2017; Boyer and Petersen 2017) by shedding light on how laypeople perceive the causes of collective layoffs. While these perceptions shape political beliefs and policy reactions, they remain currently a neglected topic. For example, prior literature shows that people react more negatively to globalization than to tech nological change, of which offshoring and automation are the most visible manifestations, even though the technolog ical change has a more significant impact on labor markets (Rodrik 2018). This disparity has been linked with country- specific attitudes (e.g., patriotism or ethnocentrism). Our social contract account offers a deeper understanding of why consumers react differently to layoffs caused by off shoring versus automation and when these effects materialize.

Practically, our work also informs the managerial dis course on the consequences of firms’ collective layoff deci sions. Prior research has mainly focused on the general effects of collective layoffs on sales and marketing mix elasticities (Landsman and Stremersch 2020) and firm rep utation (Love and Kraatz 2009). Our research advances this work by highlighting the unique consumer responses to off shoring, indicating that managers should view offshoring through a different lens than other forms of collective lay offs. Given that collective layoff decisions frequently appear in the media (study 3B), our findings imply that managers should not only consider supply-side effects of offshoring (Kotabe and Mudambi 2009) but also poten tially negative demand-side effects.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our research has several limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, the field data in studies 3A and 3B, which cover a wide range of online consumer responses and media coverage, rely on correlational analy ses, limiting the ability to draw causal inferences. In all other studies, we tested the effect of offshoring experimen tally, using both consequential and attitudinal measures. Future research could replicate these findings from highly controlled environments in real-world settings and assess whether consumer reactions also influence downstream behaviors, such as consumer boycotts (Klein et al. 2004).

Also, we did not differentiate firm motivations for offshoring (e.g., cost reduction or access to talent; Grappi, Romani, and Bagozzi 2015). If some motivations are per ceived as a less egregious violation of the social contract, offshoring firms would have an incentive to ensure the pub lic is aware of it. Moreover, studies 5 and 5B manipulated the firm’s country of origin by manipulating the firm’s HQ location. While this connection is well-grounded in the existing literature (Johansson, Douglas, and Nonaka 1985; Samiee, Shimp, and Sharma 2005), there are instances where it may be weaker (e.g., when the HQ location is related to tax considerations) or where it may be stronger (e.g., when the HQ location is related to historical factors); this may, in turn, lead to weaker or stronger reciprocity expectations emanating from the social contract. Future research could systematically explore these differences.

Third, our work did not investigate how perceptions of social contract violations depend on the economic rationale behind a firm’s offshoring decision. Based on our social contract account, however, it is conceivable that consumers react less negatively to offshoring when they perceive it as a necessary response to an unfavorable business environ ment (e.g., demand-side changes) or external market pres sures, making the perceived violation of the social contract more acceptable. Additionally, future studies could exam ine whether firms can mitigate the breach of the social con tract caused by offshoring. Our framework suggests that offering various forms of compensation to society may help reduce perceptions of social contract violations (e.g., investing in community development) and thus attenuate the negative impact of offshoring.

Fourth, we derived our hypotheses from the concept of social contracts at the firm level, which allowed us to iden tify key moderators of the negative effect of offshoring, including firm characteristics (e.g., the firm’s HQ location) and consumer characteristics (e.g., the firm’s customer base location). Future studies could build on our findings by examining the impact of social contract violations at dif ferent hierarchical levels. For instance, future research might investigate whether consumers respond differently when a social contract is violated by an entire industry compared to a single firm (e.g., if most or all firms in an industry decide to offshore production). Additionally, future work may investigate whether perceived social con tract violations vary by industry type (e.g., manufacturing vs. healthcare) or workforce type (e.g., blue-collar vs. white-collar). Exploring these questions could provide a

more nuanced understanding of the perceptions of social contract violations.

Fifth, future research may explore perceived social con tract violations arising from automation. Unlike offshoring, automation does not provide employment opportunities to a foreign society at the expense of the domestic society. Consequently, consumers may initially view automation as less of a social contract violation, as the social contract has traditionally been understood as an agreement among human members within a specific society or nation-state (Donaldson 1982; Hsieh 2015; Rawls 2009). However, advances in robotics increasingly enable firms to use machines with humanlike features and behaviors, allowing them to replace human workers across various industries (Kim, Schmitt, and Thalmann 2019; Schmitt 2019; van Doorn et al. 2017). As robots become more humanlike, they may blur the boundaries of what is considered part of the social contract (Duffy 2003). For instance, the use of humanlike machines could trigger negative social compari son processes where humans may perceive themselves as being collectively displaced or devalued by non-human entities (Granulo, Fuchs, and Puntoni 2019). Accordingly, consumers may perceive the use of automation as affecting humanity’s collective social contract. We thus encourage future research to investigate whether implicit assumptions about the social contract are indeed human-centered and to explore how automation (e.g., robotics) may expand or challenge this framework.

Sixth, our studies did not examine how specific charac teristics of the country to which production is offshored affect consumer reactions, which offers opportunities for future research. Related to this point, one could argue that the observed stronger negative reactions toward domestic (vs. foreign) offshoring firms could be influenced by the belief that foreign firms simply “return” jobs to their home countries. To address this possibility, we conducted an additional study (N ¼ 401) in which we explicitly named the country to which a firm offshores (see web appendix E for details). This study produced consistent results (interac tion effect: p < .001). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the offshoring destination might moderate the effect of off shoring based on variables such as the similarity of the countries, the relative level of economic development, and specific historical factors (e.g., diplomatic relations). We therefore encourage future research to explore contingen cies in consumer reactions to offshoring decisions based on contextual variables like these. In a similar vein, and more fundamentally, it is possible that socioeconomic and cul tural differences across countries might systematically affect how consumers perceive social contracts and their formation, an important topic for future research.
