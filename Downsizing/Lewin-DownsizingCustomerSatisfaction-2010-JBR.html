Keywords: Value Satisfaction Loyalty Downsizing Cultural differences

This study examines the impact that downsizing of suppliers' sales and support personnel has on business customers' satisfaction. The study investigates what influence cultural differences may have on business customers' evaluations of satisfaction with and loyalty toward downsized suppliers. Survey data collected from 435 purchasing professionals in the United States, France, and The Netherlands provide answers to these research questions. Findings indicate that business customers are not satisfied with downsized suppliers' performance. However the results relating to cultural influence are mixed. This report suggests possibilities for future research efforts in this area.

1. Introduction

Customer satisfaction is an important source of competitive advantage (Rust et al., 2000), often leading to customer loyalty and repeat purchase (Bolton, 1998; Lam et al., 2004). Customer satisfaction significantly influences an organization's current and future performance (Anderson et al., 1994). Research in many free market economies throughout the world support this proposition (e.g., Liang and Wang, 2004; Wiele et al., 2002). Historically, the literature views product quality as the primary driver of customer satisfaction. However, in today's global economy, customers often perceive product quality among competitors as similar. As a result, customer satisfaction relates increasingly to the quality of the ancillary services that attach to a company's overall offering (Bolton et al., 2003; Wilson et al., 2001). Within this context, positive interactions with a firm's sales and customer service-support personnel are an important source of customer satisfaction (Ennew and Ahmed 1999; Gwinner et al., 1998; Heskett et al., 1994).

Thus, when a supplier eliminates sales and support personnel, as is often the case in downsized firms (Markels and Murray, 1996), the relationships between the supplier and its customers become vulnerable to customer defections (Bendapudi and Leone, 2002). Interestingly, the important relationship between customer satisfac-

tion and consistency in sales and support personnel is seemingly overlooked by organizations engaging in downsizing activities. Instead, these organizations focus on potential reductions in operating expenses, and expected improvements in market responsiveness. However, often unexpected costs are associated with downsizing. Among these costs is the inability to fully satisfy customer needs and the concomitant reduction in customer satisfaction (Fornell et al., 1996). While limited research, coupled with anecdotal accounts, provide some insight into the relationship between organizational downsizing and customer satisfaction in business-to-consumer markets (e.g., Bryant, 1997; Fornell et al., 1996), to date few studies have empirically examined these relationships in business-to-business markets. However, many believe that behavior among buyers in these two environments is significantly different; thus empirical findings from B2C research may not be readily applicable in B2B environments (Beverland et al., 2007; Doney and Cannon,1997; Edwards et al., 2007; Lam et al., 2004).

Further, the majority of extant downsizing studies focus on downsizing among U.S. organizations. However, downsizing initiatives spread well beyond the U.S. (examples offered in the following section). Additionally, in global business environments where cultural values and norms vary among customer groups, understanding how cultural differences affect perceptions of performance quality and satisfaction becomes crucial; especially with regard to how global suppliers allocate resources within diverse cultural regions (Laroche et al., 2004).

2. Organizational downsizing and customer satisfaction

2.1. Organizational downsizing

Downsizing is commonplace in present-day industrialized markets (Nixon et al., 2004). Between 1995 and 2007, approximately 11.5 million people lost their jobs to downsizing in the U.S. alone. Further, according to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data (2005), sales and marketing personnel have been hit harder than many other groups. Not only are reductions in work force size becoming more frequent (Burton et al., 2005), organizations' rationale for downsizing is changing as well (Hodson and Roscigno, 2004). In the past, layoffs were a last option used by managers confronted with plummeting firm performance. More recently, however, healthy firms around the world are using downsizing as a proactive management strategy in a continuous quest to reduce costs (Ferraro et al., 2005). For example, Citigroup announced plans to cut 5.2% of its work force (17,000 jobs) after enjoying revenue growth of 35% over the prior 5 years (CNNMoney.com, 2007). Similarly, Germany's Allianz AG announced plans to eliminate 9000 jobs in an effort to reduce costs (Veysey and Miller, 2006) after achieving increases in net income of 69% in 2005 and 79% in 2006. Additional examples include HP, Royal Dutch Shell, Bayer Schering, IBM, and Unilever.

In an effort to explain these seemingly contradictory patterns, some argue that three forces, coercive-, mimetic-, and normativeisomorphism, strongly influence managerial decisions related to corporate downsizing. Coercive isomorphism compels firms to conform to institutional norms that define legitimate structures and activities. Mimetic isomorphism impels organizations to mimic the actions of recognized industry leaders. Normative isomorphism– emerging through professional conferences, seminars and networks– pressures executives to adopt currently accepted management practices and philosophies (McKinley et al., 2000). As such, presentday downsizing decisions often are not so much related to economic considerations as they are related to socio-cognitive organizational schemas that view downsizing as effective, necessary, and inevitable— even if the consequences for the organization are uncertain (Lewin, 2001; Lewin and Johnston, 1996; McKinley et al., 2000). The anticipated positive effects of downsizing are decreasing labor costs indirectly leading to greater productivity, improvement in overall effectiveness, and increasing competitive advantage (Lewin, 2003). However, recent empirical findings indicate no significant differences in financial performance, over time, between firms that have downsized and firms that have not downsized (Cascio et al., 1997; De Meuse, et al., 2004; Nixon et al., 2004). On the other hand, the negative effects of downsizing are escalating employee workloads, decreasing employee morale, and decreases in an organization's

ability to learn and adapt (De Meuse et al., 2004; Mishra and Spreitzer, 1998).

2.2. Organizational downsizing and customer satisfaction

Common strategies for enhancing organizational performance include increasing customer satisfaction and improving system productivity (Anderson et al., 1997; Homburg and Rudolph, 2001). Methods for improving productivity include automation of processes, substituting capital for labor, and more recently, reductions in work force size with the expectation that the remaining employee base will make up the shortfall (Brockner, 1988; Oliva and Sterman, 2001).

Organizations often attempt to enhance overall performance by achieving gains in both productivity and customer satisfaction simultaneously. However, in some situations these two goals are incompatible. For example, when labor costs are reduced through downsizing, the result may be a relatively immediate gain in productivity. However, long-term firm performance likely will be at risk when customer satisfaction is dependent on the efforts of customer service-support personnel. When this is the case, significant tradeoffs may exist between productivity and customer satisfaction (Bendapudi and Leone, 2002; Estelami and Hurley, 2003; Kamakura et al., 2002; Nixon et al., 2004).

2.3. The potential influence of culture

Companies recognize that customer satisfaction and loyalty are critical to their long-term success. However, achieving these goals is especially difficult in diverse international settings where many of the causes of customer (dis)satisfaction vary according to cultural context (Bolten and Myers, 2006; Laroche et al., 2004). Two complimentary views on national culture (Hall, 1976; Hofstede, 1980) often are applied to the examination of a variety of multi-cultural business topics (e.g., Bolton and Myers, 2006; Laroche et al., 2004; Scheer et al., 2003). In the first, Hall (1976) posits that cultures vary on a context continuum. In lower-context cultures personal achievement and welfare are of prime importance. In these cultures greater emphasis is placed on discrete encounters, with little regard for long-term commitment and loyalty. In higher-context cultures group welfare and harmony are emphasized, along with greater commitment to long-term relationships, and a stronger sense of interpersonal bonds and loyalty. Thus, in higher context cultures customers may be more forgiving of performance failures and more loyal to their suppliers, because they do not want to disturb the harmony of their established relationships (Laroche et al., 2004). In the second, Hofstede (1980) proposes five dimensions useful in evaluating differences in cultures. The first dimension, individualist/collectivist, is very similar to Hall's (1976) context continuum. In individualist cultures (low-context) individuals' accomplishments, ideas, and goals are emphasized. In collectivist cultures (high context) the emphasis is on group norms and values; and the welfare of the individual is secondary to the welfare of the group. Therefore in collectivist, as compared to individualist cultures, relationships with customers are expected to be “stronger, more intimate, and (thus) more loyal” (Bolton and Myers, 2006 p.114).

Within these paradigms the United States is classified as a lowercontext, more individualist culture. In comparison, France and The Netherlands are classified as relatively higher context, more collectivist cultures (Hofstede, 1980; http://changingminds.org, 2009; http:// www.via-web.de/273.html, 2009; Scheer et al., 2003).

3.1. Model proposal

According to Oliver (1980), consumers' pre-purchase expectations create a reference point against which post-purchase outcomes are compared. This comparative process is termed disconfirmation. Outcomes that exceed expectations create a positive disconfirmation, while disappointing outcomes create a negative disconfirmation. Operationally, this approach combines the influence of expectations and outcomes by calculating a difference score (post-purchase outcome minus prepurchase expectation = disconfirmation); where the difference score predicts levels of (dis)satisfaction. Because of the way disconfirmation is determined this approach is known as subtractive disconfirmation.

While some researchers utilize subtractive disconfirmation (Bolton and Lemon,1999; Cronin and Morris,1989), others argue that both pre-purchase expectations and post-purchase performance evaluations are direct determinants of customer satisfaction (subjective disconfirmation) and thus should be modeled independently (Churchill and Surprenant, 1982). Supporters of subjective disconfirmation argue that buyers' expectations of future supplier performance are formed on the basis of past experiences, supplier provided information, and the recommendations of others (Fornell et al., 1996). Thus, this approach is likely to be more salient in situations in which overall performance (product plus service) cannot be judged discretely (Tse and Wilton, 1988). The subjective disconfirmation approach is used in this study.

Both disconfirmation approaches model pre-purchase expectations and post-purchase performance as important antecedents of customer satisfaction. Some researchers also model perceptions of value received (sometimes termed payment equity) as antecedent to satisfaction. In addition, many models now recognize loyalty/ repurchase intentions as an important outcome of the disconfirmation process through post-purchase satisfaction (e.g., Bolton and Lemon, 1999; Fornell et al., 1996; Vilares and Coelho, 2003). Fig. 1 exhibits these modeled relationships.

Given the current gap in the B2B literature, this studyadopts the ASCI framework (which includes subjective disconfirmation) to assess if this general model is applicable in B2B contexts. Before continuing, however, readers' should note one exception between the ASCI framework and the model tested here. That is, while the ASCI model contains customer complaints as a third outcome construct, the model tested here does not. The decision to exclude the complaint construct is based on an examination of the extant literature as exhibited in Table 1, along with the results of pretests as discussed later in Section 5.1. Within the following Sections the proposed hypotheses and associated support are offered prior to model testing.

3.2. Pre-purchase expectations

When a focal product is of small importance to the buyer firm (e.g., office supplies) then issues surrounding satisfaction and loyalty diminish significantly. At the same time, if the focal product is at the other extreme (1) intrinsically strategic with (2) no acceptable alternatives available, then, by definition, issues surrounding loyalty also diminish significantly; since the buying firm has no choice but to continue to buy the strategic, non-substitutable product — at least in the near-term.

As such, in order to accomplish the study objectives, respondents need to report on a particular category of product; one that is important but, at the same time, alternatives are available. Adopting an approach used in prior research (Anderson and Narus, 1984; Rokkan et al., 2003), regarding pre-purchase expectations respondents are instructed on page 1 of the questionnaire as follows:

When answering the following questions we would like you to think of a single current supplier that most closely fits each of the five criteria listed below.

1. A supplier that has either downsized in the past 2 years and/or is currently downsizing. We define downsizing as a supplier's planned reduction in work force size through any number of methods including early retirements, layoffs, merger, etc.

H3. Perceive value relates positively to satisfaction.

Nothing within either Hall's (1976) cultural context paradigm or Hofstede's (1980) cultural factors paradigm suggests that prepurchase expectations should vary across purchasing professionals from divergent cultures.

3.3. Post-purchase performance quality

Market demands lead many suppliers to bundle additional services with their product offerings, often resulting in the need for greater interaction between customers and supplier personnel (Oliva and Sterman, 2001). As part of the supplier selection process, purchasing professionals develop expectations about the future performance of these bundled offerings (an excellent examination of this topic is found in Wilson et al., 2001). Typically in business markets ancillary services are delivered by a variety of functional areas within the supplier firm, including salespeople, customer support personnel, and technical experts. A supplier's inability to meet or exceed buyers' service-related expectations can result from a variety of causes, including the composition and behavior of the supplier's sales and customer support personnel (Estelami and Hurley, 2003; Homburg and Stock, 2004).

However, one of the frequent results of organizational downsizing is the loss of experienced key contact personnel who provide many of the ancillary services customers are promised (Heskett et al., 1994; Kamakura et al., 2002). Additionally, survivors (those employees remaining after downsizing) often lack the requisite time, skill, and/or motivation to provide the quality service customers expect (Nixon et al., 2004). When this is the case, downsized suppliers' service performance often fails to meet customer pre-purchase expectations.

H6a. Professional purchasers' post-purchase evaluations of downsized suppliers' performance quality are lower than their pre-purchase expectations.

Bolton and Myers (2006) observe that business customers from different cultures are likely to hold different perceptions of what constitutes inferior, adequate, or superior performance/quality. Separately, Laroche et al. (2004) argue that culture differentially affects customers' perceptions of service quality; such that high-context, collectivist cultures are more conservative in their assessment of superior quality and less critical in their assessment of inferior quality.

H6b. When comparing U.S. versus European samples, significant differences occur in purchasing professionals' evaluations of downsized supplier performance quality.

3.4. Perceived value

Most agree that satisfaction is significantly influenced by customers' perceptions of the value they receive (Lam et al., 2004). Perhaps the most prominent theoretical discussion of the distinction between and the importance of quality and value in customers' decision making is offered by Zeithaml's (1988) Means End Model. In accordance with this Model, product attributes such as price, reliability, and adherence to specifications influence customers' perceptions of value. Additionally, customers' interactions with suppliers' service-support personnel also influence perceptions of value. In B2B contexts, the relative influence of service-related attributes may be significantly stronger than the influence of product-related attributes on assessments of value (Humphreys and Williams, 1996).

Satisfied, committed, and productive service-support employees create the service components of value (Heskett et al., 1994; Vilares and Coelho, 2003). However, in firms that have downsized, managers frequently attempt to overcome staff reductions in service-support personnel by expecting survivors to work harder. One method survivors use to cope with these increased expectations is by reducing the amount of time they spend with each customer (Oliva and Sterman, 2001). However, perceptions of value decrease when customers feel rushed, or perceive service-support personnel lack the motivation and/or skills required to satisfy their needs.

H7a. Professional purchasers' post-purchase evaluations of value received from downsized suppliers are lower than their pre-purchase expectations.

When one considers the potential influence of culture on business customers' perceptions of value, differing assessments of performance quality as argued in the preceding section likely will have similar affects on value assessments. For example, if culture is expected to influence business customers' assessment of performance quality (i.e., Bolton and Myers, 2006), then perceptions of inferior/superior quality should similarly influence perceptions of inferior/superior value; all else equal. When this is the case, high-context, collectivist cultures will be more conservative in their assessment of value both in inferior and superior quality contexts.

H7b. When comparing U.S. versus European samples, significant differences occur in purchasing professionals' evaluations of value received from downsized suppliers.

3.5. Customer satisfaction and loyalty

This work conceptualizes customer satisfaction as a business customer's evaluation of a supplier's cumulative performance, over time, on product/service attributes (Homburg and Stock, 2004; Spreng et al., 1996). In sum, the literature on cumulative satisfaction suggests that the most important antecedents of satisfaction include past, current, and future customer expectations, current levels of product/service quality, and customers' perceptions of value received (Fornell et al.,1996; Oliver,1980; Zeithaml,1988). This is in contrast to a transaction-specific perspective where customer satisfaction is viewed as a post-purchase evaluative judgment of a specific purchase occasion (Anderson et al., 1994).

Fornell et al. (1996, p. 8) posit that by measuring not only customer satisfaction, but also its antecedents (prior expectations, perceived levels of current performance, and value received) one captures a cumulative evaluation of a firm's market offering over time, as opposed to a buyer's evaluation of a specific transaction. This is important because, “… while transaction specific satisfaction measures may provide specific diagnostic information about a particular product or service encounter, overall customer satisfaction is a more fundamental indicator of the firm's past, current, and future performance.” The findings in a number of studies support these linkages (e.g., Anderson et al., 1994; Lam et al., 2004).

Similarly, a significant body of work supports the view that customer satisfaction is an important antecedent of customer loyalty (e.g., Homburg et al., 2003; Lam et al., 2004). Oliver (1993) proposes that loyalty is developed through a multi-dimensional process wherein varying forms of loyalty can emerge at each step of the process. Oliver (1993) further argues that satisfaction is a fairly temporal state reflecting how the product or service fulfills its purpose based on either a one-time consumption situation or a repeat/ongoing consumption experience. Loyalty, in contrast, is an attained state of more enduring preference. Interestingly, Oliver (1993) further argues that ultimate loyalty is supported not only by product performance, but also by the convergence of personal and social forces — like those this work suggests. Empirical works supporting these relationships include Fornell et al. (1996), Bolton (1998), and Kumar (2002).

Based on the above discussion of customer satisfaction and loyalty coupled with the preceding arguments on performance quality and value, downsized suppliers who are providing lower levels of quality and value will engender lower levels of satisfaction and loyalty among their business customers.

H9. Professional purchasers' levels of loyalty toward downsized suppliers are roughly equal to their level of satisfaction with these suppliers.

Thus, business buyers are more likely to give suppliers the benefit-of-the-doubt in cases of performance failures in an effort to maintain harmony in the relationship. As such, purchasers from high-context, collectivist cultures are expected to express higher levels of satisfaction with suppliers (Laroche et al., 2004), and express greater loyalty to their current supplier relationships (Bolton and Myers, 2006).

4. Control variables

Within social science research the possibility of confounding due to extraneous influence exists. To help minimize potential biases three control variables are included in this study: (1) supplier sales and customer-support staff reductions, (2) the degree/percentage of supplier firm downsizing, and (3) the degree/percentage of buyer firm downsizing.

4.1. Supplier sales and customer-support staff reductions

This work proposes that downsized suppliers' inability to satisfy business customers often results from reductions in sales and customer service-support personnel (Bendapudi and Leone, 2002; Estelami and Hurley, 2003; Heskett et al., 1994; Homburg and Stock, 2004; Oliva and Sterman, 2001). To ensure that reductions in sales and customer-support personnel are included in the analysis, four scaleresponse questions specifically related to staff reductions in these key areas are included in the questionnaire.

4.2. Supplier firm's degree of downsizing

Downsizing announcements generally include information regarding the level of the intended reduction (i.e., the number or percentage of employees to be terminated). Prior work in this area establishes that the degree of downsizing effects survivors' reactions and attitudes, as well as subsequent firm performance (De Meuse et al., 2004; Lewin, 2001; Nixon et al., 2004). To measure the degree to which the focal supplier firm has downsized two questions are included. The range of supplier downsizing reported is between 1% (n=1) and 50% (n=21), with the average being 15.5%.

4.3. Buyer firm's degree of downsizing

During the pretest phase of this study (see Methodology section) the issue of the potential for respondent downsizing bias emerged. That is, if the respondent's (buyer) firm has downsized might that experience positively/negatively predispose respondents to evaluate downsized suppliers differently. As a result of these concerns, respondents are asked (1) if their firm has downsized and (2) if so, about how much. All but two of the total 435 respondents provided responses to these questions. Of these 34.4% reported that their firms have not downsized. The remaining 65.6% indicated that their firms

have downsized between 1% (n=3) and 60% (n=9), with the average being 14.2%.

5. Research method

5.1. Questionnaire development

In all butone case (loyalty) the items used to measure the constructs of interest in this study are adopted from prior empirical works. A measurement items list, including sources, is available through the lead author. After the initial questionnaire was developed a series of three pretests wereundertaken.First,theresearchcoordinatormet withagroup of 11 purchasing professionals to review the questionnaire thoroughly. One key focus area during this meeting was to ascertain the ability of purchasing professionals to accurately report on the nature and extent of supplier downsizing. The consensus among participants was that the majority of purchasing professionals are able to provide this information. Suggestions for improvements made during this initial pretest were used torevise the questionnaire.Next, fourmarketing academicians were asked to review the revised questionnaire to further assess the clarity of the instructions and questions, and to eliminate any potential biasing elements or statements. Further revisions were made based on this second set of reviews. Finally, the questionnaire was mailed to six members of the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) for additional scrutiny. Each of these questionnaires was returned, with only minor suggestions for improvement. Lastly, once the English version of the questionnaire was finalized the Dutch and French versions were created by co-researchers fluent in these two languages. Subsequent pretests of the questionnaire among Dutch and French professionals indicated that the original intent and measurement remained consistent.

5.2. Research context and data collection

Countries with mature economies where supplier downsizing occurred on a regular basis during recent years are required for this study. The Netherlands and France represent mature economies that are similar to the U.S. economy, while they are at the same time culturally different. The Netherlands and France are examples of relatively high-context, collectivist cultures, thus providing a contrast to the more low-context, individualist culture in the U.S. This allows us to explore the extent to which a country's cultural context influences the impact of supplier downsizing on customer satisfaction.

The respondents in the U.S. represent a random sample of purchasing professionals drawn from the membership of ISM. Similar organizations in The Netherlands and France provide the sampling frame for European purchasers. Cannon and Perreault (1999) point out that while others within the buying firm may have knowledge of some aspects of a supplier relationship,purchasingprofessionalsarewell-informedaboutallaspects of the overall relationship. This makes purchasing professionals the best choice of key informant in studies like the one reported here. Many other researchers in this stream support this belief (e.g., Buvik, 2002; Hutt et al., 1985; Puri and Korgaonkar, 1981). In all, 1346 packets were mailed to potential respondents in the U.S., and 3800 packets were mailed to respondents in Europe. Each packet contained a questionnaire, a cover letter, and a postage-prepaid return envelope. As a result of the initial and subsequent follow-up efforts, 266 usable questionnaires were received from U.S. respondents (19.8%), and 169 usable questionnaires were received from European respondents (4.4%). Admittedly, a stronger response rate from the European effort was anticipated. Nevertheless, as pointed out by several researchers (e.g., Harzing, 2000; Ibeh et al., 2004), response rates in survey research continue to shrink; especially among European industrial populations. 5.3. Measurement validation procedure

First, each set of items is examined using item-to-total correlations to segregate items poorly fitted to specific construct domains. Second,

the resulting sets of items are analyzed via confirmatory factor analysis using LISREL 8.5 to verify unidimensionality. The following fit statistics indicate an acceptable fit (Hu and Bentler, 1999) for both samples (U.S.: CFI=.98, NFI=.95, SRMR=.06, RMSEA=.05; European: CFI=.97, NFI=.89, SRMR=.05, RMSEA=.04).

Third, composite reliabilities are calculated and the average variance extracted (AVE) of each construct is assessed (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). Across both samples, all of the factor loadings are significant, and composite reliabilities range from .84 to .97. The AVEs range from .51 to .87; above the recommended .50 level (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). Fourth, discriminant validity is assessed by estimating a series of two-factor confirmatory models. For each pair the model is estimated first by restricting the factor intercorrelations to unity. Then the model is estimated again with the restriction removed. In each case, the chi-square difference tests (1 df) are significantly smaller in the unrestricted model demonstrating discriminant validity.

7. Implications

First, with regard to the applicability of the ASCI model in several B2B contexts, the significant negative relationship between prepurchase expectations and customer satisfaction in both the U.S. and European samples is surprising. Two possible alternative explanations may support these unexpected findings. First, purchasers are asked to report their pre-purchase expectations prior to supplier downsizing. Then, purchasers are asked to report their evaluations of performance quality, value, and satisfaction after supplier downsizing. Through this process, the disconfirmation of pre-purchase expectations leads to the negative relationship between pre-downsizing expectations and postdownsizing satisfaction. In other words, the higher a purchaser's expectations prior to supplier downsizing, the more difficulty the supplier has in meeting those high-level expectations after downsizing. This implies the higher the expectations the greater the disappointment. However, this does not explain why findings indicate a positive relationship between pre-purchase expectations and evaluations of performance quality and value (e.g., higher expectations lead to higher evaluations of quality and value). One would expect if negative disconfirmation occurs, then lower-than-expected satisfaction would stem directly from lower-than-expected performance quality and value. Thus similar negative relationships are expected. However, this is not the case here. A second alternative possibility is that during the supplier selection process professional purchasers, in their effort to select the best supplier, develop excessively high pre-purchase expectations; more so than may be the case among consumers. As a result, the higher the pre-purchase expectation the more difficulty suppliers have in meeting or exceeding these very high expectations. However, as before, this does not explain the positive relationships between pre-purchase expectations and post-purchase evaluations of performance quality and value. Thus, at least this component of the ASCI model's applicability in B2B contexts remains questionable.

Second, study findings indicate that business customers (both U.S. and European) enter into their focal buyer–supplier relationships with relatively high expectations of anticipated supplier performance (8.56 and 7.78, respectively). In this study this finding sets a baseline for comparison of subsequent performance. Against this baseline of comparison downsized suppliers fail to meet buyers' performance expectations. In both samples post-purchase evaluations of downsized supplier performance quality, value received, overall satisfaction, and loyalty are all significantly below pre-downsizing expectations. Furthermore, the link between downsizing and performance disappointment is strengthened by the finding that reductions in suppliers' sales and

customer-support staff has a relatively strong, negative impact on customer satisfaction in both samples. Thus, study findings add strength to the emerging belief that downsizing can have substantial, long-term deleterious effects.

Third, study findings relating to cultural influences are mixed. Opposite of prediction European buyers' pre-purchase expectations (7.78) are statistically lower than their U.S. counterparts (8.56). While this relationship is intimated by Bolton and Myers (2006), nothing in either Hall's (1976) work or Hofstede's (1980) work suggests this should be the case. Second, Europeans' evaluations of value received and expressions of overall satisfaction are both below their U.S. counterparts; consistent with Hall (1976) and Hofstede (1980). However, contrary to expectations culture comparisons provided no evidence of differences in evaluations of performance quality and expressions of loyalty; as the two aforementioned cultural paradigms suggest. As this work represents a first, exploratory investigation into possible cultural influences in this context, readers should view these findings with caution.

7.1. Managerial implications

This study provides empirical evidence of the impact reductions in sales and customer-support personnel have on business customers' satisfactionwith and loyalty toward downsized suppliers. Together with recent findings in the finance and management literatures, study results provide ample motivation for downsizing proponents to reevaluate this prevalent strategy. This reasoning is further strengthened by beliefs that small changes in customer satisfaction levels can significantly influence a firm's profitability, cash flow, and market equity (Fornell et al., 1996; Fornell et al. 2006; Reichheld and Sasser, 1990).

When downsizing must occur, the results of this research suggest several implications for future downsizing organizations. First, while most agree that customer satisfaction should be monitored regularly, the findings reported here indicate the need for satisfaction monitoring is significantly greater for firms that downsize their workforce, especially key contact personnel. Second, these study findings coupled with other research (De Meuse et al., 2004; Nixon et al., 2004) suggest that smaller, more strategic downsizing (i.e., minimize eliminating key contact personnel) appears to have less impact on satisfaction and loyalty. Third, in those cases where large broad-based cuts are judged advisable, proactive strategies should be developed to minimize the negative effects on both customer and survivor satisfaction — since the literature indicates that satisfaction levels between these two groups are strongly interrelated (Bendapudi and Leone, 2002; Estelami and Hurley, 2003; Homburg and Stock, 2004).

8. Research limitations and directions for future research

This study is exploratory in nature. Thus, the study implications are tentative. Also, the assumption is made that purchasing professionals, as key informants, are able to provide reliable information on the focal constructs. This assumption is tested using questions designed to assess respondents' ability to provide meaningful information. Finally, firms represented by respondents in the US sample are weighted more heavily (49.3%) toward larger firms (i.e., revenues exceed $500 million).

Future work in this area might examine if various catalysts to downsizing have (dis)similar outcomes, especially with relation to customer satisfaction and loyalty. A second area of investigation might question what, if any, compensatory adjustments downsized firms should initiate to minimize the negative impact of downsizing on customer satisfaction. Finally, comparisons of business customers' levels of satisfaction with downsized verses non-downsized suppliers may provide a more accurate assessment of the degree to which satisfaction levels across these two groups of suppliers differ.
