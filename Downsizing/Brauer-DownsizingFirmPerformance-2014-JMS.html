Workforce Downsizing and Firm Performance: An Organizational Routine Perspective

Matthias Brauer and Tomi Laamanen

University of Luxembourg; University of St. Gallen

ABSTRACT While there is an extensive body of work on how organizational routines emerge and evolve over time, there is a scarcity of research on what happens when routines are disrupted or disbanded through the elimination of key individuals involved in them. This study is the first to theorize and empirically examine the relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance applying an organizational routine perspective. Consistent with prior research on organizational routines, we posit that small-scale downsizing leads to efficiency improvements without disrupting the existing routines. While larger routine disruptions occur in both medium- and large-scale downsizing, we further argue and find that large-scale downsizing tends to be more beneficial than medium-scale downsizing. Building on prior research on routines, we reason that in medium-scale downsizing employees try to salvage the impaired, partially functioning routines, while large-scale downsizing requires a more fundamental rethinking and re-creation of routines leading to more positive outcomes. Our study contributes to downsizing research through the application of the organizational routine perspective to explain the financial outcomes of downsizing. In doing so, we depart from the widely held assumption in the downsizing literature that the relationship between the magnitude of downsizing and firm performance is linear. Our study also extends prior research on organizational routines by highlighting the usefulness of conceiving routines as mindful accomplishments where the pressure to engage in path-breaking cognitive effort may lead to better results than path-dependent repairing of routines.

Keywords: firm performance, organizational routines, workforce downsizing

Routines involve and are performed by multiple actors (Becker et al., 2006; Feldman, 2003; Feldman and Pentland, 2003), making actors and their interactions central to the idea of routines.

While earlier research has extensively analysed how organizational routines emerge and evolve over time (e.g., Becker et al., 2006; Bruderer and Singh, 1996; Tripsas, 2009), there is a scarcity of studies on what happens when routines are disrupted (e.g., Edmondson et al., 2001) or disbanded through the elimination of key individuals involved in them (e.g., Aime et al., 2010). Workforce downsizing which incurs changes in the absolute number of jobs, changes in job occupancy due to redefinitions of positions and responsibilities, and disruptions of organizational relationship networks represents one of the most commonly occurring shocks to organizational routines and their outcomes. Surprisingly, however, extant research has not integrated the organizational routine perspective to the study on the effects of workforce downsizing and firm performance.

In this paper, we suggest that the routine perspective may be a powerful lens to explain the relationship between workforce downsizing and firm performance. We argue and also demonstrate empirically that it can bring new clarity to the seemingly contradictory empirical results on the relationship between workforce downsizing and firm performance. Specifically, we argue that the magnitude of downsizing plays an important role as the likelihood for a major disruption of organizational routines increases with the extent of workforce downsizing. The likelihood for disruptions increases with the extent of workforce downsizing as the amount of work performed by the remaining employees increases greatly and as ‘holes’ in organizational routines are created. The responsiveness of employees to these routine disruptions as well as the rigour of this response, however, is likely to differ based on employees’ perceived urgency and motivation for rethinking and re-creating organizational routines. Thus, even though disruptions increase with the magnitude of workforce downsizing, the perceived urgency and motivation for employees to engage in re-conceptualizing routines can also be expected to increase.

Due to this joint effect, we argue that the relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance is non-linear, with medium-scale downsizing leading to the smallest improvements in firm performance. Small-scale downsizing is argued to lead to more significant performance improvements than medium-scale downsizing since small-scale downsizing eliminates operational inefficiencies without disrupting existing routines. While routine disruptions occur in both medium- and large-scale downsizing, greater performance improvements for large-scale downsizing relative to medium-scale downsizing are argued to result from the fact that organizational members are likely to expend more effort on how to re-conceptualize and re-create organizational routines (Armstrong-Stassen, 1994; Brockner et al., 1997; Zellmer-Bruhn, 2003). In contrast, following medium-scale downsizing, organizational members are cognitively relatively more inert and try to manage with the impaired, only partially functioning routines.

Second, our study contributes to organizational routine literature and the debate on whether routines should be conceptualized as evolutionary behavioural patterns (Ashforth and Fried, 1988) or as effortful accomplishments (Pentland and Rueter, 1994, p. 488) with a strong mindful component (Feldman, 2000; Feldman and Pentland, 2003). Though we do not directly observe the process of routine re-creation, our empirical results suggest that in addition to their path dependent characteristics (Becker, 2004), routines are also cognitive accomplishments that can be re-established at a high performance level even after major punctuated disruptions (Gersick, 1991). Adopting an organizational routine perspective, we then put forward our hypotheses on the relationship between workforce downsizing and firm performance. Subsequently, we present our research design and empirical findings and conclude with a discussion.

RESEARCH ON ORGANIZATIONAL ROUTINE DISRUPTIONS

In organizational routine literature, opinions differ on the degree of consciousness with which actors carry out organizational routines. One perspective proposes that routines are characterized by automated, habituated, or even mindless behaviours (Ashforth and Fried, 1988). A more recent, increasingly dominant perspective views routines as ‘effortful accomplishments’ (Pentland and Rueter, 1994, p. 488) capable of continuous, endogenous change (Becker, 2004; Feldman, 2000; Lazaric, 2011). Proponents of the second position argue in particular that organizational routines are not only simply followed or reproduced – rather, people have a choice between following or amending an organizational routine (Feldman, 2000; Orlikowski, 2002; Pentland, 1995; Pentland and Rueter, 1994).

Routines are thus not only products of subconscious rule-following but involve deliberate adaptive and creative behaviours by the actors involved in the routine making (Feldman, 2000; Ulrich, 2011). By introducing the distinction between the ostensive and performative aspects of organizational routines, Feldman (2000, 2003) and Feldman and Pentland (2003) made an important contribution in explicitly accounting for the cognitive ‘design’ aspects of routines. The ostensive aspect refers to the cognitive representation of a routine – the way in which certain tasks are intended to be accomplished. In contrast, the performative aspect of a routine refers to the actual performances engaged

Therefore routines are seen to have qualities for both stability and change (Pentland, 1995; Pentland and Rueter, 1994). Routines can change continuously as actors interact with each other and come up with better ways of realizing a given task (Feldman, 2000). The adaptive perspective on organizational routines thus proposes that actors in the organization adapt to the new conditions and often find more efficient operating procedures (Adler et al., 1999; Feldman, 2000; Pentland and Feldman, 2005).

When outcomes fall short of ideals, the performance feedback effect (e.g., Greve, 1998) may motivate continued striving, encouraging organizational actors to seek new ideas and actions leading to an improved outcome. Along similar lines, Zellmer-Bruhn (2003) found that teams experiencing severe disruptions will be more likely to either search for or adopt new routines from external, rather than internal sources. This may increase organizational effectiveness as the greater outside orientation may generate novel ideas or ease organizational rigidities and biases ingrained in internal decision-making processes.

In our theorizing, we build in particular on the cognitive aspects of routine re-creation. While earlier research has found that changes in routines can be driven by changes in the external environment (e.g., Cohen et al., 1996) or discontinuous changes can be driven by combinations of routines or imperfect internal replication (Becker et al., 2006), we focus on the cognitive effort and motivation needed for changing routines in the face of major routine disruption through workforce downsizing.[3]

When approaching the change of routines from the perspective of cognitive effort, we argue that there is a major difference in whether routines are simply adapted through local search with lower cognitive effort (e.g., Argote and Greve, 2007; Cyert and March, 1963) or whether a more significant cognitive effort is undertaken to re-create routines through more extensive search processes. Importantly, the two views on the amount of cognitive effort invested result in different predictions regarding the relationship between workforce downsizing and firm performance. Specifically, we theorize that depending on the magnitude of workforce downsizing, organizational members invest different amounts of cognitive effort to repair or re-create the disrupted routines, leading to different performance implications.

HYPOTHESES

Magnitude of Workforce Downsizing and Firm Performance Change

When firms engage in relatively small-scale workforce downsizing, they eliminate operational inefficiencies but at the same time are able to fully safeguard their resources and to preserve their routines. Employees will thus see little need for adaptation of organizational routines or extensive search and experimentation with better ways of carrying out an organizational routine. For the most part, a positive relationship between small-scale workforce downsizing and change in profitability is thus likely to result from improved operational efficiency. Routines change in an incremental way consistent with the view of routine evolution as a source of continuous endogenous change (Feldman, 2000).

In medium-scale workforce downsizing, most organizational routines can be expected to remain functional. Disruptions, however, are likely to occur at an increasing frequency in different parts of the organization as the organizational routines become stretched to their limits. Essentially, greater negative survivor effects paired with these disruptions following medium-scale downsizing are likely to lead to inferior performance outcomes when compared with small-scale downsizing.

While routine disruptions occur in both medium- and large-scale downsizing, the responsiveness of employees to these routine disruptions, as well as the rigour of this response, are likely to differ based on employees’ perceived urgency and motivation for rethinking and re-creating the affected organizational routines. Both can be expected to increase with the magnitude of downsizing. Though disruptions occur, medium-scale downsizing may not, however, yet force the organization to adapt or repair organizational routines beyond the level necessary to maintain current operations. Instead, there is an inertial tendency in organizations to rely on the past and to ‘make do’ with the routines that have been built over time (Hodgkinson, 2003; Tripsas, 2009).

In contrast, incremental patching or mending of existing routines is no longer viable following large-scale workforce downsizing. Prior routines become so dysfunctional due to the lack of resources that they cannot be salvaged any more and entirely new routines have to be envisioned and developed to fit to the new reality (Lazaric, 2011). Consistent with this notion that the positive effects of large-scale organizational downsizing are driven by the added pressure to cognitively rethink the routines needed in the new reality instead of trying to salvage the impaired prior routines, Zellmer-Bruhn (2003) found that teams experiencing severe disruptions will be more likely to either search for or adopt new routines from external, rather than internal sources which may increase organizational effectiveness as the greater outside orientation may generate novel ideas and/or ease organizational rigidities and biases ingrained in internal decision-making processes. As a consequence of the organization being inert to repair or re-create its routines in medium-scale downsizing, organizational performance is likely to develop less positively when compared with large-scale downsizing which increases employees’ urgency to engage in a complete rethinking of their routines. Based on the above reasoning, we predict a non-linear effect between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and post-downsizing performance:

Hypothesis 1: The relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and post-downsizing performance change is non-linear, with the relationship exhibiting a U-shaped pattern.

Moderating Influence of the Timing of Workforce Downsizing

Building on the organizational routine perspective, we posit in our first hypothesis that the magnitude of workforce downsizing is associated with disparate levels of employees’ cognitive and practical efforts in rethinking and repairing routines. In addition to the magnitude of workforce downsizing, employees’ efforts at routine repair are also likely to be governed by their discretion to do so. The timing of downsizing is therefore a potentially important process contingency.

Following Cameron’s insight, we classify firms which downsized without having experienced a decline in market value prior to downsizing as proactive and firms which downsized after having experienced a decline as reactive or defensive (e.g., Love and Nohria, 2005).

An effective reorganization of work processes requires a minimum dedication of time. In the absence of the necessary time to make these adjustments, performance is likely to develop less favourably in the post-downsizing period regardless of the scale of downsizing. Another important aspect of our theoretical argument is that (reactive) downsizing under conditions of declining financial performance increases time pressure. Under time pressure, firms are less likely to be able to conduct an ordered and wellstructured downsizing process (Ginsberg and Baum, 1994).

Deteriorating performance has also been found to be associated with crisis mentality, resistance to change, restricted communication, lack of teamwork, and a rigid response (Cameron et al., 1987a, 1987b; Staw et al., 1981). Furthermore, prior research suggests that time pressure may not only constrain the willingness to rethink existing routines but also constrain their creativity in doing so, as well as curb employees’ motivation and efforts in planning, enacting, and learning new routines. (e.g., Unsworth and Clegg, 2000).[4]

Similarly, Gilson et al. (2004) found that late downsizers were associated with significantly lower employee satisfaction and workplace performance and higher conflict when compared with non-downsizers or early downsizers.

Under conditions of reactive downsizing, heightened perceived time pressure is thus likely to decrease employees’ willingness and ability to engage in routine reconceptualization and re-creation. Consequently, not only will reactive downsizing, on average, be associated with lower post-downsizing performance improvements, but the U-shaped relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance improvements will be less pronounced under these circumstances. This is because the sense of urgency and the motivational effects which may encourage organizational members to rethink routines after large-scale downsizing may be offset by high time pressure.

In contrast, under conditions of proactive downsizing, the availability of time can be expected to positively moderate an organization’s ability to rebuild its routines irrespective of whether it is engaging in small-scale, medium-scale, or large-scale downsizing. When an organization is still performing well, routine disruptions can be expected to be addressed with careful planning. Lack of time pressure gives the organization the latitude to think about adjustments and to search for new solutions, easing the pressure to adapt or rebuild routines. Engaging in proactive downsizing thus gives an organization a better possibility to modify or rebuild its routines when compared to organizations that are under time pressure.

Therefore, when firms have more time, the increased efforts of employees in rethinking and re-creating routines following large-scale downsizing will not only become more likely but also have greater chances of success. As a result, post-downsizing performance improvements will be on average greater in proactive downsizing. SAMPLE AND METHODS

Sample and Data Collection

Our sample includes all firms that were listed on either the STOXX Europe 50 or the Dow Jones Eurostoxx 50 on any of the years from 1996 to 2006. This resulted to a set of 73 firms. Our total panel contains 803 company years. We focused on these 73 firms for a number of reasons. First, these firms belong to the same stock market index and thus all receive comparable financial analyst coverage and scrutiny. They are confronted with similar institutional pressures from the stock market. Given that the companies belong to the same peer group, socio-cognitive considerations are also likely to play a role even though the companies compete in different industries. Moreover, firms listed on these indices present their financial reports in compliance with the same set of accounting principles (IFRS) which avoids distortion of financial measures. Focusing on large publicly listed companies also allows for a better comparability with past studies on downsizing, most of which also focused on large public firms (Vicente-Lorente and Suarez-Gonzalez, 2007).

Second, the STOXX and Eurostoxx indices represent a broad mix of industries with no industry accounting for more than 25 per cent. While many downsizing studies have used samples drawn only from the manufacturing sector (see Datta et al., 2010 for a critique), we also include service sector companies in our sample. Understanding downsizing in services is particularly relevant due to the strong reliance on human capital in the service sector. Having a broader range of industries also allows for a broader generalizability of our findings. Finally, the companies in our sample are headquartered in multiple different European countries which helps alleviate any potential country biases associated with downsizing.

We obtained data for our variables from Compustat and Worldscope. In order to further assess the validity of our workforce downsizing measure, we also cross-checked the companies’ online press archives and the business briefs of Reuters and Bloomberg.

A score of 0.1 for our dependent variable would thus indicate that a downsizing firm’s ROA increased on average by 10 per cent in the three-year postdownsizing period relative to the year prior to downsizing. An alternative possible measure for examining the performance of downsizing would have been to use the change in market value over time. However, due to the forward-looking nature of the market value based measures, we concluded that the change in accounting performance after downsizing represents a more accurate and robust measure of post-downsizing performance.

Explanatory Variables

Magnitude of workforce downsizing. Researchers have typically chosen a specific numerical cut-off point in workforce reduction to represent downsizing. The most commonly used cut-off is 5 per cent (e.g., Ahmadjian and Robinson, 2001; Cascio et al., 1997; Guthrie and Datta, 2008; Vicente-Lorente and Suarez-Gonzalez, 2007). Both Ahmadjian and Robinson (2001) and Cascio et al. (1997) concluded that a 5 per cent reduction represents a significant event and likely indicates an intentional reduction in employees, a hallmark of downsizing (Freeman and Cameron, 1993). Consistent with this, we measured the magnitude of downsizing as a percentage of total employees (e.g., DeMeuse et al., 2004), and only included workforce reductions greater than 5 per cent in our empirical analysis.

Arguments can be made favouring higher and lower cut-offs. Using a higher threshold has been argued to better separate firms that have engaged in real downsizing from firms that simply streamline their operations (Guthrie and Datta, 2008). On the other hand, the companies in our sample are so large that even lower cut-offs would in many cases mean elimination of several thousand jobs. Therefore, in addition to a 5 per cent decrease in the number of employees, we tested for the robustness of our results using 3 and 10 per cent changes in employment as demarcation points. These threshold rules resulted in no substantive change in study results and conclusions.

Proactiveness of workforce downsizing. Similar to Love and Nohria (2005), we determined the proactiveness of workforce downsizing by using the change in the firm’s market capitalization in the year before downsizing. We calculated the natural logarithm of the ratio of a firm’s market value of equity plus long-term debt at the end of the year divided by its value at the beginning of the year prior to downsizing. Since market capitalization is a forward-looking measure, positive changes in market capitalization indicate stable or improving performance prospects before downsizing, implying a proactive approach. Correspondingly, negative changes would imply a reactive approach to downsizing. Our measurement of proactive versus reactive downsizing is also consistent with the research on divestitures. Scholars in the fields of strategic management and finance distinguish ‘early’ and ‘late’ divestors by analysing whether a divestiture occurred prior or after a performance decline (Dranikoff et al., 2002; Ravenscraft and Scherer, 1987).

Control Variables

In order to assess the impact of workforce downsizing on firm performance over and above previous firm performance, we controlled for past firm performance using the mean ROA for the three years preceding the workforce reduction. We also controlled for firm size, measured as the natural logarithm of total sales. Prior research has both argued and found that if firms have gone beyond optimal firm size, the performance improvements resulting from workforce downsizing are more positive for these overextended firms (Espahbodi et al., 2000; Jensen, 1993). In addition, we measure a firm’s concentration ratio in order to account for firm diversification and the effect that a firm’s performance improvement may be due to growth in particular lines of business. We also control for a firm’s readily available (financial) slack (Bromiley, 1991), using the firm’s current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities). Moreover, we controlled for firm asset change, measured as the percentage change in firms’ total assets in the year the workforce reduction took place compared to the previous year (Espahbodi et al., 2000). Controlling for asset change allows for the isolation of the effect of workforce reductions over and above changes in the physical scope of firm operations (Cascio et al., 1997; Guthrie and Datta, 2008). To ensure that portfolio optimization, which functions as a major source for performance variation in multi-business firms, is not confounding our results, we control for the number of divestitures and for the number of acquisitions undertaken by the firm in the year of the downsizing.

Finally, in order to control for industry effects (e.g., systematic differences in employee turnover across industries), we used a dummy which distinguished between service industries (‘1’) and manufacturing industries (‘0’). An alternative approach applying six industry dummies based on two-digit SIC codes rendered the same results. Effects of the different time periods during which firms downsized their workforce were controlled for by including year dummies in the regressions.

Data Analysis

In order to test our hypotheses on the implications of the magnitude of workforce reductions on the subsequent chance in firm profitability, we estimated generalized least squares (GLS) random effects cross-sectional time series regressions. Thereby, we respond to the critique expressed by Datta et al. (2010, p. 339) who lamented the overreliance on static, cross-sectional designs by previous research. In our regressions, we applied random effects models since the results of Hausman’s test (Greene, 2000; Hausman et al., 1984) did not reject the randomness of residuals hypothesis.

To further eliminate any potential concerns of multicollinearity arising from interaction terms being highly correlated with their constituent variables, we mean centred the direct terms used to construct the interaction term (Aiken and West, 1991). Subsequent collinearity diagnostics using the variance inflation factor (VIF) indicated no multicollinearity problems, with none of the VIF values near the threshold of 10 (Cohen et al., 2003; Neter et al., 1996). We incorporated non-linearity into our panel regression analysis by adding the squared term of the magnitude of downsizing into our regression models and moderation analysis by multiplying both the first and second order terms of the magnitude of downsizing with our moderating variable. In order to ensure the correct interpretation of the interaction results, we also plotted a separate graph.

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

This study focuses on the relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance. Applying an organizational routine perspective, we posit and find that the relationship between the magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance improvements is non-linear (U-shaped). Small-scale downsizing is argued to lead to more significant performance improvements when compared with medium-scale downsizing since small-scale downsizing eliminates operational inefficiencies without disrupting existing routines. Additionally, survivor effects are likely to be less severe in small-scale downsizing when compared to medium-scale downsizing.

While routine disruptions occur in both medium- and large-scale downsizing, we propose that greater performance improvements for large-scale downsizing relative to medium-scale downsizing result from the fact that organizational members are likely to expend more cognitive and practical effort on how to rethink and re-create organizational routines. Following medium-scale downsizing, organizational members are more inclined to manage with impaired, only partially functioning routines and are preoccupied with ‘making do’. In contrast, the sense of urgency of employees is significantly greater following large-scale downsizing due to the fact that the need to address the disruptions is imminent, but also because job security is higher when compared to medium-scale downsizing.

Theoretical Implications

We contribute to an improved understanding of the performance implications of workforce downsizing in several ways. First, we extend prior research on workforce downsizing by challenging the general notion that the relationship between magnitude of workforce downsizing and firm performance improvements is in fact linear, applying an organizational routine perspective. Second, our results draw attention to the fact that the timing of downsizing matters. In line with recent contributions in this area (Datta et al., 2010), we concur that the identification and analysis of critical, internal, and external conditions is an important task for improving our understanding of the effectiveness of downsizing. Third and finally, we also contribute to the research on workforce downsizing by demonstrating the existence of viable, alternative theoretical perspectives to examine the phenomenon. Though we are unable to directly observe the processes of routine re-creation as part of our study design, the empirical results support the potential usefulness of the organizational routine perspective for predicting the outcomes of workforce downsizing. Specifically, complementing motivation theory, which has been central to survivor research, with an organizational routine perspective seems to provide for a more comprehensive explanation of the mixed findings in past downsizing studies.

Our research is positioned in the intersection of the literatures on organizational routines and workforce downsizing. Despite the tendency to define routines as ‘effortful accomplishments’ (Pentland and Rueter, 1994, p. 488) and the recognition of the importance of mindfulness (e.g., Jordan et al., 2009; Levinthal and Rerup, 2006; Weick and Sutcliffe, 2006), prior research on routines has been relatively silent on the role of the cognitive effort in routine emergence. While in psychology and learning literatures, there is an extensive body of work on the effects of individual level cognitive effort of an individual’s attention, learning, and performance (e.g., Bertsch et al., 2007; Piolat et al., 2005), in organization research the role and effects of cognitive effort have so far received limited attention (O’Reilly and Chatman, 1994; Todd and Benbasat, 1999).

While earlier research has found that changes in routines can be driven by changes in the external environment (e.g., Cohen et al., 1996), continuous internal adaptation (Feldman, 2000), or imperfect internal replication (Becker et al., 2006), we argue that cognitive effort and motivation are needed for changing routines following major internal disruptions. When approaching the change of routines from the perspective of cognitive effort, we posit that there is a major difference in whether routines are simply adapted through local search with lower cognitive effort (e.g., Argote and Greve, 2007; Cyert and March, 1963) or whether a more significant cognitive effort is undertaken.

Practical Implications

Our findings also point to some implications for managers. Previous research on the performance of downsizing and non-downsizing companies largely suggests that managers should first consider viable alternatives (e.g., short-time, leave with partial pay and benefits intact, part-time work, early retirements, unpaid extra working time) before resorting to workforce downsizing. While our results indicate that workforce downsizing on a broader scale may indeed help a company to restore its financial performance, managers should not misread our results as a call for blindly resorting to downsizing activity.

In keeping with Cascio (2002), we believe that managers should carefully weigh the different alternatives that are at hand. Little knowledge, however, has been generated on how workforce downsizing should be managed if none of these alternatives are feasible. This study addresses this shortcoming by generating some tentative advice on how to implement workforce downsizing to improve firm performance. Findings indicate that the magnitude and proactiveness of workforce downsizing are important parameters in the implementation.

Limitations and Avenues for Future Research

Our paper opens up a number of potential directions for future research. First, while our theorizing highlights the importance of considering the processes of routine re-creation, our current data does not allow for a direct measurement of the extent of routine disruption and employees’ cognitive efforts in routine reformation. Micro-level studies on the internal dynamics of downsizing would thus constitute a valuable complement for our study in order to better understand the deeper level cognitive and psychological dynamics underlying downsizing. In particular, studies which employ longitudinal and repeated cross-sectional survey designs would be needed to more accurately capture the degree of routine disruption associated with different levels of downsizing and to assess the cognitive efforts and psychological states of employees following downsizing. Furthermore, our data did not allow us to discern whether downsizing has disparate disruptive effects on routines at different organizational levels. In-depth multiple case study analyses which examine the effects of downsizing on routines of different order (Winter, 2000) and at different organizational levels thus bear great potential to advance our understanding of the relationship between workforce downsizing and firm performance. Moreover, building on the work by DeRue et al. (2008), simulation studies could also be used to examine how individuals’ cognitive effort changes when the magnitude of workforce downsizing exceeds the threshold beyond which the team can no longer salvage its routine for performing a given task.

In conclusion, since downsizing has in recent years become a common practice for management teams in large firms to react to different kinds of threats and setbacks, we call for a more fine-grained research on the contextual determinants and longer-term consequences of downsizing. This is an area where scientific research has potentially a lot to give for management teams operating under high external short-term pressures to satisfy the capital markets.
