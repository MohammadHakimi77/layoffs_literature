Employee layoff under different modes of restructuring: exit, downsizing or relocation

Kristien Coucke, Enrico Pennings and Leo Sleuwaegen

Pressured by globalizing economies and growing international competition,

an increasing number of firms are forced to rationalize productive operations.

Especially poorly performing firms need to improve their profitability through

downsizing or relocating their operations, which in many cases causes collective

employee layoffs. This article adds to the emergent literature on the consequences

of downsizing by looking into the determinants of collective employee layoffs.

Based on a unique sample of Belgian firms reporting collective layoffs this article

analyzes the firm’s decision to dismiss all employees (exit), a significant proportion

of its employees without creating new employment elsewhere (downsizing), or to

move production abroad (international relocation). We theoretically derive the

conditions under which a firm prefers one strategy to another. The empirical

results confirm that relocating firms are most profitable among the restructuring

firms, have invested more in the recent past, operate in sectors with significant

economies of scale and belong more often to a multinational group than firms

opting for downsizing or exit. Downsizing firms are more capital intensive than

relocating firms, while exiting firms are less profitable, smaller, younger, and more

labor intensive than downsizing or relocating firms.

1. Introduction

In an environment characterized by a strong rise in global competition, an increasing number of firms have to adapt their strategy in order to survive. In most cases, the change in strategy is followed by a drastic organizational restructuring (Cascio, 1993). Organizational restructuring can take many forms but most of the time results in a significant layoff of employees. The decision to layoff employees reflects a difference in the current size of the workforce and the size of the workforce that would maximize the market value of the firm. The resulting decrease in labor force may however reflect different strategies. The decision to relocate follows from the presence of new and growing markets with large differences in labor costs and can be stimulated by attractive government regulations in foreign countries. Relocation is further enhanced by a reduction in transport costs, which allows global firms to choose the most cost efficient production location elsewhere and transport it back to the final consumers.

An incumbent firm can also try to become more cost efficient at its existing plant through closing down part of its operations without moving activities abroad (downsizing). Though downsizing may occur without layoffs through voluntary buyout and early retirement programs, downsizing here refers to a reduction of the size of the firm’s workforce through collective layoff, i.e. a dismissal of a significant part of the workforce. In Belgium, one of the most open economies in the world, where global competition has risen to maximum levels, and where labor cost is high, firms have a strong incentive to further automate their production process and substitute labor for capital. Ultimately, if a firm is unable to raise its profitability to positive levels, it will decide to exit the market.

Notwithstanding the attention that relocation and downsizing activities have gained in the last decade, and the insights that organizational consequences including employee reorientation practices are strongly dependent on the mode of restructuring, the existing literature provides no evidence regarding the choice of firms whether to relocate, downsize, or exit from the market (Freeman and Cameron, 1993; Dewitt, 1993). This study attempts to fill this gap by linking strategic restructuring choices to firm and industry characteristics, enabling a better insight into the magnitude and consequences of the associated employee layoff.

We first propose in Section 2 a theoretical framework detailing how firm and industry characteristics have an impact on the decision to choose one of the three restructuring modes. Our theoretical propositions are tested within a multinomial logit approach, discussed in Section 3. In Section 4, we present the data. Section 5 examines the results, and Section 6 concludes.

2. Modes of restructuring through employee layoff

In this section, we set up a theoretical framework to study the possible impact of firm and industry characteristics on the decision to relocate, downsize, or exit. The decision to restructure follows from possible cost savings the firm can realize from taking such actions. Especially firms in financial distress will be prompted to undertake a strong restructuring of their operations. The gains and costs of undertaking restructuring actions will depend on a set of industry and firm characteristics. Obviously, if the net result turns out to be negative, firms will refrain

Exit is the most drastic restructuring decision among the three options considered. Stopping all activity reflects the failure of the firm to survive profitably in the market. The firm will choose to exit if it makes negative profits, and other modes of restructuring are not possible or too costly. Compared to other modes, the adjustment cost of exit is positive and equals the scrap value of the firm. If the scrap value is small, because capital is sunk, more recent investment induces more committed capital, thereby increasing the continuation value of the firm. Caves and Porter (1976) further argue that capital-intensive industries and industries with a large average firm size exhibit strong barriers to exit.

It may appear that the assumption that technology and skills are equal among countries imposes strong restrictions on our model, especially if destination countries for relocation include developing countries. Indeed, if skilled and unskilled labor are imperfect substitutes and/or capital-skill complementarity exists, relocation to countries lacking skilled labor could become less attractive (see, e.g. Krusell et al., 2000). Likewise, reducing unskilled labor through using more capital, which increases the productivity of skilled labor, would favor the downsizing option more than it does in our model. Moreover, if technology adoption becomes endogenous and is allowed to differ across countries, the choice of technology will depend on absorptive capacities and availability of skills in relocation countries. In such case, the decision to relocate becomes a lot more complicated than merely an evaluation of cost differences. Dependent upon skill differences, the firm may choose to implement a different technology in the long run. In the short run, with the existing technology, the skill gap may negatively affect production7 (see e.g. Caselli and Coleman, 2006, for an interesting model and implications of endogenous technology choice across countries).

Since, however, the largest part of relocations from Belgium concern relocations to other developed countries, (64% of all relocations), especially other EU countries, we expect our basic model to hold. In any case, in testing the model we have split the data set into firms that relocate to less developed countries and firms that relocate to developed countries. If technology/skill differences do play a major role we expect a different regime or rejection of the proposed model for the latter group of countries.

Moreover, in testing the model we needed to control for other factors beyond production and cost optimization. In addition to the neo-classical cost considerations made above, there exists an empirical and theoretical literature on the exit decision, which emphasizes other characteristics of the firm. In the widely acclaimed model of Jovanovic (1982), firms learn their relative efficiency and are forced to leave the industry when they cannot meet the required efficiency. As a result, small and young firms are more likely to exit from the industry. Furthermore, firms will also exit if other restructuring modes are either not feasible or too costly from a resource perspective. Young firms, for example, are often too inexperienced to take the risk to relocate their production process. At the same time, small and young firms will find it difficult and costly to further automate their production process and lack the

The most important factor in the decision to restructure has been hypothesized to be a lack of profitability as compared to similar firms operating in the same market. Shareholders will require clearly underperforming firms to restructure and to achieve a higher profitability. We include the return on equity (ROE) as a measure8

Together with poor performance, the financial structure of the firm has been hypothesized to affect the restructuring decision. Highly leveraged firms are more likely to restructure. Especially firms in financial distress underinvest in fixed assets (Myers, 1977) and have a higher propensity to restructure. Debtholders will exert pressure to layoff employees, liquidate or sell assets when the firm fails to meet its interest payments. Moreover, debt-holders, as being first on the creditors list, will fight against continued operation if the net value they receive from selling assets exceeds their expected proceeds from continued operations (Schary, 1991). The leverage ratio is defined as the ratio of the book value of debt to the sum of the value of debt and equity (LEV). Though theory predicts a positive sign between a firm’s restructuring decision and financial leverage, Schary (1991) surprisingly finds a negative sign of leverage in the cotton industry. Kovenock and Phillips (1997) do discover a positive relationship between leverage and exit, but significant only in highly concentrated industries. In the trucking industry, Zingales (1998) also finds a positive effect of leverage.

For downsizing, there are also mixed empirical results for the leverage variable. Ofek (1993) shows in a sample of US firms that restructuring measures, such as employee layoffs, are affected by capital structure. The latter finding confirms the argument by Jensen (1986) that highly leveraged firms will respond faster to a decline in firm value. Ofek (1993) reports a positive effect of leverage, while Kang and Shivdasani (1997) find a negative sign for a group of Japanese firms. Whereas the latter two empirical studies use a sample of firms that experienced a substantial decline in operating performance, our sample consists of firms reporting a layoff regardless of their profitability. In order to isolate the effect of leverage of firms in financial distress we construct a binary variable DIS which takes a value of 1 if a firm has a negative ROE in the year preceding the collective layoff and interact DIS with the leverage variable (LEV).

The learning theory of firms’ growth argues that young firms grow faster if they learn through market forces that they started with the right structure and their performance compares well with existing competitors. However, many young firms find out the contrary, and therefore will face more pressure to restructure than more experienced firms. We include the firm’s age (AGE) for experience.

3.2 Product differentiation and scale economies

As competitive pressure may be assumed less severe in industries characterized by product differentiation, the probability to restructure may on average be lower. We include the industry variable PRODIF which is proxied by the intra-industry trade index of Grubel and Lloyd (1975). The G-L index at the industry level (Marvel and Ray, 1987) can be defined as follows: G Li ¼ 2min(Xi, Mi)/(Xi þ Mi), where Xi equals total exports in industry i and Mi total imports in industry i. Many studies found the last variable to correlate strongly with product differentiation (Helpman, 1987; Bergstrand, 1990), though it may also be an indication of exposure to international competition.

With economies of scale present, volume advantages gain importance, and firms will be more likely to prefer to relocate total production to a location with lower production cost than to downsize and/or fragment its current operations. Downsizing would lower the effective output of the firm such that economies of scale at the plant or firm level would be lost. The variable for minimum efficient scale (MES) is defined as the log of the median size of firms in an industry. Instead of MES, Haynes et al. (2000) employ industry concentration, which should be highly correlated with MES, as explanatory variable and find a negative effect of the variable on divestment.

3.3 Size and labor-intensity

In accordance with the basic theoretical results, larger firms facing the need to restructure will choose to relocate part of their activity or to downsize instead of exiting from the market. As a consequence, we expect firm size to have a negative impact on exit and a positive impact on downsizing and relocation. We include the firm’s value added as a proxy of the SIZE of the firm.

Through its proximity to a mass market and its relatively high wage costs, Belgium has established a comparative advantage in capital-intensive industries (Tharakan and Waelbroeck, 1988; Sleuwaegen and De Backer, 2001). The variable C/L is a measure of the capital to labor ratio. Following our theoretical model, a low capital to labor ratio should increase the probability to relocate.

3.4 Recent investments

Investments in capital constitute a barrier to exit and downsizing, because of the important costs that are sunk in specific plant and equipment and intangible investments (Caves and Porter, 1976). The latter aspect may be better reflected by the variable RINV, measuring recent investments in capacity through the acquisitions of tangible assets. RINV has been hypothesized to limit the firm’s ability to restructure. The higher the amount of recent investments, the higher will be the adjustments costs to restructure, especially in the case of the exit mode. The more recently the

firm has invested in resources, the less depreciated the resource base, and thus the higher the resale price required to make exit more attractive than continued operation (Harrigan, 1980). Especially investment in plant and equipment involving important sunk costs would render the downsizing or exit decision less attractive (Hopenhayn, 1992; Lambson, 1991). More recently, Dixit (1989) and Dixit and Pindyck (1994) demonstrated that in exploiting the option value of waiting the extent and timing of exit depends crucially on sunk costs and the degree of uncertainty about future profit opportunities. Lacking good alternative opportunities, firms will persist to operate even if profitability is low, causing a special type of hysteresis.

Clearly, not all firms have the same option to relocate capacity to another country, especially when setting up a foreign plant involves substantial sunk costs. We, therefore, expect those firms that already operate a multinational network to benefit from this location flexibility in reducing costs. Since the adjustment cost to relocate for those firms is strongly lowered by their operating flexibility, we assume that especially multinational firms will prefer relocation to downsizing or exit. The fact that they compare costs comparatively across the different plants makes also reported profitability of one production unit in a particular location, often influenced by transfer pricing techniques, a less good performance measure (Kogut and Kulatilaka, 1994; Pennings and Sleuwaegen, 2000). The dummy variable MNE distinguishes multinational firms from firms without a multinational production network (MNE ¼ 0).

4. Data

In Belgium every firm with more than twenty employees needs to report a dismissal of more than 10% of the employees (a so-called collective layoff) at the Federal Government. Subsequently, the Federal Planning Bureau (FPB) in Belgium sends a questionnaire to the three Belgium unions. In the questionnaire it is asked if the layoff is motivated by relocation. This way there is a very good sample of all restructuring firms, the mode of restructuring, and the number of job losses involved. Between 1990 and 1999 a total number of 861 firms reported an amount of 1218 collective layoffs. At least one questionnaire was answered by 659 firms reporting a total of 827 collective layoffs. These numbers induce a 77% response rate at firm level and a 68% response rate for each collective layoff.

Within the sample exit, relocation, and downsizing account for 10%, 21%, and 69% of total job destruction, respectively. Though the figures do not include all restructuring within the sample period, it gives some intuition of the relative importance of the different modes of restructuring. Considering the impact of product differentiation, the results in Table 1 show that PRODIF has the expected sign. The strong negative effect of product differentiation on the decision to relocate, suggests the importance of specific market conditions in the location decision. The possibility of occupying a strategic niche in the market requires close market contact and renders a pure efficiency seeking strategy through relocation less attractive. Likewise, product differentiation leads to less downsizing, in line with the previous reasoning.

As a next step, as to test the validity of the model across developed and developing countries, we restrict the sample of relocating firms to those firms that move activities to low wage countries (Eastern Europe, North Africa, East Asia). The number of relocations to developing countries equals 38 out of the original sample of 105 firms. The results from the multinomial regression with the more narrow definition of relocation (Table 3), show that, in contrast with the previous table, size is a positive and significant determinant of the relocation versus downscaling decision. The intuition behind this significant difference is that firms relocating to low wage countries emphasize volume advantages in the relocation decision, in line with the theoretical model. So, whereas we previously rejected the hypothesis in Proposition 1 about the size effect, we now cannot reject the hypothesis. There are no major changes in the empirical estimates for the other variables, supporting the robustness of the results across countries.

6. Conclusions

Pro-active policies to deal with the negative consequences of collective employee layoffs necessitate a better understanding of the organizational and contextual features that influence the strategic decision process concerning relocation and downsizing of firms. The model and results presented in this study constitute an important step in helping to better understand and predict what firms will likely need to restructure and what mode of restructuring (relocation, downsizing, exit) will be chosen. We find the decision to restructure and layoff employees to relate strongly to industry wide and firm-specific features, including among others, the technological possibilities to work in a more capital-intensive way, the degree of product differentiation in the industry, the existence and experience with operating in multinational networks. For instance, firms having a well established multinational network will relocate sooner if profits start to decline than firms lacking such a network. All our results could be expanded in such an interpretative way, and lead to specific predictions concerning the likelihood of choosing among the alternative restructuring modes.

More specifically, the empirical results show that especially manufacturing firms characterized by a low profit performance and that are highly financially leveraged show a higher probability to restructure than other firms. Product differentiation

Interpreting these results, we believe that our modeling approach has uncovered important antecedents influencing the strategic downsizing, relocation and exit process, in a way that they can be usefully integrated in a strategic decision making framework. A shortcoming in adopting our quantitative approach is that we sometimes had to use summary measures, e.g. poor financial performance, which in turn depends on a wide set of environmental variables, including the growth and degree of competition in the industry. Further research may refine these dimensions to uncover specific contextual drivers explaining the observed performance. We also believe that a qualitative research approach, in particular the development of specific case studies searching for evidence of the way in which, or in other words, ‘how’ relocation, downsizing and exit decisions are taken, could strengthen and refine our results.
