Using a longitudinal survey that followed a cohort of recently hired scientists and engineers at a multinational oil and gas company from 2012–2016, we find that a major reduction-in-force during our study resulted in significant gender disparities in firm departures. Results indicate that about seventy percent of the gender gap in departures can be explained by underlying gender differences in the formal layoff criteria and workers’ experiences in the firm prior to layoffs, pointing to formal organizational processes as insidious mechanisms driving inequality. Results suggest that the firm’s performance rating system may provide a pathway through which women’s relational disadvantages became formalized in seemingly bureaucratically neutral ways. Our findings illustrate how increasing insecurity in professional careers may disadvantage women and shed light on the organizational mechanisms that reproduce gender inequality in professional work today.

Introduction

Since the 1970s, globalization, technological advancements, and deregulation have weakened job security for US workers. The standard employment relationship has eroded in the face of employers’ desires for cost reduction and flexibility in the new economy (Hacker 2008; Kalleberg 2009). For many US firms, mass layoffs, downsizing, and corporate restructuring have become standard organizational practices (Kalleberg 2009). Professional jobs once epitomized stability and security, yet professional workers are not immune from this increased uncertainty.

Growing insecurity poses implications for gender inequality in the labor market, which tenaciously persists even among professional workers. Recent studies suggest that career instability exacerbates gender inequality (Patterson, Damaske, and Sheroff 2017) and that layoffs can decrease women’s representation in management (Kalev 2014). As professional jobs grow more precarious, layoffs present an opportunity to investigate how instability reproduces gender inequality in high-status occupations. Layoffs are an ideal case for examining how structural conditions of insecurity may disparately impact women’s careers, as a focus on mass exits driven by macroeconomic conditions circumvents individualistic explanations that women are choosing to leave professional jobs (see Stone 2008).

Research has shown that effects of layoffs on gender inequality vary across firms depending on layoff rules and other organizational procedures (Kalev 2014). Organizational contexts shape the effects of downsizing on gender inequality because layoffs occur at the firm level. Gender disparities in layoffs could be due to underlying differences in worker characteristics relevant to layoff determinations (Kalev 2014) or outright discrimination at the point of layoffs (see Elvira and Zatzick 2002; but see Petersen and Saporta 2004). Investigating the organizational mechanisms that contribute to gender disparities in layoffs presents a challenge because scholars can rarely observe the layoff process as it unfolds.

In this article, we investigate organizational mechanisms of gender inequality through a case study of layoffs, using a longitudinal survey that followed a cohort of all recently hired scientists and engineers at a major oil and gas company from 2012–2016. Over the course of the study, oil prices fell by almost 80 percent, and the company went through multiple rounds of mass layoffs in which they cut nearly half of their technical workforce. We find that a large portion of our cohort left the company during layoffs, but women left at significantly higher rates: almost half of the women left, compared with around one-quarter of the men. Focusing on a cohort of relatively homogenous professional workers hired at the same time effectively holds constant common individualized explanations for disparities in layoffs, such as job marginalization or seniority, and provides an ideal case for investigating organizational explanations. Our study builds on and complements Kalev’s (2014) organizational approach to managerial job loss, examining how organizational processes and layoff rules shape individual outcomes among non-managerial professional workers. Our findings elucidate the organizational mechanisms underpinning women’s continued disadvantages in professional careers.

Background

Organizational Mechanisms of Gender Inequality

Sociologists have long recognized that workplaces play a crucial role in generating economic and social inequality. Firms link the macro and micro dimensions of work: societal forces affect workers through organizational processes, and firms’ actions impact their broader environments (Baron and Bielby 1980). An extensive tradition of organizational scholarship argues that organizations are infused with the power hierarchies of their historical and cultural contexts that generate inequality (Acker 2006; Dalton 1959; Kanter 1977a; Roscigno 2011; Tomaskovic-Devey and Avent-Holt 2019).

This view forms the basis of Acker’s (2006, 443) concept of organizations as inequality regimes, which she defines as “loosely interrelated practices, processes, actions, and meanings that result in and maintain class, gender, and racial inequalities within particular organizations.” Gender inequalities within a firm result from actions and interactions of individuals in the organization, but the characteristics of the organization influence the nature and extent of disparities by structuring relational processes of inequality (Gorman and Mosseri 2019; Reskin 2003). As organizational structures have evolved and diversified, identifying sources of inequality that persist within organizations is key for understanding gender inequality in the modern labor market, where organizational inequalities can easily translate into broader inequalities in career trajectories in the context of increased job insecurity.

Theories of relational inequality conceptualize an organization as a bounded network of social relations (Tomaskovic-Devey 2014; Tomaskovic-Devey and Avent-Holt 2019). Relational explanations of inequality focus on how inequalities arise out of relations between people, through categorical distinctions connected to status and power (Ridgeway and Correll 2004; Roscigno 2011; Tomaskovic-Devey 2014). Gender status beliefs influence behavior and evaluations in contexts where gender is effectively salient, such as where actors differ in sex category or the activities involved are linked to stereotypic traits and abilities of a specific gender (Ridgeway 1997; Ridgeway and Correll 2004). Organizational contexts where gender is effectively salient may marginalize women in terms of whose voices are heard or sought out, who receives credit, and how responsibilities are apportioned—which can influence workers’ behaviors and perceptions in ways that legitimize gender disparities (see Byron and Roscigno 2014).

Organizational Characteristics Organizational characteristics shape relational inequality by framing the choices available to actors and moderating the influence of preferences and biases (Gorman and Mosseri 2019). Formal characteristics represent how an organization is supposed to function, whereas informal characteristics shape the actual dayto-day operation. Formal organizational processes may be the most insidious mechanisms contributing to gender inequality in the workplace because they are cloaked with the veneer of objectivity.

An organization’s formal structure delineates how work is defined and coordinated (Scott and Davis 2015). One source of gender inequality in an organization’s formal structure is the division of labor, which can segregate women and men into different types of work. Some studies suggest that the more fluid allocation of tasks within teams can benefit women (Kalev 2009; Smith-Doerr 2004), but others find that teamwork can obscure individual efforts and prevent women from receiving credit for their contributions (Heilman and Haynes 2005; Williams, Muller, and Kilanski 2012). Another aspect of formal structure that can influence gender inequality is an organization’s rules and practices; if rules provide clear guidelines and expectations, work processes are less subject to dynamics that disadvantage women. For instance, ambiguity regarding performance expectations and evaluation criteria can leave room for gender to influence evaluator’s perceptions (Heilman 2001), and “exceptions” to rules can be applied in gendered ways (Smith-Doerr et al. 2019). Further, rules outlining supervisors’ behavior can prevent supervisors from favoring workers with whom they have closer connections, which can disadvantage women when supervisors are predominantly men (Roth and Sonnert 2011; Neely 2018).

Informal characteristics include the organizational culture and informal structure. The informal structure consists of patterns of behavior and interactions among people in the organization, whereas organizational culture is a set of shared beliefs and values. The gender composition of a firm’s workforce shapes gender inequality within the organization through both its informal structure and culture (Gorman and Mosseri 2019). The Case of Layoffs

Layoffs are key employment outcomes in increasingly precarious professional careers and can improve our understanding of how economic instability reproduces gender inequality (Kalev 2014). Layoffs not only have an obvious effect on workers’ individual career trajectories, but they also have repercussions for gender inequality within an organization and broader industry. Second, layoffs can cause subtle organizational gender biases and preferences to become salient because they compress processes of inequality that may otherwise only lead to perceptible gender disparities over time. The compression of these processes occurs because the institutional logic guiding layoffs underlies other areas of career development, as well. However, in contrast to organizational processes that have repeated tournaments (Rosenbaum 1979)—such as raises, promotions, or performance evaluations—layoffs represent a single winner-takes-all contest in which inequalities congeal at one point in time rather than accumulating across workers’ careers (see Gorman and Kmec 2009). Though a wealth of scholarship focuses on gender inequality in these tournament processes (see, e.g., Castilla 2008; Gorman and Kmec 2009; Smith-Doerr et al. 2019), mass layoffs provide a stark view of the consequences of systemic gender inequality and a discrete outcome for identifying organizational mechanisms.

Sources of Gender Disparities in Layoffs Gender disparities in layoffs within an organization may arise from two general sources: (1) discrimination at the point of layoffs and/or (2) preexisting gender differences in workers’ characteristics and experiences that disadvantage women in the layoff determination process. Discrimination can be difficult to identify because it generally transpires in contexts where it can remain obscured, or where employers have the greatest opportunity to discriminate (Petersen and Saporta 2004). For instance, Elvira and Zatzick’s (2002) study of a financial firm revealed racial disparities in layoffs and performance ratings but not wages or promotions, suggesting that inequalities depended on the managerial discretion and accountability involved in employment decisions. In our study, workers only received severance pay if they signed a termination agreement that included a

A gender gap in layoffs but not other indicators of career progression does not necessarily imply that individual biases of decision-makers are seeping into layoff decisions; disparities could also reflect the workings of a gendered organization (Acker 1990). For diversity and regulatory purposes, large companies generally monitor and standardize determinations related to wages or promotions to ensure decisions do not disparately impact women. Thus, even if women have worse experiences than men in the firm, relative equality in observable indicators of career progression could mask organizational processes that disadvantage women. Layoffs may unveil these processes by giving the firm less incentive to monitor disparate impacts (see Elvira and Zatzick 2002). In that case, women’s greater vulnerability to layoffs would be a product of the inequality regime rather than individual biases in layoff determinations.

In making layoff determinations, firms generally seek to reduce payroll burdens while retaining employees that will be valuable to ongoing business, and firms are increasingly using formal layoff rules to reduce legal exposure. The effectiveness of formalization in reducing inequality is not entirely clear (Dobbin, Schrage, and Kalev 2015; Elvira and Zatzick 2002; Kalev 2014; Reskin and McBrier 2000), and bias can persist in ostensibly fair procedures that can be used to justify discriminatory decisions (Byron and Roscigno 2014). Biased formalization will result in gender disparities if a firm’s layoff rules ignore underlying gender differences in the formal layoff criteria (Kalev 2014). Formal layoff rules tend to be based on individual measures of performance or workers’ structural attributes, such as position or tenure.

How formalization affects gender inequality may vary depending on organizational characteristics. If men are disproportionately the gatekeepers of local resources, they may engage in opportunity hoarding that benefits in-group members (men) and limits opportunities for out-groups (women) (Tomaskovic-Devey 2014). The salience of informal gendered processes may be exacerbated by rules that limit actors’ discretion, as decision-makers rebel against threats to autonomy (Dobbin, Schrage, and Kalev 2015). Further, formalization may matter less in smaller firms with flatter network organizations, as greater flexibility provides more routes to advancement and greater transparency provides natural accountability to organizational outsiders (Smith-Doerr 2004).

Layoff Rules at the Organization in This Study The firm in our study told workers that layoff determinations would be based on performance ratings and skill sets. Though skill set is somewhat ambiguous, a main way the company defined skill set was according to workers’ occupations and specializations. The company indicated that a high performance rating could compensate for an obsolete skill set because they were willing to retrain top performers; thus, performance might be considered the primary component. To the extent gender differences exist on these formal criteria, even an unbiased application would lead to gender disparities in layoffs.

The importance of performance ratings in the layoff rules will disadvantage women if they receive lower ratings than men on average, either due to bias in the evaluation process or systemic inequalities in work experiences that factor into evaluations. Though Kalev’s (2014) findings suggest that performance-based layoff rules may be relatively equitable, a sizeable body of research shows that women tend to receive lower performance ratings than men. In performance evaluations, women are consistently assessed as less capable and competent than men (Brewer et al. 2020; Correll et al. 2020). Women are particularly disadvantaged in male-dominated and technical fields; they are judged as less competent at male-typical tasks, and they may have to perform better than men to receive similar recognition (Cech 2013; Correll, Benard, and Paik 2007; Ridgeway 1997). If women end up performing female-typical tasks that require less technical skill, their contributions may be overlooked or devalued (Acker 1991; Fletcher 2001). A recent study showed that evaluations of women and men can also vary depending on the structure of the evaluative tool used, such as the number of categories in the rating system (Rivera and Tilcsik 2019). Even when women and men receive similar performance ratings, performance-reward bias may result in women receiving lower rewards (Castilla 2008; Joshi, Son, and Roh 2015).

To the extent workers’ skill sets are defined by occupational fields and specializations, this layoff rule effectively aligns with workers’ positions. Layoff rules based on positions generally disadvantage women due to gender segregation (Kalev 2014). The workers in our study fall into two broad occupations: geoscientists and engineers. Though both occupations are male-dominated, a higher proportion of women are geoscientists; thus, women are at greater risk of layoffs if geoscientists are more likely to lose their jobs than engineers. Gender segregation within these occupational groups in terms of specializations or across jobs and tasks might also disadvantage women (Leahey 2006; Levanon and Grusky 2016). For instance, Acker’s (1991) classic comparable worth study found that jobs objectively scored as comparable in value paid more when filled by men versus women.

Gender differences in assignments and opportunities for skill development could contribute to disparities in workers’ performance evaluations or skill sets. Compared to men, women tend to perform fewer developmental tasks (Benschop and Doorewaard 1998) and are more likely to engage in tasks that have little effect on their evaluation and career advancement (Babcock et al. 2017). Because skill development occurs through on-the-job experience, differential allocations of tasks and assignments may generate a skills gap between otherwise-similar women and men (Gorman and Kmec 2009). In fact, one study found that gender gaps in commissions among stockbrokers were the result of management assigning women inferior sales opportunities (Madden 2012). Beyond formal assignment allocations, supervisors may also steer men and women toward different career goals (Fernandez and Sosa 2005).

Considering the array of organizational processes that may contribute to higher layoff rates for women, we account for these processes to the extent possible in our analysis. The main way we can measure organizational processes

Data and Methods

Research Setting and Data Collection

Although the oil and gas industry is notorious for its sexism, in recent years it has embraced diversity as a goal, in part to address problems with retention (Williams, Kilanski, and Muller 2014). This was the case with the company in our study, a multinational oil and gas company that we call General Oil & Gas, or GOG (a pseudonym). When this study began in 2012, GOG was especially concerned about women’s attrition, a problem endemic to science careers in general (Glass et al. 2013). Frequent concerns were expressed about the impending “Great Crew Change,” or the U-shaped age distribution of the workforce characterized by many early-career hires, an aging leadership, and few mid-career scientists and engineers to step into leadership positions. Human relations professionals at the company wanted to understand how to better cultivate and retain their recent technical hires in preparation for anticipated retirements.

To identify factors associated with retention and attrition, we designed a survey to follow a cohort of scientists and engineers. Our sample was based on an email list that GOG provided of all US-based recent (last five years) geoscience and engineering hires; about 90% were early-career workers. The survey was written and administered by our research team without corporate oversight. Human resource representatives from GOG wrote an e-mail introducing us and encouraging employees to respond to the initial survey. The survey was administered five times at one-year intervals (from 2012–2016) to the cohort of scientists and engineers (population N = 360).1 By definition, all members of the cohort were college educated—the engineers at least at the bachelor’s level and the scientists at the master’s and Ph.D. levels. They earned very high salaries, putting them in the top five percent of all income earners in the United States. They were also disproportionately white; most non-white employees were not US citizens and were often from oil-rich countries (e.g., Venezuela, Nigeria).

The questionnaire included items about topics such as job satisfaction, team and supervisor experiences, performance ratings, and roles at GOG. We also included open-ended questions to learn more about respondents’ employment experiences. For employees who left the firm, we asked about their reasons for leaving and subsequent employment. The survey yielded an 81% overall response rate in our first two waves combined, resulting in a sample of about 290 respondents. (Wave 1 non-respondents who answered in Wave 2 were given

Measures for Multivariable Analyses Departure during Layoff Period. Our dependent variable is a dichotomous measure of whether respondents left GOG after Wave 3, which captures the period in which layoffs occurred. We discern who has left the company in two ways. First, respondents reported if they had left GOG at each survey wave; about 40% of respondents’ departures were ascertained through survey responses. Because response rates were higher for women overall, more of women’s departures were obtained through the survey (45%) versus men’s departures (30%). Nonrespondents are counted as having left the company if their corporate email was returned as undeliverable when we administered the survey wave (indicating that they were no longer at the company). We cross-referenced determinations with information from online sources such as LinkedIn. About 60% of departures were ascertained through returned corporate emails; we still attempted to contact all respondents using alternate emails or websites such as LinkedIn.

Because most departures are captured through undeliverable emails, we do not know why every person left GOG. However, a few things give us confidence that our outcome reflects involuntary layoffs. First, we limit our measure of layoffs to employees who left during the mass layoff period. For the subset of departures captured through survey responses, all respondents said they left through the layoff process and received severance. Among this subset, about 75% characterized their layoff as involuntary. Part of the layoff process was that workers could volunteer for layoffs prior to determinations. Management still had to approve voluntary layoffs; if workers left outside of the layoff process, they would not receive severance pay. Subsequent employment rates were similar regardless of how respondents characterized the voluntariness of their layoff, with less than 30% reporting new employment within our survey period. This underscores the universally poor employment prospects across the industry during the downturn and suggests that most workers who left voluntarily were not leaving for other opportunities but may have been pushed out by layoff conditions.

Interactional Dynamics and Value to Organization. In addition to the formal layoff criteria, we assess workers’ experiences at GOG prior to the layoff period to shed light on organizational processes that might contribute to gender differences in layoffs. We include indicators that relate to two aspects of workers’ experiences that could influence layoff determinations. The second aspect is value to the organization, which we measure using workers’ career development, experience in supervisory positions,and how central their teams are to generating profit. Additional Measures for Descriptive Analyses

We include information on several indicators of career progression at GOG, to assess how they compare to layoffs. All measures are based on workers’ selfreports. We have measures of salary and promotions, which are supposed to be based on a standardized scale/schedule. The number of promotions indicates promotions that occurred between waves 1 and 3 (range of 0-2). We also have a measure of perceived career progression, which is a categorical indicator of whether a worker’s career has progressed slower than average, about average, or faster than average compared with their peers. Finally, we report basic demographic and work characteristics for the sample, including age, race, family structure, and tenure at GOG. For parsimony, we do not include these measures in our models; ancillary analyses indicate that including these measures does not affect our results.

Analytic Plan

We investigate organizational mechanisms of gender inequality at GOG through a series of analyses that describe gender differences among workers and assess their contribution to gender disparities in layoffs. First, we examine patterns in men’s and women’s rates of departure from GOG before and during the layoff period. Next, we compare men and women descriptively to assess gender differences in layoff criteria, worker experiences, and career progression prior to layoffs. We then estimate logistic regressions predicting job departure during the layoff period, focusing on the gender gap. We conduct logistic regressions rather than survival analyses because we do not have departure dates for most workers. We evaluate possible mechanisms by first including each independent variable in separate logistic regression models and then combining all measures in the full model. In addition, we estimate Kitagawa-Blinder-Oaxaca3 (KBO) decompositions based on each regression model. Our final statistical analysis assesses the unique contribution of each measure to the gender gap in departures using a detailed KBO decomposition. Throughout the statistical analyses, we discuss the open-ended survey responses to elucidate possible organizational processes.

For our primary analyses, we restrict our sample to workers employed at GOG when the oil bust occurred, as they would have been exposed to layoffs. This drops approximately thirty respondents who left GOG prior to Wave 3, resulting in a final analytic sample of about 260 workers. We handled missing data on independent variables with multiple imputation using chained equations (m = 10 imputations). Rates of missing were 5% or below on all independent variables other than performance ratings, which had 8% missing. Results are consistent using single imputation or listwise deletion. All analyses include probability weights that account for selection into the analytic sample and the aggregation of multiple waves of data. First, these weights account for women’s slightly higher response rates. To better estimate the effect of the economic downturn on departures, the weights also account for workers’ propensity of leaving GOG prior to the downturn (based on key demographic characteristics, job satisfaction, and performance ratings at Wave 1). Finally, the weights account for the fact that most measures are averages across waves, such that respondents with more waves of data contribute more information to the estimates.4 Unweighted analyses yield similar results.

Gender Differences in Job Departures before and during Layoffs

Figure 1a shows how the gender composition of the cohort at GOG changed over our survey period and multiple rounds of layoffs (population N = 360); for comparison, figure 1b shows a graph for survey respondents only (n = 290). The price of oil was high ($110/barrel) when we began the study in 2012, and it remained high for the first few years. By Wave 4, in 2015, oil prices had dropped by over half (below $50/barrel). A 10% reduction in professional technical staff at GOG occurred that spring, and an additional 15% reduction was scheduled for the fall. In 2016 (Wave 5), oil prices dropped to their twenty-year nadir, and two additional rounds of layoffs occurred at GOG.By the last wave of our survey, almost 45% of workers in the cohort had left GOG. The cohort already had a high proportion of men when our study began, reflecting the male-dominated nature of the oil industry and physical science occupations. Mass layoffs left the cohort even less diverse, reducing the proportion of women workers from 28% to 23% over a relatively short period of time.

In figure 2, we break down employment outcomes, to compare gender differences in job departures prior to and during the layoff period. Looking at the full cohort population, we see that men and women left GOG at similar rates during good economic conditions (∼12%), but a significantly greater proportion of women left during the layoff period (45% vs. 27%). This suggests that layoffs, specifically, are driving the gendered retention patterns in figure 1. Patterns are nearly identical among survey respondents. For our main analyses, we exclude the roughly ten percent of respondents who left GOG prior to the oil bust and focus only on workers at risk of layoffs.

We begin our investigation of the gender gap in departures during the layoff period by assessing gender differences in the layoff criteria, worker experiences, and career progression prior to layoffs. Table 2 presents weighted bivariate statistics for workers in our sample, highlighting significant differences between men and women. In table 2, gender differences on the formal layoff criteria point to biased formalization as a likely source of gender inequality in layoffs; even assuming an objective application of these basic criteria, we would expect a gender gap in layoffs. Because performance ratings were based on a personnel ranking policy, the requirements for receiving a high rating were nebulous, as illustrated by one woman’s response:

I met or exceeded every expectation in my performance goals and . . . was ranked a 4 [low]. My supervisor was very candid that this is a result of the forced ranking policy.

The open-ended responses provide additional insight into the relationships between formal and informal organizational processes and how these dynamics might disadvantage women. One common theme in workers’ responses was that performance ratings were too dependent on teams—who was on your team, who your supervisor was, and how assignments were apportioned among team members. Considering that women report worse relationships with supervisors and poorer career development, the importance of these factors in the performance ratings process may partially explain why women generally received lower ratings. The experiences of workers at GOG reveal how teamwork can harm women under some circumstances because teams essentially constitute smaller bounded networks of social relations within organizations, generating hyper-localized relational inequalities. The more fluid division of labor and the within-team performance rankings system meant that a worker’s opportunities and rewards were apportioned in relation to other members of their team and at the discretion of their supervisor; therefore, the identities and interactions of the people on a team influenced how equitably these processes operated.

Workers noted that supervisors varied in how often they gave promotions and how active they were in helping workers advance. Analysis of the Gender Gap in Job Departures during Layoffs

How much of the gender gap in departures during layoffs can be explained by gender differences in the layoff criteria and other pre-layoff characteristics and experiences? Table 4 presents average marginal effects (AMEs) from logistic regressions predicting departures during the layoff period. The AMEs can be interpreted as an average change in the probability of departure; for example, the baseline model shows that women had about an 18 percentage point higher probability of leaving than men (P(departure | women)-P(departure | men) = AME [.44-.26 = .18]). Considering correlations among the predictors, we assess each measure individually before presenting the full model. In addition to the AMEs, each model includes the percentage of the gender gap explained in a KBO decomposition. Models 2 and 3 show that the formal layoff criteria significantly predict workers’ departure during layoffs. Geoscientists have a 15 percentage point higher probability of leaving compared with engineers, and one point worse on the performance ratings scale translates into a 22 percentage point higher probability of leaving. Job field does not explain a significant portion of the gender gap, whereas women’s lower performance ratings alone explain about 45% of the gap. Among other measures of worker characteristics and experiences, women’s worse relationships with supervisors (Model 4) and poorer career development (Model 6) each explain a significant portion of the gender gap in departures.

Together, the measures explain about 70% of the gender gap in Model 9, indicating that pre-layoff gender differences translated into gender disparities in departures during layoffs. Among these measures, performance ratings and job field are the most objective indicators we have of the formal layoff criteria. Because skill set is an ambiguous criterion, it is unclear how management may have used that ambiguity to differentiate between workers with similar performance and job fields. Thus, the other measures could have contributed to the gender gap in layoffs through their correlation with performance or job field, or by further distinguishing between workers in layoff determinations.

Discussion

Despite strengthening individual and corporate commitments to diversity and gender equality in the United States, women continue to fare worse than men in professional careers. We argue that layoffs present an opportunity to investigate the organizational mechanisms that reproduce gender inequality by compressing and revealing gendered organizational processes. An intense period of layoffs during our longitudinal study of STEM professionals at an oil and gas company allows us to not only document gendered patterns in layoffs but also speak to possible organizational processes driving the patterns. We find that women left the company at significantly higher rates than men during the layoff period, and gender differences in workers’ pre-layoff characteristics and experiences account for most of the gender gap in departures. Our findings point to formal organizational processes as insidious mechanisms of inequality, and workers’ open-ended responses suggest explanations consistent with theories of relational inequality as to how the formal rules and structure of work may reify gender inequalities in the firm’s informal structure and organizational culture.

We find evidence of biased formalization (Kalev 2014), such that formal layoff criteria perpetuate gender inequality due to underlying gender disparities on relevant employment characteristics. Women’s lower performance ratings, poorer career development, and concentration in a lower-status field explain about 75 percent of the gender gap in layoffs, suggesting that the layoffs largely reveal existing biases in organizational processes at GOG. The importance of supervisors to performance ratings and performance-related processes such as assignments and skill development highlights a possible pathway through which women’s relational disadvantages might become formalized in seemingly bureaucratically neutral ways.

These findings suggest that male supervisors may have engaged in opportunity hoarding to give other men better opportunities or ratings despite—or perhaps,

because of—the rigid rules governing performance evaluations at GOG (see Dobbin et al. 2015; Tomaskovic-Devey 2014). Though women reported similar team experiences to men, the male-dominated teams and attendant dynamics held gendered consequences for career development and the personnel ranking system. The team structure, power given to supervisors, and importance of performance ratings for advancement in the context of a large bureaucratic corporation meant there was little chance for the flexibility or transparency identified by Smith-Doerr (2004) as key components of gender equity in biotech firms.

Research on layoffs’ effects on managerial diversity has found that rules based on performance evaluations are relatively equitable toward women (Kalev 2014). Our study presents contrasting findings among non-managerial workers, showing that gender inequalities in performance assessments account for much of the gender gap in layoffs among a cohort of STEM professionals. The firm in our study espoused a commitment to diversity; they wanted to recruit and retain more women workers and had formal procedures to mitigate gender disparities in career progression. Layoffs may have revealed the limits to this commitment, allowing gender inequalities with potentially long-reaching consequences for workforce diversity under the guise of corporate necessity. However, without this commitment to diversity during normal economic conditions, gender disparities may have been even worse.

The patterns we find in gender disparities across work outcomes are consistent with Elvira and Zatzick’s (2002) findings regarding patterns of racial inequality: disparities in layoffs and performance, but not wages or promotions. However, parity in wages and promotions at GOG was masking gender differences in workers’ experiences; women reported worse relationships with their supervisors, poorer career development, and slower perceived career progression. Thus, the organizational processes driving gender inequality within the firm were always there, but they only translated into observable gender disparities in outcomes during layoffs. The layoff process may have provided a distinct opportunity for organizational biases to emerge, as workers could not complain of disparate treatment if they wanted to receive their much-needed severance packages.

We can explain much of the gender gap in departures, but we cannot know how GOG management made individual layoff decisions. The gender differences on variables in our data could reflect observable distinctions between workers on relevant layoff criteria, which may have been applied objectively or used as post hoc justifications for discriminatory decisions. Further, some measures could simply be capturing systemic sexism within the organization. Though our KBO decomposition apportions the gender gap among variables in our model, it cannot establish causality and can be sensitive to model specification. Ancillary analyses indicate that the portion of the gap attributed to the layoff criteria is robust to a host of demographic and career indicators, but we cannot account for characteristics and processes outside the scope of our data. Deciphering exactly how women’s organizational disadvantages translate into layoffs is not particularly important in the context of this study; rather, that these

disadvantages translate into layoffs suggests that layoff patterns largely reflect systemic gender inequality within the firm. To the extent that systemic inequality resulted in gender differences on the formal layoff criteria, these differences imbue women’s higher layoff rates with a false sense of legitimacy.

Though this study focused on layoffs, our findings may shed light on organizational mechanisms underlying gender disparities in long-term career trajectories because layoffs compress processes of inequality. Layoffs reflect the local inequality regime and make gender differences within the organization salient at a discrete point in time, as opposed to disparities manifesting slowly and cumulatively across workers’ careers. The patterns in layoffs indicate who is vulnerable at decision points and who is likely to succeed within the organization by revealing organizational biases and preferences. Our study presents a unique case for examining gender inequality because many mechanisms that contribute to gender disparities in career progression are effectively “held constant.” The individuals in the cohort are all highly skilled, professional STEM workers; were hired by the same company within a five-year period; and have similar demographic characteristics. Yet, we still find significant gender differences among our homogenous sample—which suggests organizational, rather than individual, explanations for these differences.

Our findings also speak to issues of gender inequality within STEM occupations specifically. Despite efforts to increase women’s representation in STEM, recent research has highlighted the persistent paucity of women at high levels (Cech and Blair-Loy 2010) and high exit rate of women from STEM professions, especially engineering (Glass et al. 2013; Hunt 2016). In contrast to explanations of women leaving voluntarily or being pushed out of STEM professions, our findings implicate layoffs as a mechanism contributing to women’s long-term attrition. The gender composition of our cohort dropped from 28% to 23% women, suggesting that job instability may help explain the dearth of women in high-status positions in the oil and gas industry (see Eagly 2007). The workers who remained at the company will likely fill management positions in the future, but GOG disproportionately culled its already small pool of women candidates (see Kalev 2014).

This study employed longitudinal data on a cohort of professional workers within a single firm. This narrow focus avoided confounding issues related to occupational and tenure disparities, but our cohort did not represent all workers at GOG or even all professional workers. Ancillary analyses indicate that some gender differences were more pronounced among engineers than geoscientists, so patterns of gender inequalities may vary depending on organizational position. Research using a broader sample of workers can illuminate how organizational processes of inequality vary across positions. In addition, we may be capturing a time in workers’ careers when gender differences in performance ratings are particularly large. Status characteristics may influence performance ratings more among workers with a shorter history in the firm. This is also when many workers are having children, so parental leave or responsibilities may differentially impact men’s and women’s work experiences. Future research might examine

how parenthood shapes gender disparities in layoffs or whether the sources and extent of organizational gender inequality vary for older workers.

Sample size limited our analysis, but we still found striking and consistent gender differences among this relatively small cohort of workers. However, our sample contained only a small number of racial and ethnic minorities, so our findings mostly relate to white women’s disadvantages and vulnerability to layoffs. Future research with a larger or more diverse sample is needed to build intersectional understanding of gendered processes, as previous research has highlighted the complex nature of inequality in STEM careers (Alegria 2019; Alegria and Branch 2015). Our measure of layoffs was limited by our inability to discern the reason for most departures. Available data suggested that most layoffs were involuntary, and management had to approve voluntary layoffs; thus, we believe the patterns are driven by firm decisions. Even if we could distinguish voluntary layoffs, workers had to volunteer before determinations were made; it would be impossible to know whether they would have been laid off involuntarily. If volunteers were less likely to be laid off involuntarily, then our results may be conservative in terms of the explanatory power of the layoff criteria.

Our focus on layoffs adds an important dimension of gender inequality to existing literature and demonstrates how companies may ultimately undermine women’s career trajectories. Layoffs provide a glimpse at organizational mechanisms that perpetuate gender inequality and illustrate how increasing insecurity in professional careers may disadvantage women. The findings in this study suggest that periods of corporate downsizing can reveal the organizational processes that reproduce gender inequality—processes that may remain veiled during “boom” times.
