An Empirical Research on the Institutional Theory of Downsizing: Evidence from MNC’s Subsidiary Companies in Taiwan

CHENG-FEI TSAI, SHU-LING WU,† HSING-KUO WANG† & ING-CHUNG HUANG‡

Department of International Business, Wenzao Ursuline College of Language, Taiwan; †Institute of Human Resource Management, National Sun Yat-sen University, Taiwan; ‡National Kaohsiung University, Taiwan

ABSTRACT The research uses 18 business cases to verify the institutional theory of downsizing (McKinley et al., 2000). It shows that the rationale behind enterprises’ downsizing practices is mixed with economic factors, institutional factors and social cognition processes. Moreover, the influences of institutionalization appear not only on the motivation of downsizing, but also on the practices used by enterprises to execute the downsizing strategies. The implications are: institutionalization would influence the entire process of downsizing and the future research should try to make the institutional theory of downsizing more concrete and complete. Meanwhile, the findings are useful references for enterprises and employees when dealing with the labour relations when downsizing, especially for the global HRM strategies in MNCs.

KEY WORDS: Downsizing, institutionalization

Introduction

Research Motivation

Studies have revealed that downsizing is not an absolute positive and beneficial practice to enterprises, and it usually ends up harming thousands of employees and causing social problems. But why it has still remained a popular practice since the 1980s. Quite a lot of studies have explained the motivation of downsizing from the economic point of view (e.g. Greenhalgh et al., 1988; Cascio, 1993; Cameron, 1994; Cascio et al., 1997; Rigby, 2002). It is puzzling that since enterprises have known that downsizing is not necessarily advantageous to their companies, why do they insist on applying the practice? McKinley et al. (1995)

have explained this by using institutional theory to declare that three social forces from outside organizations make enterprises downsize even though they know the expected benefits of downsizing are not remarkable. The three institutional forces are constraining, cloning (imitation) and learning. Since then, there has been some research proving that the institutional factor is one of the motivations making enterprises downsize (Budros, 1999; Lamertz & Baum, 1998; Mentzer, 1996; Norman, 1995). McKinley et al. (2000) have further explained managers’ psychological processes in deciding the downsizing practices from the viewpoint of socio-cognition. They recognized that apart from economic and institutional factors and after going through the social cognition process, managers have considered that downsizing must be a legitimate and ethically acceptable management practice. Managers have widely accepted the idea that downsizing has been institutionalized as an efficient and inevitable management practice. McKinley et al. have considered that the institutional theory of downsizing would conclude: (1) the rationale behind downsizing decisions would derive from economic factors, institutional factors and social cognition. Some enterprises consider one single factor, some may consider two (for example, both economic and institutional factors), and some all three factors; (2) the downsizing decision made considering these three factors occurs mostly in the post-industrial societies in western countries.

The theoretical viewpoints from McKinley et al. are rather meaningful because they provide more explicit explanations and predictions on the motivations of downsizing. Furthermore, they help people to better understand the practice of downsizing, which is the most commonly used strategy (McKee-Ryan & Kinicki, 2002). However, no empirical research supports or modifies their ideas. In this study, we aim to find out if institutional theory of downsizing can both explain the motivation of business downsizing and the phenomena happening in the process, for example, to explore if the downsizing practices could be influenced by institutionalization. Nevertheless, due to the lack of the downsizing theories, current institutional theories of downsizing remain a theoretical concept. And the relational model of the relevant variables is still immature. Therefore, it is difficult to conduct empirical quantitative research with a big sample. So we make in-depth interviews with 18 enterprises in order to explore the possible existence of the combination of the three factors driving enterprises to practice downsizing. We also aim to verify if the existence of combined factors occurs only in the enterprise in the post-industrial societies and to find out if institutional factors also affect the downsizing strategies and related practice apart from the downsizing motivation.

Expected Contributions

Our expected findings will make the institutional theory of downsizing more solid due to the exploration of actual empirical supports and modifications. The results obtained will lead to some propositions being made for further research and we hope to motivate more research on this topic. Additionally, from the viewpoint of managerial practices, the findings also may provide enterprises with a more subjective referential framework when deciding on downsizing strategies.

Literature Review

Downsizing has been one of the consistent trends in business since the 1980s (Naumann et al., 1995; Fisher & White, 2000; McKinley et al., 2000; McKee-Ryan & Kinicki, 2002;

Landry, 2004). Every researcher has a slightly different definition concerning downsizing. In summary, downsizing is defined as: ‘In order to adjust to the environmental changes, break through management difficulties and improve organizational efficiency, promote their competitiveness and productivity, companies use different methods to reduce costs, restructure organizations, and cut down labours.’ Downsizing as the major or the last necessary resort has been widely used by both public and private sectors worldwide. It is recognized as the most effective management strategy to restructure organizations, promote organizational efficiency, and reduce organizational cost (Sun & Yeh, 1996; Sun, 1997a, b). In the past, downsizing was perceived as an indicator of organizational decline; now it is regarded as a formal strategy to restructure an organization (McKinley et al., 1995). Managers even downsize and rebuild organizations consistently in order to promote productivity and increase share prices. Even some profit-making companies, for example, Kodak, Citicorp, and Levi Strauss apply this strategy (Ellis, 1998). Downsizing cuts the number of employees deliberately (Cameron, 1994; McKinley et al., 1995). Due to its long-lasting popularity, it remains a controversial whether it is advantageous or disadvantageous to organizations and employees (Mone, 1997). The institutional theory of downsizing of McKinley et al. has placed the emphasis on the research of motivation.

Causal Factors Driving Organizational Downsizing

According to the literature, the motivations driving enterprises to downsize are different. We summarized the relevant ideas as: (a) macro-level factors (external management environment), e.g. economic depression, government policies, merger and acquisition, technological development, heavy competition and deregulation; (b) micro-level factors (internal organizational environment), e.g. financial difficulties or losses, bad performance, inappropriate HR management, organizational reconstruction, seasonal adjustments, and budget constraints. Baron & Kreps (1999) have also taken the trend of global competition into account and summarized the following factors: (a) global trends which cause the enterprises to encounter stronger competitors; (b) the shortened product life cycle forces enterprises to act on this situation; (c) increasing labour costs makes companies cut the number of employees in order to reduce HR costs; (d) due to the development of information systems, much administrative work has been automated, hence the demand for labour is reduced; (e) the arrangement for flex-work reduces the companies’ willingness to employ formal employees; and (f) shareholders’ demand for higher return on equity. From the end of the 1980s, most of the studies have shown that the organizational decline is the major reason for downsizing (Greenhalgh et al., 1988; McCune et al., 1988). Still, many enterprises have downsized for other reasons, for example to gain competitive advantages and technology upgrading (Shah, 2000). In the 1990s, many enterprises have also downsized after mergers and acquisitions in order to gain economic scale (Hirschman, 2001; Tsai, 2001). The puzzling problem is that the result of downsizing is not always what enterprises have predicted (Cascio, 1993, 2002; Cameron, 1994; Cascio et al., 1997; Rigby, 2002). There are other hidden costs of downsizing, such as the deconstruction of organizational network (Fisher & White, 2000), the negative psychological effect on the employees (Millward & Brewerton 2002), and even the erosion on the companies’ social capital (Prusak & Cohen, 2001). McKinley et al. (2000) have incorporated the institutional social factors (McKinley et al., 1995) and divided the motivation of downsizing into three categories: economic factors, institutional factors and social cognition.

Economic factors. For those organizations using efficient effective economic behaviours to gain profits and satisfy their stakeholders, their actions are naturally economically motivated. Therefore, it is not surprising to know that most of the studies since the end of the 1980s have pointed out the decline of organizations as the main reason for downsizing (Greenhalgh et al., 1988; McCune et al., 1988; Dewitt, 1993). Many researches have shown the reasons for downsizing, such as pursuing new technology, competitive advantages, organizational effectiveness, and raising share prices (Freeman & Cameron 1993; Ellis, 1998; Shah, 2000; Freeman, 1999; Hirschman, 2001; Tsai, 2001; Cascio et al., 1997; Cascio, 2002; Rigby, 2002). It is also a proactive management strategy aiming to improve corporate performance (economic benefits). Some other researchers have also analysed the most possible factors for downsizing. Drew (1994) found out the top three reasons were a decrease in sales volume and profit, unfavourable financial performance and international competition. Therefore, the economic factors are significant drivers causing downsizing.

Institutional factor, (social forces from organizational external environment). According to the open system theory, organizations tend to be influenced by the external environment and to adjust their behaviours accordingly. McKinley et al. (1995) have also provided another explanation. They proposed that downsizing was caused by three institutional external social forces: constraining, cloning (imitation), and learning. Managers implement downsizing due to the influences of social factors. This is because downsizing has been perceived as an institutionalized norm with legitimacy. The following researches have supported that institutionalization is also a reason causing downsizing (Budros, 1999; Lamertz & Baum, 1998, Mentzer, 1996; Norman, 1995). Budros (1999) pointed out that some irrational external factors, such as institutionalization, social network (e.g. certain practices have been commonly perceived as an effective method) and irrational internal factors such as organizational culture and the traits of leaders also affect the scale of downsizing.

Social cognition. McKinley et al. (2000) viewed downsizing as a process proceeding according to social cognition. From the process of social interaction, managers collectively decide on the alternatives according to the schema of downsizing and expel the conformation bias, then make a cognizable and workable strategy. Additionally, the prevailing cultures in our society are employee self-reliance and de-bureaucratization. They simply mean that organizations are continuously restructuring in dynamic environments and the practices of downsizing are effective and inevitable actions. It has been an acceptable institution and a social reality in the business ethics. The decision on the alternatives means that at the early stage in the process, managers have to evaluate the relationships between the effectiveness of downsizing and the ethical acceptability.

Therefore, the business ethic is an important element in formatting the motivation for downsizing. Although McKinley did not discuss the context of ethical acceptability, we consider that the principles of ethical behaviour1 of the individual enterprises would influence the motivation for downsizing. The legitimacy in the institutional environment is also a radical force when integrating the schema of downsizing via social interaction. Legitimacy means complying with the social norms.2 It tends to unify the alternatives and resolve the contradictory opinions in order to ensure the concreteness of schema. In other words, legitimacy will affect the companies’ decisions on schema.

Summarizing the three factors proposed by McKinley et al. (2000), we have concluded the following: (1) Enterprises conduct downsizing according to economic factors,

institutional factors and social cognition. Some mainly consider one single factor; others consider two factors (e.g. economic factors and institutional factors); and some all three; (2) the motivation driven by the three factors happened mainly in post-industrial societies in western countries. The propositions of McKinley et al. have been quite meaningful because they provide definite explanations and predictions on the companies’ motivation for downsizing. However, the propositions need sufficient proof on their accuracy. Unfortunately, there is no relevant empirical research in this area. Therefore, this research is mainly an empirical study.

Downsizing Practices

The viewpoint of institutional theory of downsizing (McKinley et al., 1995, 2000) has thoroughly explained the effects of institutionalization of downsizing as a whole. However, downsizing is a social action that comprises causes, processes and effects. Since institutionalization is a factor causing downsizing, would it also generate the effects from the process of downsizing or will institutionalization influence the types of downsizing strategy and practices? Or will enterprises take identical strategies and practices even though they have individual managerial philosophy, vision, value and the resources for downsizing? We believe that the concepts and discussions of institutional theory of downsizing should be expanded from the motives to the strategies and practices of downsizing.

Neo-institutional theory makes members obey the norms of good management and organizational structure. Members could hence reduce uncertainty and access legitimacy (McKinley et al., 2000). Therefore, if enterprises’ behaviours comply with the social expectations, they can increase their legitimacy, resources and survival ability and end up with success (Baum & Oliver, 1991; Carroll & Hannan, 1989; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Oliver, 1991, 1997a, b; Scott, 1987). Once downsizing is perceived as a social convention, managers are forced to take this option. Behaviours of isomorphism possess higher legitimacy; meanwhile they also reduce inter-firm isomorphism pressures (Oliver, 1997a) and are comparatively acceptable to internal and external stakeholders. Thus, we assume that the strategic modes and actions for downsizing should be also influenced by institutional factors.

Research Method

This research concerns complex human behaviours. Therefore, a qualitative research method is used to formulate some propositions, which can be the basis for further quantitative researches. Here, qualitative research can better distinguish the specific characteristics of HR practices and find out the actual reasons (Sherer & Leblebici, 2001).

Sample and Sources

Research targets. The research cases are selected from Taiwan, mainly because Taiwan has been an important member in the Asia Pacific Newly Developed Countries. According to the statistics from the Ministry of Economics, Taiwan’s trade volume has become one of the top 15 globally and in the top 5 from the Global Competitiveness Report of World Economic Forum in 2003. Data Collection

Data are collected using the methods of focus group interview, questionnaire, and interviews in person. We have kept the respondents in the collection process well informed, following the suggestions proposed by Gupta et al. (2000).

Critical Context and Content Exploration

First of all, we divide the main participants of downsizing practices into three focus groups: top management team, HR managers and union leaders (who are representing the employees). In the focus group interviews, we explore and summarize the critical context and content concerning downsizing. After that, we construct the encoding system from the literature review to gather data.

Questionnaire

Design. We apply the pre-set encoding system to construct the following codes: context codes, situation codes, process codes, activity codes, event codes, strategy codes, and relationship/role codes, and make up a 46-question-semi-structure-questionnaire. A space for other opinions is also reserved in the questionnaire. Some questions allow for multiple answers or adding other, more appropriate, answers. The purpose is to gather subjective data and avoid bias. Space is also provided for respondents to express their special opinions.

Pre-tests and modifications. We pre-test a company and compare their answers as a pilot survey. The pre-test is conducted to ensure interviewees understand the meanings of the questions and the methods in answering the questions. Questionnaire distribution and collection. We distribute questionnaires to the targeted companies, and collect them back in person, on the phone or via the internet.

Primary Data Summary and Deletion

The rules of deletion are set if the responses from the four respondents in the same company are different.

Rule 1. For questions related to corporate strategy or policies, the opinions of senior managers would have priority. Rule 2. For questions related to HR practices, the opinions of HR managers would have priority. Rule 3. For questions related to technology, re-engineering, work re-design, the opinions of the respective department heads would have priority. Rule 4. For the questions related to employees, the opinions of the union leaders would have priority.

Data Summary and Analysis

Context analysis and content analysis are employed to explain the downsizing strategies and actions of the 18 companies. The analyzed similarities and differences are the basis for future predictions and for constructing propositions. We analyse the motivation for downsizing from the aspects of the product life cycle, profitability, competitors’ strategies, HR strategies, HR redundancy, and the percentage of labour with needed core competency, the benchmarking comparisons, the decision-making process of downsizing, ethical principles, social obligation and corporate image etc. We also analyse the context of downsizing strategies and actions from the scale, the procedures and the selective criterion of lay-off, the compensating offerings and the alternatives. The downsizing motivation is analysed according to the Institutional Theory of Downsizing of McKinley et al. (2000). Triangulation is used to explore the motivation, downsizing modes, and the social cognition process of the top managers, HR managers and department managers.

Meanwhile, we also compare the Taiwanese subsidiaries of MNC with the local enterprises in order to explain the Institutional Theory of Downsizing in the cross-cultural contingency.

The analysis and discussion of the survey results are made in the light of the three main categories of downsizing motivation according to the Institutional Theory of Downsizing proposed by McKinley et al. (2000).

Economic factors. Many literature reviews have pointed out that companies downsize due to the eagerness to improve the financial performance, such as management effectiveness, profitability, and productivity. Table 3 summarizes the economic factors influencing downsizing decisions, including stagnant or declining profitability, new managerial models, unspecific core competency, employees without competitive advantages and long-term stagnation. Among these, stagnant and declining profitability is mentioned in all 18 cases. Even some state-owned enterprises have recognized the problem of stagnant and declining profitability and wanted to implement downsizing. Those companies that depend heavily on profitability to survive, view economic factors as the critical motivation for downsizing. For local companies and joint-ventured companies, they downsize for two reasons: employees without competitive advantages and new business models. These two reasons also correlate closely with economic performance.

Institutional factors. From the viewpoint of institutional theory, downsizing is adopted because the enterprises follow the social convention: isomorphic actions have their legitimacy. McKinley et al. (1995) proposed that there are three institutional factors affecting the execution of downsizing. They are constraining, imitation and learning. Many companies indicated that the factors of institutional theory are the main motivation behind downsizing actions. According to the respondents, the factors of institutional theory are: the command from higher hierarchy, downsizing as a publicly accepted paradigm, learning from the bench-marked companies. See the downsizing motivation – the consideration of institutional factors in Table 4. Among them, the command from higher hierarchy is the major influencing factor. The decisions of downsizing are possibly influenced by the factors of institutional theory, but are not necessarily carefully evaluated. The findings also reveal that many enterprises downsize to cope with the whole organizational change and to implement business process re-engineering. Currently, business process reengineering (BPR)5 is recognized as a fad and many managers recognized the concepts of BPR as downsizing.

Social cognition. The consideration of social cognition means that managers recognize downsizing via mutual interactive mental modes and institutional process. On the other hand, after job re-design, the percentage of redundancy is figured out and the downsizing decision is made by top management. Downsizing motivation is the obvious evidence of the social cognition processes of mangers. Collectively, these top managers instil the concepts of employee self-reliance and de-bureaucratization and, furthermore, put the concepts into action. Simultaneously, people generally accept that organizations must re-organize in the dynamic environment, and therefore that downsizing is inevitable. Apart from that, downsizing also relates to external social interaction. From the interviews, we find out that the managers in Taiwan usually have many opportunities to get access to up-to-date management knowledge, such as managerial journals, further education, practical and academic seminars or similar social functions. From Table 6, we find that all the companies that downsize comply with the requirements of local laws and regulations, but are inconsistent in respecting employees’ basic human rights. Summary

Compound motivation

The downsizing motivation of our case companies are summarized as follows. Three factors coexisted: B, SJ, C, Y, F1, M, H2, L, H1, P, NY, CT, CB; two factors coexisted (economic and institutional): T1; (Economic and social cognition): S; (Institutional and social cognition): TL, A, PH. We therefore conclude that the motivation influencing downsizing is not uniform, but rather various and complicated. Apart from the economic factor, it is also influenced by institutional and social cognition factors. Previously, companies downsized solely due to stagnant or declining profitability. However, more companies have decided to downsize for various other reasons. These companies also revealed that downsizing would not necessarily improve the performance. Currently, companies can easily access information from other companies. They tend to learn from each other, which also represents the viewpoint of institutionalization. The finding from the research also reflects the concepts. Apart from economic and institutional factors, the managers’ social cognition will also make companies treat downsizing as a necessary reorganization tactic.

The Comparisons of Downsizing Motivation between the Subsidiaries of MNCs and the Local Private Enterprises

Except for Company Y, the downsizing motivation of all the subsidiaries of MNC is influenced by the wishes of parent companies or the regional HQ. Compared with the purely foreign-invested companies, joint-ventured and local privately-owned companies tend to be influenced by economic factors. More subsidiaries of MNCs execute business process re-engineering than the local privately-owned companies. Therefore, the downsizing motivation is formed through the process of social cognition. During the process of social cognition, the ethical principles from purely foreign-invested enterprises are more definite and consistent.

Institutionalization, Downsizing Strategies and Practice

From the interviews, we also found that institutional factors will influence not only the downsizing motivation, but also the downsizing strategies. From Table 7, we found that most firms take a gradual downsizing strategy. Furthermore, as seen in Table 8, apart from M, S and NY, most of the firms have provided lay-off packages. Except for M, SJ, TL, all firms have provided consultation or job-seeking services that are also influenced by social institutionalization. In Chinese society, influenced by Confucianism, firms have to follow the specific social norms, people have to be generous and kind to each other, and then the whole society can be prosperous and stable (Graham & Lam, 2003). The interviews also revealed that many employees commented that the downsizing action was ‘not fully satisfactory but acceptable’. It is closely related to firms’ downsizing strategies and the lay-off packages, and also means that institutionalization will affect the results of downsizing.

Summarizing the above analyses, we propose a proposition about downsizing strategies and relevant practices.

Proposition 4. Institutional factor and social cognition both influence downsizing

strategies and related practices.

Conclusions and Suggestions

Our empirical research supported the Institutional Theory of Downsizing proposed by McKinley et al. That is, the motivation of downsizing is mixed with economic factors, institutional factors and social cognition. Moreover, this mixed motivation has also occurred in Taiwan, which is a newly developed country but not a post-industrial country; which contrasts the proposition of McKinley et al. We have explained the Institutional Theory of Downsizing and the relevant practices in downsizing strategies and execution. It is obvious that the Institutional Theory of Downsizing not only affects the motivation but also affects the relevant downsizing practices. Most of the enterprises downsize for a profitable purpose, but to maintain a fair corporate image, the practices should comply with the social norms. The respect of employees’ human rights and principles of justice could be different due to various cultural backgrounds and social systems. For example, the ethical principles in foreign-owned enterprises are clear and consistent; however, joint venture subsidiaries or local enterprises tend to be influenced by local culture and social systems. Research Implications

The evidence for this research comes from Taiwan, but it is worthwhile choosing more sample cases from other cultural backgrounds in order to discuss further the influences of institutional theory of downsizing in cross-cultural contingency. For example, choosing other developing countries, such as China, Malaysia, Thailand as research targets, the generalization of the institutional theory of downsizing will be more concrete. At the same time, it is also interesting to find out if the strategies and actions taken by the downsized employees and the other stakeholders will be also influenced by institutional factors. It is also worthwhile investigating the influences of the institutional motivation and the related strategies on the performance after downsizing (such as the firm performance and the acceptability of the employees). In addition to downsizing, will other prevailing management practices such as BPR, 6s, knowledge management be influenced by institutional factors or the process of social cognition? It will be worthwhile researching this.

Managerial implications. The research has proved that MNCs emphasize global integration. They also focus on local responsiveness when formulating HR managerial practices. For example, the interviewed firms in this research revealed that they downsized because the parent companies asked them to do so. Meanwhile, they still obeyed the local norms and legal requirements. Through the socialization process of the managerial teams, firms can understand the motivation for downsizing and take advantageous strategies and actions. Then, the downsizing action will be effective and comply with the business ethics and further meet the expected goals. Institutionalization can reduce the uncertainty and ensure downsizing can proceed smoothly. Sometimes, firms overemphasize the importance of institutionalization and neglect individual conditions. Therefore, when downsizing, firms have to evaluate carefully both external and internal conditions.
