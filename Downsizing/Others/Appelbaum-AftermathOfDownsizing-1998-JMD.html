The aftermath of downsizing

A case study of disengagement, disidentification, disenfranchisement

and disenchantment

Steven H. Appelbaum Concordia University, Montreal, Canada

Manon Leblanc Air Canada Vacations, Montreal, Canada, and

Barbara T. Shapiro Concordia University, Montreal, Canada

Introduction and background Dominion Travelogics Unlimited (DTU) is a company that recently survived a major corporate reorganization. DTU is a wholesale tour operator. Its primary business is the packaging and selling of vacation tours through travel agencies. The company was created about 25 years ago to increase package tour sales in the leisure market. With offices located in six major Canadian cities DTU employs approximately 200 people nationwide. In the past, DTU had total freedom of deciding on the products it wanted to offer and had the liberty of choosing its own suppliers. DTU’s mission was focused on profitability which was clearly reflected in corporate policies.

In the early 1990s, DTU started losing money due to the opening of too many branch offices too quickly and because some airlines decided to forego previously popular markets. As a result of this, in 1993, DTU redefined its mission. The new mandate was to increase its profitability selling allocated leisure capacity (a pre-determined number of seats for tour packages provided by carriers on certain flights) and vacation experiences that satisfy typical clientele. The new mission affected DTU in many ways, but primarily there was less product that was available for sale. The new mission led to less revenue for the company. Therefore, fewer employees were needed resulting in multi-level layoffs (i.e. reservation agents, ticketing agents, staff in inventory control, accounts receivable, payables, destination services and customer services).

Downsizing had detrimental effects on the employees as indicated by the attitude (climate) survey (Appendix). After examining the restructuring process chosen by the company, the main problem appeared to involve cultural issues, which were aggravated by a lack of leadership and poor communication. It is important to note that over 50 per cent of employees are female, the average age is 32 years, and 75 per cent work in technical support positions.

Although the numbers of employees has recently been reduced from approximately 350 to 200, the result is a very tightly knit group and a powerful grapevine. DTU operates in a very competitive environment, where commercial issues must be kept confidential. Communication problems often arise because some confidential information is shared beyond the managers and some routine directives fail to reach the office staff.

DTU employees shared the values and norms that they had helped to define. After the reorganization, not only did the employees feel a loss of freedom, the policies and the rules of the game were being redefined. Major budget cuts resulted in cancellation of social events and employee recognition programs which led to a significant drop in motivation. Salaries have been frozen for three years resulting in hiring not being permitted even to replace employees who have left. As a result, many departments have seen their job load increase but no compensation has been offered.

The restructuring process chosen by the company appears to be related to the cultural problem. Small groups of employees were being laid-off on Friday afternoons without warning. The loss of valuable co-workers who were also close friends could be compared to the death of a loved one. There was a sense of loss and a feeling of insecurity among the survivors about their own jobs. It was rumored that if the financial position of the company did not improve, additional layoffs would be announced.

In the past ten years, DTU has had five presidents, each of them originating from very different disciplines. They arrived at DTU with their own vision, strategies, and ways of thinking and working. They all had a two-year mandate during which employees had to adapt to the newly imposed culture. Although there was never any real continuation from one CEO’s view to the next, the employees always seemed to slowly adapt to the new views. The changes being implemented were mostly organizational changes that only had an indirect effect on DTU employees. The most recent change included major staff downsizing and this time the remaining employees have not been able to adapt well at all.

What is the problem? Individuals vary widely in their openness to and enthusiasm for change (Carr, 1994, p. 55). It is inevitable that people are going to feel anxiety and stress after downsizing. Many researchers of organizational change have observed from their surveys that organizational productivity deteriorates after downsizing. The employees who remain after downsizing are referred to as the “survivors.” The symptoms of an unproductive survivor culture have been described by human resource professionals as a lack of motivation, loyalty, trust and recommitment to the new organization. Feelings of being deceived, anger and frustration all contribute to the low employee morale and lost productivity (Cameron, 1994, p. 190; Markowitch, 1994, p. 70). One of the most common reasons why companies that downsize perform so poorly is that they often are successful at anticipating and preparing for the employees who are released,

The central problem for DTU is to initiate a process that will promote development of a new corporate culture which is congruent with the needs of the company as a business entity and the needs of employees. The relevant issues are:

• examine the current culture to discover specific problem areas; • select techniques appropriate to DTU for addressing the cultural problem; • make recommendations concerning the techniques including a suggested timeframe for action and need for feedback measures; • that management is committed to accepting feedback with a view to acting on recommendations which have a sound basis in research and historical practice regarding downsized companies; • that employees and management understand human resources are a company’s most significant asset.

Organizational climate The questionnaire which was developed for this study and which will be further described next in the methodology section of this article was intended to describe the attitudes or climate of DTU employees. Determining the organizational climate within an organization is a different and complex task for a consultant, human resource specialist or administrator. Each organization has a personality of its own, and this dynamic variable is its organization climate. Personality is to the individual what organizational climate is to the organization.

Once the dimensions of an organization climate are determined, a strategy can be developed to enable a manager to intervene and change that climate. The strategy might include these steps:

(1) Diagnosis. The process in which the need for change is examined. (2) Strategy planning. Determines methods to be employed such as surveys,

interviews, team building, etc. (3) Education. Required to unfreeze old patterns and incorporate new

information into the organization. (4) Intervention and analysis. The process of interaction between consultant

and manager in order to yield data and analysis. (5) Feedback. The essence of the organizational climate study. It involves

discussion of the data by the department under study. (6) Facilitation. The means of assisting the department manager in

implementing changes in climate.

Organizational climate is the pattern, style and feeling characteristic of interpersonal relationships among members of an organization and also characteristic of the relations between members of the organization and people outside of the organization. It may be assessed by observing many aspects of the relationship in many different situations. Organizational climate is a relatively enduring quality of the internal environment of an organization that:

• is experienced by its members; • influences their behavior; and • can be described in terms of the values of a particular set of characteristics (or attributes) of the organization (Litwin and Taguiri, 1968). The climate can shape or modify the behavior of individuals in groups that become new members of the organization as well as being the guideline for the relationships among organization members of longer seniority. Causal relationships among climate variables and also outcomes may not be unidirectional; for example, the innovative adoption of new practices by an organization may change the climate in the organization as well as the consequence of the climate. Individual perceptions of the variances within the organizational climate when combined are relatively stable over time and are often closely related to outcomes of technical importance (Appelbaum, 1984, p. 52).

To study a climate, surveys consisting of interviews or questionnaires have enjoyed wide-spread use in organizations as sources of information on functioning. It is a rare employee who has not responded to at least one survey of his or her morale, feelings, attitudes, beliefs, or opinions. The rationale for conducting organizational surveys – whether conducted by outside consultants or in-house staff such as human resource specialists – is to assemble information that will aid management in diagnosing problems and selecting remedies. Top management occasionally feels that it has done the right thing just by authorizing a survey. It is assumed that workers will feel much better after having “blown off steam” and gotten the impression that management is interested in them. This is not always the case.

A survey’s immediate effect may be somewhat positive; respondents sometimes experience mild catharsis in expressing their attitudes and feelings. But in responding, employees may be led to expect that survey information, including their own statements, will be used to rectify the problems and unpleasant situation being described. These employees may become cynical, if not angry, about management’s motives in instigating the survey. One can make a strong case that it is better to let sleeping dogs lie and not conduct a survey

If survey results are to be used effectively, managers must understand the results and their implications, and incorporate this information with existing attitudes and behavior. In addition, at the conclusion of the survey, participation continues via the feedback process involving all work groups. The more relevant the survey results, the more likely they are to elicit the interest and motivation of management. Feedback on both individual and organizational performance can be motivating and cause the individual to increase his/her level of aspiration for future achievement (Appelbaum, 1984, p. 56).

Methodology This study utilized a nonprobability sample design since it was not possible for the researchers to estimate the accuracy of sample estimates of population characteristics. The rationale for utilizing this design was convenience, economy and ease of building a sample since in much organizational research, as was this particular study, nonprobability samples are often the only choice available. In this case, a purposive sample was employed in that a decision was made to consider people typical of the population of interest and to get a range of opinions about the major issue of downsizing. The researchers utilized key internal personnel inside the target organization to help select the sample since one of the researchers was able to reconstruct relevant events for the group and was in a strategic position to observe/record critical aspects of the organization culture.

Data were collected via a questionnaire developed to assess people’s opinions/attitudes (climate) and proved to be a uniform method of data collection. However, the rigid structure could not be altered to fit any of the unique circumstances of each respondent. The instrument was developed mainly utilizing an ordinal scale to allow the researcher to assess the relative position of an object or individual on a variable. Unfortunately, nonprobability sample designs do not allow the researcher to generalize beyond the sample and some uncontrollable events surrounding the study may affect external validity. It is also important to note that some change in the respondents participating in the study could account for some results, not the variables controlled by the researchers. In essence, this method of measurement may affect respondents attitudes, not the variables of interest. For this particular study a questionnaire (see Appendix) was developed in which 30 questions explored downsizing practices, improvement of earnings, satisfaction and morale. These data which were analyzed from 52 respondents (out of possible 100) will be presented in Figures 1-17.

This particular exercise was a hybrid case and field study. It is a case study in that it represents an in-depth analysis of one setting in which interviews, questionnaires and observations were utilized. This approach can be vulnerable

Organizational culture Edgar H. Schein defines culture as:

a pattern of shared basic assumptions invented, discovered, or developed by a given group as it learns to cope with its problems of external adoption and internal integration that has worked well enough to be considered valid, and, therefore is to be taught to new members of the group as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel in relation to those problems (Schein, 1985, p. 247). Studies conducted by Schein also indicate that one can divide a definition of an organizational culture into three levels of cultural phenomena: basic assumption, values and artifacts (Schein, 1985, p. 252).

Basic assumptions Basic assumptions in an organization are circumstances taken for granted as the correct way of doing things. They lie at the deepest level of culture and are the hardest to change. The basic assumptions present at DTU encompass all of the beliefs taken for granted, the habits of perception, thoughts and feelings. For example, employees take for granted the following: free or reduced travel costs, lavish Christmas parties, monetary incentives, “free” time off. Removal of the above advantages has generated a negative reaction. A problem is that DTU employees had become accustomed to all the extra benefits and special treatments they received before the downsizing.

Values Values refer to a sense of what “ought” to be. DTU’s norms and values are the heart of its organizational culture and are influenced by the company’s mission. Norms are the expectations regarding the behaviour of employees and can be found in the DTU employee guide. The values that exist among DTU employees reflect their preferences of different activities and possibilities. A high level of customer service, professionalism, helpful and friendly telephone manners, the “casual Fridays” dress code, drinking and eating at work stations,

and informal break periods are examples of behaviors related to DTU’s values. Their vision is to be recognized by both customers and the travel industry as one of Canada’s best corporations.

Artifacts Artifacts are the overt behaviors and other physical manifestations of culture. They are usually directly observed and are easier to change than assumptions and values, however, changing artifacts rarely results in a change of culture. The artifacts consist of DTU’s visible organizational structure and process. An example of artifacts that one may observe is the numerous leisure posters and holiday pictures than are present on many walls and desktops.

It has been proposed by Vecchio and Appelbaum (1995, p. 660) that the maintenance, reinforcement or change of an organization’s culture can be best understood by knowing DTU is very much results oriented. Higher levels of management emphasize sales, profits and budget control. One can easily observe that numbers come before people. Middle management seems more concerned with employee satisfaction than higher level management is, but they do not have the necessary authority to conduct any major changes.

Another aspect of culture is illuminated in that top management is very discrete even secretive in the way it reacts to a major crisis. A minimum number of people help solve the situation, and all efforts are concentrated towards minimizing the adverse publicity created by the crisis. The way in which the downsizing issue was kept from employees is a concrete example of how DTU’s top management operates. They often adopt a follower strategy, such that when a change is required, management prefers to wait and see how the competition is confronting the matter. Preference is given to a very conservative approach which avoids taking any sort of risk in the decision making process.

Furthermore, rewards and promotions have been rare events at DTU during the past few years. Company policy is to base bonuses and promotions on performance and also to promote internally as much as possible. If the prerequisites cannot be satisfied from within the company, the next step is to contact different colleges and universities. The firing process is dependent on the reason behind it. Violation of any major item described in the employee guide results in immediate dismissal. Employees usually receive several warnings for misconduct before they are fired.

Changing the organizational culture Change is a constant feature in today’s organization and many changes emerged when DTU redefined its mission. The way in which the process for change is designed and presented will heavily influence the outcome of how it is viewed by employees. Support from top management counts for more than any other variable in the change equation. Management must demonstrate a willingness and ability to apply strategies that put employees in control of change rather than having change control them. DTU needed to change (cut cost, restructure and downsize) to remain competitive. The way it made

organizational changes was a problem. During the layoff period, employees did not know what to expect when they came to work. The transition period is still very difficult and employees tend to try to cling to what had once been, making it difficult on themselves and the organization to move forward.

One must remember that employees do not resist change, they resist being changed (Woodward and Buchholz, 1987). The reason behind such resistance is strongly related to the fear of the unknown. This may explain why employees chose to be reactive, started rumours, and complained. As demonstrated in the attitude survey, morale and job satisfaction decreased. The employees never received a clear picture of the situation and were mostly kept in the dark.

The loss of fellow employees who were also personal friends and part of informal groups was another reason why morale was so low in this company. They experienced a reduction in the socio-emotional interaction they shared with the workers that were laid off. A sense of loss existed among the remaining employees, and social interaction had been reduced throughout the organization ever since the downsizing. The loss of energy created disengagement, the focus on the past created disidentification, and the shared anger created disenchantment. All these emotions were visible among the remaining employees and needed immediate attention in order for DTU to progress.

In their book, Aftershock, Helping People Through Corporate Change, Woodward and Buchholz (1987) discuss many issues regarding corporate change and how to help people manage change. They defined the change from a comfortable secure environment to a feeling of betrayal, loss, and confusion as the “aftershock” experience. They also suggest that it can be explained by the way the employees are living within the experience of change rather than focusing on the change strategy. The employees do not know what to believe or whom to trust anymore. For example, even though the survey questionnaire stated that it was for research purposes only, many employees questioned whether this study was “undercover” work by DTU’s president. The problem at DTU is that even though the changes occurred over two years ago, employees are still very bitter. Instead of moving through “aftershock”, they persist in it. Management underestimated the impact downsizing would have on its employees. Many employees responded to the survey by complaining about not being consulted before the downsizing, while others disagreed on the decisions made regarding who should have stayed and who should have gone. Employees contend that “good” employees are gone and “bad” ones are still with the company. Some of the comments (both positive and negative) expressed during this exercise and process proved to be interesting and were later reflected in the Attitude Survey (Appendix 1) and respective Figures 1-17 presented throughout this paper.

The message of change should travel through the organization’s culture. The first step in changing organizational culture is to learn and understand the existing culture. Two key dimensions of organizational practice must be examined. First, management must determine to what extent the culture is employee or job oriented. Second, to what extent the culture was process or result oriented. One must also keep in mind the existence of subcultures in different areas of the organization. For this reason, management must locate the culture’s foundation by understanding the relationship network (“informal” authority structure) of the existing culture.

Employees develop greater degrees of shared values and norms by participating in common organizational experiences which in turn help to improve the communication and the decision-making process.

Any important decisions are either made or approved by the company president. The survey results also show a low level of satisfaction and that employees are not consulted before decisions are made. Relating these responses to Likert’s system, DTU could be classified as an exploitative authoritative organization (System I). Failure to offer accurate information to employees promotes negative rumors in the organization as indicated in the survey comments. Employees will be very resentful of learning of changes from outsiders. Also, top management needs to demonstrate visible and consistent support for change. For example, if the change effort calls for team-building, then top management should be the first group to try to build teams. Management’s leadership in the change effort seems to be the key determinant of whether that change will succeed. It is not enough to say that leadership is critical. What is different is the type of leadership being recommended; a leadership that does more than just create and articulate a new vision for the organization; leadership that is open and collaborative. Part of communication and collaboration involves linking the intended changes to organizational outcomes such as productivity and quality of work life. The DTU survey indicates that management is not sensitive enough to the concerns of its employees (Figure 7).

Another key leadership feature involves role modelling of expected behaviors. This was supported mostly in Figure 8. This obviously affected some employees since it appeared in the survey comments.

Organizational politics means acquiring, developing and using power to achieve one’s objectives. At the individual level, management needs to identify people who hold powerful positions and have developed a reputation of being powerful. It is also important to look at interest groups that control key resources. Powerful networks can be identified by studying key linkages between individuals and coalitions. Not only must the advocate of change be aware of politics and power plays, they must also use power and politics themselves. One major problem is that much of DTU’s top management has not been able to succeed in the transition that requires “soft skills”. Fundamental change of management style is difficult in practice. Leadership is an interactive relationship between leader and follower. This relationship is based on trust, and trust is generated by a person’s sense of integrity. To be a good leader, integrity is very important and it must be combined with competence, vision, and enthusiasm. Without personal integrity, there can be no mutual trust. DTU needs to train managers to be effective in communicating, collaborating and demonstrating support for successful changes while demonstrating trustworthiness.

Communications systems Survey results Communication is a social process that includes both perception and influence. The accurate conveyance and reception of a message, plus the impact the message has on the receiver, are equally important in explaining the phenomenon of communication (Vecchio and Appelbaum, 1995, p. 562). If the organizational climate is one of openness and trust, then incomplete or controversial communications are more likely to be interpreted favorably. But when distrust is the norm, messages may be harshly scrutinized for “hidden meanings”, and even good news may be greeted with suspicion and ridicule (Vecchio and Appelbaum, 1995, p. 580).

Poor communication creates a misunderstanding of goals and strategic mission as well as misconceptions about team relationships and individual responsibilities (Vecchio and Appelbaum, 1995, p. 570). It spurs anxiety and conflict, a sense of disenfranchisement, poor productivity and increased cost to the company (Vecchio and Appelbaum, 1995, p. 570). Employees cannot support the changes the organization is making if they do not know what is going on. In a survey of 30 companies, 46 per cent of the respondents said that

Decision-making dynamics at Dominion Travelogics Unlimited Some models Stewart in “Culture and decision-making” refers to decision-making as an “overt process that is the bridge between mind and action” (Gudyhurst et al., 1985). Introducing an improved decision-making process can help rebuild group cohesion and provide management and employees with an inventory of problems with mutually agreed on priorities. To do this, it was decided that NGT be utilized.

The “nominal group technique” (NGT) is a decision-making method that combines structure and important opportunities for social interaction while being a task-oriented procedure. The basic steps of the technique are outlined in Table I.

Some key advantages of NGT are: • benefit of small group social interaction to establish rapport, build cohesion, and offer improved decision-making quality (suggested group size ranges from seven to ten people); • relatively short timeframe to expect results and limited cost (as compared with a Delphi technique or exhaustive surveys). Gainsharing: a prescription or a bandaid? A high level of empowerment can be obtained by gainsharing. It can be described as a process that combines participatory management and profit sharing. According to Markowitch, “it give employees selfish reason to care and increase productivity by linking their bottom line (pay check) with the company’s bottom line” (Markowitch, 1994, p. 70). Gainsharing attempts to have employees think and act like business owners.

Based on the above findings and analysis of actual practices, a final proposed process model (Figure 18) is recommended for use by organizations as a guide when planning to downsize. This final model includes two major changes: improving the survivor support plan by setting it up as a sub-process in the model, and including an additional sub-model that includes elements that relate to operational practices after downsizing both in HRM practices and in work processes.

As was pointed out by Koonce (1991) and Isabella (1989), addressing survivor needs is essential to the new organization if its strategic objectives are to be achieved. This therefore necessitates a stronger emphasis on survivor needs, since this seems to be the major weakness in all organizations studied. A structured process is therefore recommended for the survivor support plan as well. This may be done through:

(1) setting objectives for the survivor support plan; (2) determining additional costs for the plan; (3) developing the plan; (4) implementing the plan; (5) evaluating the plan after implementation is complete; and (6) amending the plan, if and when necessary. (Labib and Appelbaum, 1993,

p. 87; 1994, p. 81.) Addressing survivor needs, by itself, however, does not ensure the successful attainment of corporate objectives after downsizing. Survivors must also be supported by the new organization operationally. This, therefore, is not just an HRM issue, but a strategic and operational management issue as well. Organizations can address these issues through several activities that can be initiated before, during, and after downsizing that pertain to the restructuring of HRM practices and organizational operating processes. Examples of these are a:

• comprehensive, continuous, and honest communication program; • restructuring of job designs and the supporting compensation packages; • comprehensive employee development program that reinforces the new career paths, the new performance standards, the organization’s commitment to its human resources, and the organization’s emphasis on security in personal development rather than job security within the organization; • radical change in the day-to-day operating standards and procedures to reflect the changes in the organizational structure and the job restructuring. This should be accompanied by corresponding changes in the performance evaluation systems of the organization.

An important element in the employee development plan is a strong emphasis on the employees’ need to manage their own careers independent of the opportunities available within the organization (Labib and Appelbaum, 1993, p. 89; 1994).

To address survivor needs, the communication plan does not have to be formal and support does not necessarily have to include expensive counselling. Frequent dialogue with key members of management may be all that is needed. Also, retaining the services of an industrial psychologist may prove less costly than contracting an outplacement firm, if the number of employees involved is not very high. Other types of counselling, such as financial advice and jobsearch techniques can be provided by the organization’s own finance department and HR recruitment and selection staff may advise employees about job search techniques if they are trained to do so. In fact, for organizations that are in a continuous downsizing mode, providing some outplacement services such as financial counselling and job search training in-house may be a less costly alternative to using outside sources.

It is important for organizations to realize that although the proposed model involves incurring costs that are not directly related to the actual downsizing, they cannot afford not to address survivor needs in a more comprehensive and operational way. To do so, HRM policies must be changed to emphasize the new orientation and changed vision of the organization and work processes must be adjusted to the smaller size and different objectives of the new organization. In the long-run, the price an organization pays for not adequately addressing these issues may either be its “death”, or growing “fat” again and having to experience the pains of major downsizing all over again (Labib and Appelbaum, 1993, p. 90).
