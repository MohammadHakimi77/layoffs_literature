Problem Solving or Responsibility Avoidance? The Role of CEO Internal Attribution Tendency in Shaping Corporate Downsizing in Response to Performance Shortfalls

Wei Shia, Guoli Chenb and Boshuo Lia

aUniversity of Miami; bINSEAD

ABSTRACT  This study examines how CEOs’ internal attribution tendency – to attribute an observed outcome to internal factors – shapes the extent to which corporate downsizing activities are driven by performance shortfalls. Despite the known effectiveness of downsizing in creating leaner organizations and enhancing firm performance, CEOs often avoid it as it runs counter to their own interests. Applying the awareness-motivation-capability (AMC) framework, we argue that CEOs’ internal attribution tendency influences their awareness of responsibility for performance shortfalls, and ultimately whether they engage in downsizing actions in response to such shortfalls. This effect becomes more salient when CEOs have the motivation (e.g., scrutiny from financial analysts) or capability (e.g., the existence of an unfavourable external environment) to avoid taking such responsibility. Using a sample of 2424 US firms, we find support for our arguments. Our study highlights the importance of CEO internal attribution tendency in explaining whether firms resort to downsizing actions to address performance shortfalls.

Keywords: internal attribution tendency, heuristics and biases, performance feedback, strategic leadership, corporate downsizing

As boundedly rational decisionmakers are subject to cognitive limits and biases (Cyert and March, 1963; March and Simon, 1958), their awareness of their responsibility for firm outcomes may differ, resulting in heterogeneous responses to performance shortfalls.

Our study focuses on a fundamental cognitive characteristic that shapes CEOs’ awareness of their responsibility for performance shortfalls: internal attribution tendency, which captures the extent to which a CEO attributes an observed outcome to internal factors (Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1973; Weiner, 1985). Where a CEO’s internal attribution tendency is strong, the CEO is more likely to see the firm’s performance as his or her personal responsibility and thus to respond to performance feedback. However, where a CEO’s internal attribution tendency is weak, the CEO is more inclined to attribute performance to factors beyond his or her control and hence less likely to undertake actions to address performance issues.

To investigate the role of internal attribution tendency in shaping strategic actions in response to performance shortfalls, we focus on corporate downsizing – a corporate restructuring activity aimed at reducing personnel and streamlining organizations to increase resource allocation efficiency (Nixon et al., 2004). THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

CEO Internal Attribution Tendency

According to attribution theory, people have an innate tendency to make sense of their surroundings. Like ‘naive psychologists’ (Heider, 1958), they seek to determine the cause of events in an effort to establish control over their lives and improve their ability to predict future events (Kelley, 1971). Attribution theory suggests that in answering ‘why’ questions, people distinguish between internal (self) and external (outside of self) explanations, thereby determining the locus of causality (Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1967). Identifying the locus of causality has been at the heart of attribution theory since its inception (Martinko et al., 2006). When the cause is seen as related to an attribute of the individual such as his or her effort or ability, it is referred to as ‘internal’; when the cause is seen as related to situational factors beyond the individual’s control, it is referred to as ‘external’.

Building on social psychology research, management scholars have investigated how different types of executive attribution (i.e., internal or external) influence corporate leaders’ decisions (e.g., Lee et al., 2022; Park et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2019a; Shin et al., 2022). However, although internal or external attribution can be situational (e.g., situational on positive or negative outcomes),[2] an individual is likely to be dominated by either a stable internal or external attribution tendency, and this tendency triggers distinct responses in the individual when problems arise (Calder and Burnkrant, 1977; Neumann, 2000; Ryan and Deci, 2002; Turban et al., 2007). Executives with an internal attribution tendency attribute performance outcomes to internal factors and accept responsibility for them, whereas those who attribute performance outcomes to external factors avoid taking responsibility.

Our study focuses on CEOs’ internal attribution tendency – to what extent CEOs draw internal (versus external) inferences about their responsibility for an outcome. Those with a strong internal attribution tendency accept responsibility for an observed outcome, be it favourable (positive performance) or unfavourable (poor performance). Those with a weak internal attribution tendency will point to external factors, accepting neither responsibility nor credit for the outcome. Internal attribution tendency – an important cognitive characteristic – is particularly relevant in the context of performance feedback research because it shapes awareness of responsibility for performance outcomes,[3] which in turn influences whether a firm engages in problemistic search and the choice of strategic actions to address performance issues.

Performance Feedback and Corporate Downsizing

Management scholars have made a significant effort to understand the role of performance feedback in organizational behaviour. The behavioural theory of the firm holds that performance feedback prompts organizational adaptation and that decision-makers assess the need to change existing routines by benchmarking performance against an aspiration level – a satisfactory performance level (Cyert and March, 1963) – below which they may resort to problemistic search, defined as a search for solutions, with the goal of reversing the decline and improving future performance (Greve, 1998, 2003b). Thus, CEOs may eschew downsizing activities as a means of addressing poor performance (Johnson, 1996).

HYPOTHESES

To develop hypotheses about the role of CEOs’ internal attribution tendency in responding to performance shortfalls, we turn to the AMC framework (Chen, 1996). Building on social cognition and organizational change (Fiske and Taylor,  2013; Kiesler and Sproull, 1982), the AMC framework was originally developed in the field of competitive dynamics (Chen and Miller, 2012; Downing et al., 2019) to show how three independent components – awareness, motivation, and capability – trigger responses and actions by competitors. However, since the AMC framework by nature pertains to a response process from ‘problem sensing’ to ‘problem solving’ (Kiesler and Sproull, 1982), it has great potential to be applied to other fields related to generalized responses to problems. Recent research has applied the framework to other contexts such as firms’ responses to the threat of shareholder activism (Shi et al., 2020), the threat of negative earnings surprises (Guo et al., 2020), and interorganizational spillover (Shi et al., 2022). These studies contend that the central cognitive aspect of awareness pertains to ‘problem sensing’; how efficiently ‘problem sensing’ translates into ‘problem solving’ will depend on the individual’s motivation and capability (Shi et al., 2020, 2022). We extend the scope of the AMC framework to managerial decisions in response to performance shortfalls by positing that internal attribution tendency shapes the CEO’s awareness of responsibility for such shortfalls and exploring how the degree to which such tendencies shape strategic response hinges on a CEO’s motivation and capability to avoid responsibility for performance outcomes.

CEO Internal Attribution Tendency: Awareness of Responsibility for Performance Shortfalls

In the AMC framework, awareness is considered ‘a prerequisite for any move’ in the decision-making process (Chen, 1996, p. 110). The psychology literature proposes that internal attribution tendency is closely related to self-awareness (Duval and Silvia, 2002), i.e., ‘making internal attributions of responsibility for events that have negative consequences causes people to feel more aware of themselves’ (Rogers et al., 1982, p. 959). Thus, a CEO’s internal attribution tendency can influence their awareness of responsibility for firm performance, shaping whether the firm resorts to corporate downsizing activities in response to performance shortfalls.

We posit that CEOs with a strong internal attribution tendency will attribute observed outcomes to internal factors (i.e., their own responsibility for poor performance). Accordingly, they are more willing to learn from past experiences (Desai, 2015) and proactively take strategic actions to address performance shortfalls. They perceive that organizational actions (e.g., strategic decisions taken, sales efforts, product or process development, or steps that impact production efficiency) affect firm performance (Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1973; Weiner, 1985). As a result, to address performance shortfalls, they will be likely to undertake downsizing activities even if these run counter to their own interests in the short run. In contrast, those with a weak internal attribution tendency will blame external factors (e.g., the economy, the competition, or regulators) for poor firm performance (Ford and Baucus, 1987; Milliken and Lant, 1991). Moderating Effects of CEOs’ Motivation and Capability to Avoid Responsibility

We next investigate how the motivation to avoid taking responsibility for firm performance influences CEOs’ internal attribution tendency in shaping firms’ responses to performance shortfalls through downsizing. In the AMC framework, awareness, characterized as individuals’ cognition of taking actions, is the first stage of the response process. Motivation, which captures the subjective motive to actively respond, comes in a close second in the psychological drive to take action to respond (Chen, 1996). We investigate the role of one source of motivation – scrutiny by financial analysts – because of analysts’ important role as external evaluators who can influence CEOs’ motivation to take responsibility for performance shortfalls.

Research in a wide range of academic disciplines has considered how managers operate under the watchful eye of financial analysts (Benner and Ranganathan, 2012; Gentry and Shen, 2013). Financial analysts evaluate CEO performance by raising questions about the firm’s operations, collecting proprietary data via meetings and conference calls, and disseminating that information to the public via reports (Lang et al., 2004). They have been shown to influence managerial compensation and dismissal decisions (Chen et al., 2015; Wiersema and Zhang, 2011). This could provide motivation for CEOs to ingratiate themselves with financial analysts (Westphal and Clement, 2008).

When firms are covered by many financial analysts, CEOs come under the scrutiny of these external performance evaluators and thus have strong motivation to project a favourable image. Corporate downsizing in response to a performance shortfall may signal that they have made unwise strategic decisions in the past, such that CEOs with a weak internal attribution tendency will be reluctant to engage in downsizing to address such shortfalls. In contrast, when analyst coverage is sparse, CEOs are less subject to external scrutiny or pressure, and hence those with a weak internal attribution tendency may feel less concerned about undertaking downsizing activities that could otherwise be associated with notions of incompetence or poor strategic decisions. Where the internal attribution tendency is strong, CEOs are aware of their responsibility for poor performance and therefore decisions are less likely to be influenced by how they will be perceived by financial analysts. Consequently, they are more likely to resort to downsizing in response to shortfalls whether or not the firm is closely followed by analysts. It follows that the moderating effect of a CEO’s internal attribution tendency will be stronger in the presence of a higher level of analyst coverage.

We now discuss how the capability to avoid responsibility for firm performance affects the role of internal attribution tendency with respect to downsizing in response to performance shortfalls. The third component of AMC refers to the capability to respond and act. Unlike motivation, which implies a subjective attitude to taking action, capability largely depends on objective dimensions such as resource endowment or the external environment (Chen, 1996). In this respect, we consider the moderating effect of an unfavourable/favourable external environment in developing our third hypothesis.

The external environment is closely related to a CEO’s capability to avoid responsibility for firm performance. A less favourable external environment, such as an economic crisis, makes it easier for CEOs with a weak internal attribution tendency to blame external factors for poor performance (Eggers and Song, 2015; Martinko et al., 2006). Put differently, when they can avoid responsibility for performance shortfalls, they refrain from corporate downsizing activities that harm their interests. In contrast, if the external environment is favourable, it is difficult for CEOs with a weak internal attribution tendency to ascribe poor performance to external factors and deny their own responsibility; as a result, these CEOs may be more willing to engage in downsizing activities to address performance shortfalls. We posit that for CEOs with a strong internal attribution tendency, strategic decisions are less influenced by the external environment – be it favourable or unfavourable – since they accept responsibility for firm performance and thus will undertake corporate downsizing to address performance shortfalls. In contrast, CEOs with a weak internal attribution tendency react differentially in favourable/unfavourable external environments. In this sense, internal attribution tendency plays a more salient role in moderating the effect of performance shortfalls on corporate downsizing actions when the external environment is unfavourable.

Hypothesis 3:  The positive moderating effect of CEO internal attribution tendency on the relationship between performance shortfalls and downsizing actions is stronger when the external environment is more unfavourable.

Predictors

Performance shortfalls. Various performance indicators have been used to measure a firm’s performance relative to its aspiration levels, such as return on assets (ROA) (Audia and Greve, 2006; Chen and Miller, 2007; Shimizu, 2007) and market share (Baum et al., 2005). We chose ROA as it is widely used in this line of research (Shinkle, 2012).

Managers use organizational historical performance and performance of peer firms to measure aspiration levels (Cyert and March, 1963). A historical performance shortfall is based on a recursive measure that weights prior performance and prior aspiration levels to measure current aspiration levels (Kuusela et al., 2017). It is defined as At = αPt−1 + (1 −α)At−1, where At refers to the aspiration level at time t, and Pt−1 is the firm performance at time t − 1. The coefficient alpha (α) indicates the relative importance of the prior aspiration level (t − 1) versus the actual prior performance (t − 1). Following Kuusela et al. (2017), we chose a relatively large value of α (= 0.75), but the results are robust, α = 0.6 or 0.5.

The social aspiration level is typically measured by comparing the focal firm’s performance with the industry average/median (Audia and Greve, 2006; Baum et al., 2005; Iyer and Miller, 2008). Since CEOs do not pay the same attention to all players in the same industry, preferring to focus on a smaller reference group of firms that are similar in size and belong to the same strategic group as their peers (Fiegenbaum and Thomas, 1995), we construct our social aspiration levels by using a matching approach to identify the most relevant peer firms. Specifically, we use the Text-based Network Industry Classification (TNIC-3) to identify peer firms (Hoberg and Phillips, 2016).[5] We apply exact matching based on year and TNIC-3 industry classifications to identify the n closest matching firms for each observation, relying on a product similarity score and total assets. Then we choose the peer firms by ranking the Mahalanobis distance score between the focal firm and industry peers (Mahalanobis, 1936). Given that CEOs have cognitive limitations and are more likely to focus on a smaller number of comparable industry peers, we follow Kuusela et al. (2017) to set n = 5. To construct our social aspiration level, we use the average performance of matched peers.

We then combine social and historical aspiration to create an overall aspiration level (= a × Social aspiration + (1 −a) × Historical aspiration). We follow Harris and Bromiley (2007) to give equal weights to social and historical aspirations (α = 0.5).[6] Moreover, we follow prior research to split the performance measure into two variables. CEO internal attribution tendency. As noted, we used CEOs’ speeches during quarterly earnings conference calls to measure CEO attribution. Conference call transcripts have two parts: prepared remarks and question and answer (Q&A) sections. Our readings of transcripts suggest that CEOs make attribution statements in both. We therefore used both prepared remarks and Q&A sections to measure CEO attributions. We followed guidance on computer-aided textual analyses (McKenny et al., 2018; Short et al., 2010) to analyse CEOs’ words in these transcripts.

We first extracted CEOs’ speeches from earnings conference call transcripts and identified causal sentences using causal words included in the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) text analysis program (Pennebaker et al., 2007). Based on these causal sentences, we captured CEOs’ internal attribution and external attribution. As psychology research suggests that personal pronouns reflect the attention allocation of the speaker (Davis and Brock, 1975; Gunsch et al., 2000; Tausczik and Pennebaker, 2010), we assume that CEOs exhibit strong internal attribution when they frequently use first-person pronouns (both I and we forms) in causal attribution sentences. We thus counted how frequently a CEO used first-person pronouns to capture internal attributions. As managers often refer to general economic and external factors when making external attributions (Baginski et al., 2004), to construct our word list for external attributions, we randomly selected 100 transcripts and read them to identify external factor words.[7] We counted how frequently a CEO used external factor words to measure external attributions. Thereafter, CEO internal attribution tendency is measured as the ratio of the number of internal attribution words used by CEOs in causal sentences in earnings calls in a year to the number of both internal and external attribution words.[8] We conducted several analyses to validate our measure (see Appendix 1).

Moderators

The first moderator is analyst coverage, which is measured as the number of financial analysts covering a firm in a year (Wiersema and Zhang, 2011). We took the natural log of analyst coverage, as the moderating effect of analyst coverage may decrease as the value of analyst coverage increases. The data used to measure analyst coverage were obtained from the International Brokers’ Estimate System (IBES) database. The second moderator is unfavourable external environment. We use the 2007–08 financial crisis to capture an unfavourable external environment (Erkens et al., 2012). The crisis was triggered by excessive risk-taking by banks and led to the most severe global recession since the Great Depression (Erkens et al., 2012), which created an unfavourable external operating environment for companies. This moderator takes a value of 1 for years 2007 and 2008, and 0 for other years. Our results are similar if we use only year 2008 to denote the financial crisis.

Control Variables

As indicated earlier, our theoretical focus is performance below aspiration (i.e., performance shortfall); therefore, we first control for performance above aspiration (ROAaspiration [above]) in all models. We also include several firm- and CEO-level control variables that may influence organizational change. At the firm level, we first control for firm size (measured by taking the natural log of total assets) as larger firms may have more assets to be downsized. We also control for slack resources. ‘Slack’ refers to the excess resources available to an organization during a given planning cycle (Nohria and Gulati, 1996), which may affect firms’ willingness to engage in downsizing actions. We follow prior research to create a composite slack index, slack resources, by adding the standardized scores of three categories: available slack (measured by current assets divided by current liabilities), absorbed slack (measured by working capital divided by total sales), and potential slack (measured by the equity-to-debt ratio) (Bromiley, 1991; Chen, 2008). Models

The dependent variable of our study is the number of downsizing actions, which is a count variable. The two commonly used methods to analyse count data are negative binomial regression and Poisson regression. Given that our dependent variable has the property of overdispersion, we use negative binomial regressions to test our hypotheses. To address serial correlation in panel data sets, we adopt random-effects negative binomial models. Such models treat the data in a time-series cross-section panel and can handle the spatial and temporal effects in the data. In all regressions, we measure our dependent variable at t + 1 and all the other variables at t.

Theoretical Contributions

Our findings contribute to several streams of research. First, our study adds to performance feedback research by incorporating CEOs’ internal attribution tendency into the literature. Although corporate downsizing provides an effective way for firms to enhance short-term performance, CEOs may not necessarily resort to such actions to mitigate performance shortfalls. Our study suggests that whether CEOs engage in downsizing activities to address performance shortfalls hinges on the degree of their internal attribution tendency. Those with a strong internal attribution tendency are aware of their responsibility for performance shortfalls and willing to take the blame, and hence resort to any activities that can enhance firm performance, including downsizing actions, despite their potentially negative implications for the CEO. Those with a weaker internal attribution tendency are more likely to eschew downsizing activities in this situation since they fail to accept their responsibility for performance shortfalls. In essence, our findings suggest that CEOs’ internal attribution tendency is critical to understand whether performance shortfalls will trigger corporate downsizing activities.

Relatedly, recent research has started to examine the role of executive characteristics such as CEO power and overconfidence in shaping firms’ responses to performance feedback in terms of risk-taking propensity, resource allocations, alliances, and acquisitions (e.g., Blagoeva et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2022; Lim and Audia, 2020; Schumacher et al., 2020). Our study adds to this stream by considering the role of a CEO’s internal attribution tendency in shaping downsizing actions that can lead to a reduction in firm size rather than growth-oriented risk-taking decisions.

More broadly, our study provides new insights into research on executive heuristics and biases (e.g., Chatterjee and Hambrick, 2007; Foss and Weber, 2016; Hodgkinson and Healey, 2011; Li and Tang, 2010; Nadkarni and Chen, 2014). Our study not only introduces a new executive cognitive bias – CEO internal attribution tendency – to the literature but also illustrates that the role of CEO internal attribution tendency as a cognitive bias in shaping firm strategic decisions hinges on firm financial performance. Our analyses suggest that CEO internal attribution tendency affects corporate downsizing when a firm underperforms, but such an effect does not exist in the presence of desirable financial performance. This highlights the importance of considering organizational performance in unpacking when executive heuristics and biases can affect firm strategic decisions.

In addition, we introduce the AMC framework developed from competitive dynamics research (Chen et al., 2007; Chen and Miller, 2012) to performance feedback research. Whereas this stream of literature implicitly contends that problemistic search in response to performance shortfalls is contingent on actors’ motivation (e.g., managerial risk preference) and capability (e.g., presence of slack resources) (for a review, see Posen et al., 2018), it assumes no awareness component. Our findings imply that CEOs’ internal attribution tendency shapes their awareness of responsibility for performance shortfalls and how the firm responds. The effect of that awareness is dependent on their motivation (when under external scrutiny) and their capability to avoid responsibility for performance shortfalls (when the external environment can be blamed). By using the AMC framework to develop our hypotheses, we introduce a new theoretical perspective to understand what factors can shape firms’ responses to performance feedback.

Our study also advances management research on executive attribution. Research has shown that CEOs are susceptible to a self-serving bias – that is, they tend to ascribe positive outcomes to themselves and negative outcomes to external factors as a means to maintain a positive self-image and/or project themselves favourably to outsiders (Hayward et al., 2004). Other research has focused on what influences boards to attribute CEO successes and failures to internal or external factors (e.g., Shi et al., 2019a). Our study departs from existing research on self-serving bias by focusing on CEOs’ internal attribution tendency, a cognitive characteristic that pertains to their heterogeneous propensities for ascribing observed outcomes to internal versus external factors, which in turn may exert an influence on how firms respond to performance shortfalls.

Practical Implications

The implications of our study will be of interest to both corporate boards and executives. First, it is important for directors to understand CEOs’ internal attribution tendency, notably that those with low internal attribution tendency are less willing to undertake downsizing activities in response to poor performance. Board members thus need to intensify their monitoring to ensure that CEOs have considered downsizing actions in problemistic search. Second, our study suggests that executives should make a self-assessment of their internal attribution tendency to be aware of how far it may bias their decisions and constrain their strategic options. To address this issue, firms should pair executives with respectively strong and weak internal attribution tendencies in the top management team to enhance diversity in mindset.

Limitations and Future Research Avenues

The limitations of our study – which future research could help to overcome – are as follows: First, although validity testing shows that our measure of CEO internal attribution tendency, which is based on analysing the content of CEOs’ earnings conference call transcripts, is reasonably good, future research could use more fine-grained qualitative data to identify variations in internal and external attributions and examine whether different types have differential influences on organizational responses to performance

Second, we focus on CEOs in our study because they are the most important decision-makers in firms. However, we have only investigated how CEOs respond to overall performance shortfalls rather than poor performance directly related to their own decision-making. Future research can extend this study by investigating whether CEOs with strong internal attribution tendency tend to engage in downsizing activities by divesting assets acquired by themselves, for which they are directly responsible, versus other assets. Moreover, large-scale investments may call for participation from other non-CEO top executives and the board of directors. An interesting avenue for future research would be to explore how attribution inconsistency between key top executives shapes strategic decisions. For example, if the CEO exhibits a high internal attribution tendency but the COO also shows a low internal attribution tendency, the influence of CEO internal attribution tendency may be attenuated given that the COO shoulders some responsibility for strategic decisions. Similarly, how the board of directors’ internal attribution tendency may strengthen or weaken the CEO’s internal attribution tendency offers another potential avenue for future research.

Third, we acknowledge that some CEOs may be motivated to hide their responsibility for negative outcomes even though they are aware of their responsibility for them, a behaviour triggered by self-serving attribution bias (Martinko et al., 2007; Miller and Ross, 1975). Future research could investigate what psychological attribute could trigger CEOs’ hiding action, and the relationship between self-serving (or situational) attribution bias and general internal attribution tendency. In addition, research could also explore whether other psychological and cognitive attributes such as overconfidence (Malmendier and Tate,  2005), the ‘Big Five’ personality factors (Nadkarni and Herrmann, 2010), temporal focus (Nadkarni and Chen, 2014), and hubris (Hayward and Hambrick, 1997) influence how firms respond to performance shortfalls. For instance, those with a stronger present temporal focus are more likely to initiate organizational change as they are more sensitive to the urgency of changing performance shortfalls and engaging in intensive problemistic search.

Finally, while our study focuses on the number of downsizing actions, we acknowledge that heterogeneity in downsizing actions, such as the magnitude of downsizing and the speed of downsizing, may also be affected by performance shortfalls and CEO internal attribution tendency. Exploiting such heterogeneity, future research can explore to what extent firms tend to conduct incremental or radical downsizing. Relatedly, our study captures overall scrutiny by financial analysts using analyst coverage; however, the type of analyst recommendations (i.e., buy, hold, or sell) may also shape CEOs’ motivation to avoid taking responsibility for performance shortfalls (Shi et al., 2017a). For example, when analysts issue a buy recommendation, CEOs with a weak internal attribution tendency may be more likely to avoid responsibility for poor performance. Thus, future research can investigate the role of different analyst recommendations in shaping the relationships between performance feedback, CEO internal attribution tendency, and corporate downsizing activities.

APPENDIX 1 VALIDATION OF THE MEASUREMENT OF CEO INTERNAL ATTRIBUTION TENDENCY

The following steps were taken to validate CEO internal attribution tendency based on content analysis. We first manually classified attributions for 200 randomly selected conference call transcripts, then identified attributions made in each of them. A statement was considered an attribution if a CEO explained the factors behind a performance outcome (be it financial, market, or operational performance). An attribution was considered internal if it focused on factors within a firm (e.g., strategic decisions or managerial efforts), and external if it referred to events outside a firm (e.g., competition, supply disruption, weather, or regulatory changes). In a very limited number of cases, statements contained both internal and external attributions.

A total of 349 attribution statements were identified among 200 conference call transcripts by one author of the study. To ensure consistency, one graduate student also independently coded the attribution statements. Inter-rater reliability between the two coders was high (κ = 0.90, p < 0.001) (Landis and Koch, 1977). Differences in coding were reconciled through discussion prior to validity tests. Among the 349 attribution statements, 219 were classed as internal, 111 as external, and 19 as both.

We then calculated CEO internal attribution for each attribution statement based on our dictionaries. Afterwards, we investigated the relationship between our content analysis measure and manually coded measure. For ease of presentation, we focused on the 330 (i.e., 219 + 111) coded as either internal or external. To evaluate the explanatory power of content analysis, we conducted a logistic regression with the content analysis measure as the independent variable. The dependent variable received a value of 1 if a statement was manually coded as external, and 0 if it was coded as internal. We find that the coefficient estimate of the content analysis measure (CEO internal attribution) is negative and statistically significant (β = −8.46, p < 0.001). The model correctly classifies 71 per cent of attributions, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve is 0.72, which is considered acceptable (Hosmer and Lemeshow, 2000). These findings indicate that our dictionaries capture CEO internal versus external attributions reasonably well.

Prior research (Chatterjee and Hambrick, 2007) has measured CEO narcissism using CEOs’ usage of firstperson pronouns as one of the narcissism indicators, but its focus is mainly on the singular versus plural form (I versus we). Our measure of CEO internal attribution tendency includes both singular and plural forms of firstperson pronouns and, moreover, differs from CEO narcissism in an important way: we focus only on sentences related to causal attributions and then use a ratio of internal attribution words to total attribution words to measure CEO internal attribution tendency. In addition, we also developed a narcissism measure for a random selected sample of 500 CEOs, following Chatterjee and Hambrick (2007). The correlation between these measures of CEO internal attribution tendency and CEO narcissism is only 0.03, alleviating the concern that our measure of CEO internal attribution tendency does not have high discriminant validity.

The list of words pertaining to internal attribution includes I, Id, I’d, I’ll, Im, I’m, Ive, I’ve, me, mine, my, myself, lets, let’s, our, ours, ourselves, us, we, we’d, we’ll, we’re, weve, we’ve.

The list of words pertaining to external attribution includes environment, demand, supplier, supply, customer, client, consumer, government, regulation, economic, economy, market, competitor, rival, competition, weather, disaster, industry.

APPENDIX 2 ROBUSTNESS CHECKS

Selection Bias It is possible that board members select CEOs with a stronger internal attribution tendency and simultaneously lead firms to engage in downsizing actions. In other words, there could be unobservable heterogeneity that influences CEO internal attribution tendency and our dependent variable. To address this issue, we conducted additional analyses using the inverse-probability-of-treatment-weighted (IPTW) methodology, a two-stage selection-on-observables estimation technique (Robins et al., 2000) that helps correct for the non-random assignment of CEO internal attribution tendency. IPTW has been used in prior observational research to mitigate selection bias and adjust for non-random treatment (Almandoz and Tilcsik, 2016; Shi et al., 2019b). This weighting approach is akin to what would occur if all firms had both high and low levels of CEO internal attribution tendency, addressing the possibility that specific types of firms have CEOs with a high level of this tendency.

To implement IPTW, we first estimate a probit model predicting the probability of a firm having a high level of CEO internal attribution tendency. We include all the variables used in predicting restructuring actions in addition to firm control variables and industry dummy variables. In addition, we include the state average of CEO internal attribution tendency as an instrument (based on where a firm is headquartered). Research suggests that firms are more likely to hire locally, such as recruiting top managers from the same state (Yonker, 2017). Thus, the internal attribution tendency of CEOs of firms located in the same state could predict that of the focal CEO. However, the former may not exert a direct influence on the focal firm’s downsizing actions. In unreported probit regressions, the coefficient estimate of state average CEO internal attribution tendency is positive and statistically significant (β = 0.669, p < 0.05), consistent with our arguments.

Other Analyses First, instead of testing the moderating effects of analyst coverage and unfavourable external environment using triple interactions, we also conducted subgroup analyses. In untabulated results, we find that the coefficient for CEO internal attribution tendency × ROA-social aspiration (below) is 14.43 (p < 0.01) for the high analyst coverage subgroup and 6.82 (p < 0.05) for the low analyst coverage subgroup.
