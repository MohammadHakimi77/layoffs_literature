From an agency theory viewpoint, the board of directors serves as the representative for shareholders and is the primary monitoring and supervising mechanism to align the interests of managers with those of shareholders (Fama & Jensen, 1983; Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Achieving effective supervision depends largely on the independence of the board of directors. In a similar vein, the resource dependence theory (Hillman et al., 2000; Hillman & Dalziel, 2003; Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) suggests that board effectiveness is determined by the external resources that individual members are able to bring into the firm. Board directors can reduce firm uncertainty by connecting the firm to outside communities. The evidence for the impact of board gender diversity on firm performance is not conclusive. Some studies have found a positive relationship between female board representation and firm performance, as measured by accounting-based performance or stock market returns (Carter et al., 2003; Christiansen et al., 2016; Erhardt et al., 2003; Krishnan & Park, 2005; L€uckerath-Rovers, 2013). They usually overlook what actions a board with women directors tends to make, and how such actions affect firm performance. From the strategic view of human resources, this study pinpoints a personnel strategy that mediates the relationship between women on boards of directors and firm performance, namely the strategy of employment downsizing. Downsizing is a frequently used business strategy for reducing costs or returning firms to short-term profits. Downsizing has complex consequences on human capital, human resource practices, and employee behaviors, which aggregate into numerous parts of the organization and has varied and profound impacts on the people of the organization (McMahan et al., 2012). It is a stressful, life-changing experience for everyone involved — the victims of downsizing, the survivors of downsizing, and the managers who undertake downsizing. Therefore, downsizing is probably one of the most important decisions for business managers. The strategic view usually states that because of large investment in employees through hiring and training processes, employees should be treated as assets to be nurtured rather than expenses to be minimized (De Meuse et al., 1994). Whether the personnel strategy is decided in terms of an asset-cultivating or costcutting perspective, and hence the decision of downsizing, may be related to gender differences in the preferences or values of business leaders. Previous studies have indicated that female leaders, more than their male peers, are more altruistic, value more self-transcendence, and have greater concern for the well- being of their employees, even at the expense of short-term profits (Adams & Funk, 2012). Female directors tend to emphasize the value of employees and question the cost reduction of downsizing, as downsizing may breach the psychological contract of workers, lower their morale, and lead to more recruitment and training costs when filling the vacancy in the future. This paper uses data of Taiwanese firms to test the relationship between women on boards, employment downsizing, and firm performance. A challenge for the Taiwan labor market, arising from the special Taiwan-China relation, makes downsizing a crucial mediating factor to be studied. Because of China’s rapid economic growth, Taiwan has been suffering from brain drain. The huge Chinese market has created more jobs and faster career development, attracting foreign talent and skilled workers to move to China. This flow of talent is large in Taiwan because people in Taiwan share common language, ethnic links, and cultural traits with Chinese. Many Taiwanese have strong affection and commitment for their original families in China. While downsizing may improve short-term profits, it will create the shortage of talent in the future as the downsized employees, especially skilled workers and professionals, have a great tendency of moving to China. Thus, it is imperative for Taiwanese firms to consider how to retain their employees and cope with rapid brain drain in the job market. The decision of downsizing matters for Taiwanese firms, with the importance no less than innovation activities or other corporate strategies. Considering downsizing as a mediating strategy in examining the impact of women on boards on firm performance manifests the role of women in deciding the strategy related to the survival and development of firms.

The sample of this study is all listed and OTC manufacturing firms on the Taiwan Stock Exchange (TWSE) in 2017. By reviewing their annual reports, we construct a panel data set of the proportion of female directors for all firms’ board elections held from 1996 to 2017. The panel data set is unbalanced because firms may have board elections taking place in various years. This sampling procedure differs from that of other studies applying panel data, as their observations are based on annual data so that the board composition tends to be the same for the years during the board term. By focusing on the time of board director elections, the relationships between changes in board gender diversity, downsizing strategy during each board term, and resulting performance can be more accurately estimated. From the perspective of econometric analysis, our mediation model has significant endogeneity problems. Although using panel data can control for the endogeneity arising from unobserved confounding factors, the model has two other sources of endogeneity: endogenous determination of downsizing, and potential reverse causality between women on boards and firm performance. We will use the dynamic probit model and dynamic panel regressions to mitigate endogeneity problems.

2. Issues and hypotheses

2.1. Women on boards and employment downsizing

The theory of upper echelons (Carpenter et al., 2004; Hambrick, 2007; Hambrick & Mason, 1984) states that top managers differ in the cognitive frames, which determine the interpretation that managers make about the situations they face, and consequently their choices and decisions. Managers’ cognitive frames are greatly influenced by their personal experiences, values, and personalities. This theory can serve as a framework for linking board diversity with board actions. Bertrand and Mullainathan (2003) indicate that business leaders have their own personal styles when investing, financing, and making other strategic decisions. They find that the estimated manager-specific effects on strategic decisions are associated with unobservable characteristics such as experiences and preferences. Gender differences may be an underlying source of such unobservable characteristics. The gender-related literature (Andreoni & Vesterlund, 2001; Babcock et al., 2017; Byrnes et al., 1999; Niederle & Vesterlund, 2007) has indicated that women tend to engage in more prosocial behavior, more altruism, more risk aversion, and shy away from competition. Women are less inclined to secure financial rewards than men, and are more patient in taking actions that give nonfinancial, delayed rewards, or low probability of promotion. Adams and Funk (2012) also suggest that female directors hold more self- transcendence value, like universalism and benevolence, but less self-enhancement value of achievement and authority, than male directors. The gender differences in these core values are not very different from culture to culture.

While traditional stereotypes link firm leadership to masculinity, some studies show that successful female leaders tend to adopt a transformational leadership style rather than a transactional leadership style that is purely male-specific leadership (Bass & Avolio, 1994;; Dezs€o & Ross, 2012). Transformed leaders build the consensus and commitment of employees to the organization’s goals through their influence and charisma, and based on that, leaders create favorable conditions for their followers to change belief and behavior, inspiring them to achieve high levels of performance. Through transformational leadership, women take

This means that female directors may lead their firms to take more stakeholder-oriented actions when there is tension between the interests of shareholders and those of other stakeholders, such as maintaining stable employment and wage levels during periods of declining demand (Adams et al., 2011).

There is evidence that women have a greater orientation towards employees, society and different stakeholders. Matsa and Miller (2013) look at the impact of Norway’s 2006 board gender quota system and find that companies affected by the system have a fairly different employment policy from those not affected by the system. Affected companies tend not to reduce their labor employment after the quota system. This may be due to changes in decision preferences and values resulting from changes in the gender structure in the leadership of the firms. The fewer downsizing practices reflect a longer-term and more stakeholderoriented policy under a gender-diversity quota system. Their another study (Matsa & Miller, 2014) also finds that female-dominated companies are more likely to engage in labor hoarding than male- dominated companies during the recession and are therefore less likely to lay off. More broadly, a number of studies have indicated that women on boards are more likely able to engage with multiple stakeholders (employees, customers, public communities, and environment) other than just shareholders, and respond to their needs, resulting in an avenue for demonstrating the firm’s achievement in corporate social responsibility (CSR) (for a review, see Byron & Post, 2016; Rao & Tilt, 2016).

Fewer downsizing activities and more stable employment arising from the employee (stakeholder) orientation of female directors exemplify that women on boards can bring greater effectiveness to the decision-making of the boards of directors. Based on the corporate governance theories, several studies have stated that female directors tend to bring a greater variety of perspectives, experiences and network ties, to show greater willingness to work together and collaborate on conflict resolution, and to encourage the decision-making considering the different interests of all stakeholders (Baixauli-Soler et al., 2016; Lucas-Perez et al., 2015). These traits provide the necessary independence to the board and improve board effectiveness through high audit quality and less vulnerability to group-think (Adams & Ferreira, 2009; Carter et al., 2003). In terms of downsizing, while organizations engaging in downsizing may have short-term gains by reducing labor costs, they may incur longerterm losses outweighing the initial gains. Hiring and training costs of employees are essentially sunk costs, so stable and long-term employment is the key to maximize the return of these costs (Oi, 1962; Parsons,

In sum, current views point out that differences in core values, leadership styles, and interpersonal relationships that board directors generate because of gender differences may enhance board effectiveness and lead to longer-term, more employee-oriented personnel policies. We propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1: The firms having a greater proportion of women on the board of directors are less likely to engage in employment downsizing.

2.2. Employment downsizing and firm performance

Although a major benefit of downsizing is the purposed savings in labor costs by eliminating redundant employees and streamlining operations, in reality, the benefit is not so much because the financial enticements and severance packages given to downsized workers can reduce any labor savings (De Meuse & Dai, 2012), and because as noted in the previous section, longer- term losses may outweigh these savings. Indeed, numerous studies examining the impact of downsizing on organizations have found that downsizing does not have apparent effects on either increasing financial performance or halting the downward spiral of financial loss, and may even deteriorate firm performance (Cascio et al., 1997; Cascio & Young, 2003; De Meuse et al., 1994; Guthrie & Datta, 2008; Kawai, 2015; Lowe, 1998). Overall, downsizing firms do not outperform (even underperform) the non-downsizing firms in the year of layoffs, as well as the post-downsizing period. There is also evidence that the negative relationship between downsizing and firm performance is particularly significant for certain implementation means of downsizing (Luan et al., 2013; Mu~noz-Bullon & Sanchez-Bueno, 2010), and that investors may amplify negative response to downsizing in periods of industry downsizing waves, in the face of changes in macroeconomic conditions, and subsequent to declining firm performance (Brauer & Zimmermann, 2019).

Psychological contract theory (Morrison & Robinson, 1997; Rousseau, 1995) provides a theoretical explanation for the negative outcomes of downsizing. This theory emphasizes a mutual relationship based on trust

and predictability between employees and employers. Downsizing is often perceived by employees as a breach of the psychological contract, which causes them to retract psychologically or physically and reduce their work engagement and organizational commitment. The negative impact of downsizing on performance may result from demotivated employees, increased voluntary separation, decreased job satisfaction, lower affective attachment to the organization, disrupted intraorganizational social networks, and the failure to retain critical skills, capabilities, experience, and knowledge (Amabile & Conti, 1999; Lopez Bohle et al., 2018; Mellahi & Wilkinson, 2010; Shah, 2000). Psychological contract theory implies that successful organizations are characterized by high employment security. A stable employment policy enables the development of employee–employer trust and enhances communication, cooperation, and organizational commitment. This is a primary human resource policy to increase the capacity of performance and innovation.

Although many management studies might adopt loosely specified empirical models, which ignore that downsizing is endogenously predicted by organizational or environmental factors (Datta et al., 2010), they typically discover that there appear more disadvantages than advantages to lay off large numbers of employees. Organizational distress caused by mass employee outflows may take several years to overcome. For Taiwanese firms, the adverse consequence of downsizing would be amplified. The above-mentioned tremendous talent flow from Taiwan to the Chinese market may reinforce the harmful effect of downsizing. The firms’ rapid downsizing without making an effort on the retention of employees will hasten the movement of downsized workforce to China. This threat on the labor market further worsens the performance of the firms because it becomes more difficult, and frequently at higher salaries, for firms to recruit new employees when there is recovery. Therefore, we have the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2: Employment downsizing has a negative effect on firm performance.

2.3. Women on boards, firm performance, and mediation of employment downsizing

As mentioned earlier, the literature concerning board gender diversity suggests that female directors can lead to a more effective board by introducing different perspectives and ruling out prejudices, obtaining diverse resources and external connections, and encouraging positive board behaviors such as philanthropy programs, better attendance and audit quality. We contend that such improvement in board effectiveness

brought about by women on boards will be notable in the context of Taiwanese society. The stereotype of bias against women is still prevalent in Taiwan. Influenced by the traditional Confucianism, women have been considered more of a supportive role in many aspects due to the prejudice of their limited strength and intelligence. Women are being encouraged to support their spouse and family instead of pursuing their personal career achievement. Therefore, there are stronger cognitive and human capital varieties that separate male and female members of the corporate upper echelons in Taiwan, which may not present in many developed countries. The result is that the rarity of women participation in Taiwanese firms’ boards may make their cognitive contribution more potent to the performance improvement of the firms, once they have been recruited into the boards. We can hypothesize that women on boards tend to positively affect firm performance.

However, this prediction may be more relevant to accounting-based performance rather than market performance. Even though the genderbalance in the workplace has made gradual progress in the past several decades, most powerful women in Taiwan are empowered by their families, and to some extent the family name gains more trust from the investors, as they trust the men behind the name rather than the women in charge. To most of investors, women on boards are just a token of the family firms’ intention of showing their progress. Female directors perhaps really make the board more effective and conducive to firm performance, yet investors perceive that instead of having female directors, appointing the family men behind women to the board will generate higher firm value. Thus, when women starting to join the boardroom, the investors may react to this change negatively by reducing their shares of firms based on their subconscious bias against women. Accordingly, the effect of women on boards on stock market performance is likely to be attenuated or becomes negative because of investors’ bias and unfavorable reaction. Taking all these into account, the following hypothesis is formulated.

Hypothesis 3: Women on boards of directors have a positive effect on accountingbased performance, but have no or a negative effect on market performance.

Firms may have different conditions on the contextual factors for downsizing so that we have to control the heterogeneity in these variables among firms in the regression analysis. Only when the effects of contextual factors are removed can a general and regular relationship between women on boards and the likelihood of downsizing be clearly found.

Some studies suggest that a corporate governance system that has less concern about the short-term shareholders’ interests may facilitate managers to forgo downsizing in favor of stable workforce (Abe, 2002; Chuma, 2002; Matsumoto et al., 2011). Managers are more likely concerned with the interests of shareholders when they face a board of smaller size or of more outside directors (Boone et al., 2007; Linck et al., 2008). Monitoring on managers can be more efficient by smaller boards because they can be cohesive and productive and can make rapid collective decisions for shareholders’ interests. A board having more inside directors, reflecting an employee-sovereign style firm, will make the decisions more in line with the interests of employees. In contrast, outside directors are more independent as a monitor of management; their emphasis on financial control introduces a short-term bias into the decision-making process. Thus, it can be expected that board size and the proportion of inside directors on the board are negatively associated with the tendency of downsizing. Board size (BOARDSZ) is measured as the number of board directors. The proportion of inside directors on the board (INSIDER) is measured as the number of inside directors, who are both the board members and the members of the management team, divided by the total number of directors.

Previous studies also address the influence of financial constraints (B€aurle et al., 2018; Benmelech et al., 2011; Sharpe, 1994). When firms face financial constraints, they have to adjust both labor and capital inputs based on available funds. Not only is the capital investment decision, the employment decision is also greatly influenced by the financial pressure. Financially constrained firms are not able to hoard labor to the same extent as unconstrained firms; they are more likely to downsize to meet the cost-minimization employment. For firms with loosening financial constraints, their ample funding leads to stable employment and lower tendency of downsizing. In the empirical literature, the firms with less cash flow and more debt are typically assumed to have more financial constraints, and such firms are more likely to downsize. The cash flow (CASHFL) is calculated by net income plus depreciation and amortization divided by total assets. The debt ratio (DEBT) is measured as total debt divided by total assets.

A dummy variable (LOSS), which is equal to 1 if the firm experiences negative earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT), is specified to capture this effect. Capital investment tends to be associated with the required changes in employment. The firms with less capital investment are more likely to curtail workforce than those with more capital investment. We control this effect by including a variable of the capital investment scaled by total assets (CAPINV). All of above variables for corporate governance, financial constraints, firm size and age, profitability, and capital investment are measured at the year-end before the t-th board term begins.

Finally, two dummy variables are added. An industry dummy (ELEIND), which is equal to 1 for information and electronics firms, is used to control the effect of industrial heterogeneity. Taiwanese economy has relied heavily on electronics and information industries. Firms in these industries often face rapid industry changes and intense global competition, their decisions to downsize could be differentiated from the firms in other sectors. The other dummy indicates whether the year of board election is in the period of economic recession (DOWNTURN), including internet bubble crash of 2000–2001 and global financial crisis of 2007–2009. It can be predicted that the board members are more likely to downsize in order to survive the downturn when they are elected into the board during those years.

For the variables Vit–1 in the performance regression of Equation (2), we include firm size, debt ratio, and R&D intensity, which are common control variables used in the literature. Firm size is measured as the average logarithm of total assets for the years of the previous board term (L.FSIZE). The debt ratio is measured as average ratio of total debt to total assets for the years of the previous board term (L.DEBT). The implication of the debt ratio on firm performance is that there might be an enhancement effect from motivating managerial and organizational efficiency (Jensen, 1986), or alternatively a detraction effect caused by interest obligations. The R&D intensity is measured as the average ratio of R&D expenditure to total sales for the years of the previous board term (L.RD). The R&D intensity represents the extent to which firms

5. Discussion and implications

Whether women on boards can yield higher firm performance, to a certain extent, depends on the socio-cultural context in which board gender

diversification occurs. Examining the gross effect of women’s representation on boards, we find support for our contention that the cognitive frames and human capital brought to boards by female directors, particularly non- family female directors, increase the pool of information considered in the decision-making and improve the firm’s accountingbased performance. However, in Taiwanese gender culture where the Confucian philosophy has been perpetuated as the quasi-official ideology up to the present day, investors’ bias against women may lead to unfavorable (although statistically insignificant) market valuation of the firms with female directors. It is likely that our results can be generalized to other Asian countries with social values and corporate culture shaped by Confucianism.

These results also echo the findings of a meta-analysis conducted by Post and Byron (2015). They show that the relationship between women on boards and accounting returns is more positive in countries with stronger shareholder protections, as these protections may motivate boards to make good use of the knowledge, experience, and values that women bring to boards. They also indicate that the relationship between women on boards and market performance is positive in countries with greater gender parity and negative in those with lower gender parity because gender parity facilitates investors’ evaluations of the future earning potential of firms that have more human capital of female directors. Over the past two decades. Taiwan government has devoted itself to strengthening corporate governance and protecting shareholders’ rights. This has gradually encouraged the boards of directors to realize the benefits that women bring to the company, making a strong positive correlation between women on boards and accounting performance. Unfortunately, Taiwanese society still has low gender equality, so investors are reluctant to have a high evaluation to the profitability potential of firms with more women’s human capital.

The implication is that if the government intends to follow the experience of European countries to increase women’s representation on boards by legislative regulation or through a corporate governance evaluation system, how to raise social awareness about gender equality so that the majority of investors can have more understanding about the importance and benefits of diversity and inclusion is a crucial task. Otherwise, in the case that market investors cannot support the value of gender diversity behind the recruitment of female directors, the firms that raise the capital they need from these investors will lack the willingness to move towards the goal of high women’s representation on boards. In this way, the legislation and governance evaluation system will not be able to achieve the desired results.

More intriguing results appear when we look into the indirect effect of women on boards on firm performance through the mediation of downsizing. Evidence reveals that because female directors’ leadership style and preferences may bring perspectives that require the board to discuss issues in more depth and to consider a wider variety of stakeholders, the firms with a higher proportion of women on the board of directors tend to implement less downsizing, in particular reactive downsizing, and this talent-retaining strategy constitutes a stable employment policy, which is definitely beneficial to firm performance. Most importantly, although female directors have opposite gross effects on accounting-based and market performance, the positive indirect impact on firm performance is investors’ bias-free, that is, it is reflected on both measures of performance.

This is a noteworthy result because we find an employee-oriented channel (i.e. less downsizing) through which female directors can exercise positive influence on firm performance for both accounting-based and stock market measures. This positive impact of women’s employee-oriented attitude is corresponding to the literature suggesting that women on boards may be a factor in building companies towards more sustainability and producing the benefits of diversity for corporate social performance (e.g. Fernandez- Feijoo et al., 2014; Galbreath, 2011; Shaukat et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2013). These studies show that because of the difference between female and male directors’ moral and social value systems, board gender diversity is generally associated with greater sustainability reporting, more corporate CSR engagement, and better CSR disclosure and rating. In our study, as downsizing is harmful to firm performance, women directors, who adjust board decisions to less downsizing and more talent retention, may enhance corporate social performance, and resulting better financial performance. Therefore, to increase the benefits of board gender diversity for firm performance, efforts may be directed at giving women directors more decisive power over stakeholder management and improving the status of women in the upper echelon of the firms. As mentioned before, Taiwan is experiencing enormous brain drain to China. Taiwanese government and business have been collaborating in devising or implementing policies to halt this trend. We believe that encouraging firms to recruit more female directors, who facilitate boards’ decisions to view employees in terms of talent assets to be fostered, rather than the costs to be cut for pursuing short-term profit, may be a solution to tackle this issue and eventually will be beneficial to firm performance.

6. Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature on women on boards by showing how female board representation shapes the board’s involvement in the

strategy of employment downsizing, which in turn mediates the women directors’ influence on firm performance. The analysis is based on the data of Taiwanese manufacturing firms over 1996–2017. Considerable endogeneity is contained in the econometric model with mediating variables and potential reverse causality, so we mitigate the endogeneity bias by adopting appropriate dynamic models and GMM estimation procedures.

Although gender equality in the workplace has improved in Taiwan in the past two decades, the society is still influenced by the traditional culture, which is often associated with disparaging women. Taiwan also has an economy where fighting the talent drain is an urgent priority for business leaders. Our findings highlight that women directors can be leveraged for higher accounting-based returns and stock market valuation through their less engagement in downsizing. We suggest that government and businesses can make an effort on raising the social consciousness about gender equality, with the expectation that the positive impact of women on boards can be fully reflected on the stock markets. Besides, recruiting more women directors to change the orientation of the decision making towards employees may be a practicable way to deal with brain drain. In our opinion, these policy implications are not specific to Taiwan; they can become a general concern for other countries that confront gender inequality and the brain drain problem.

Given fewer studies examining the relationship between women’s board representation and board strategy involvement, this study proposes a human resource strategy, downsizing, as a theoretically grounded intermediary mechanism that may link two otherwise distal constructs, which are board composition and firm performance. To further explore the relationship between women on boards and firm performance, future research could continue to examine how board composition influences the extent to which boards involve in the strategies that have significant influence on firm performance.
