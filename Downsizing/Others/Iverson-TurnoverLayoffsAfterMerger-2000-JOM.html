Determinants of Voluntary Turnover and

Layoffs in an Environment of Repeated

Downsizing Following a Merger:

An Event History Analysis1

Roderick D. Iverson Jacqueline A. Pullman

University of Melbourne

In this study we formulate and test models of voluntary turnover and downsizing in a hospital undergoing workforce reduction following a merger. Targeted separation packages were primarily employed by the hospital in selecting departments and units as having surplus staff, as well as identifying individual employees. Determinants for the models were derived from the disparate disciplines of economics, sociology, and psychology, as well as demographic and reactions to change variables. Applying event history analysis to data from a sample of 415 hospital employees over a five year period, the results indicate that older, full-time employees, who were less absent, and had an acceptable workload, yet experienced lower co-worker support and responded negatively to the amalgamation of the hospital were more likely to be downsized. Conversely, employees who were younger, white-collar, intended to leave, and predisposed to the amalgamation were more likely to resign. In addition, the differential effects (based on discriminant function analysis) of the five categories of variables found that age, blue-collar, co-worker support, full-time, amalgamation, and work overload discriminated between the two forms of turnover. The theoretical and practical implications of the findings are discussed. © 2000 Elsevier Science Inc. All rights reserved.

Downsizing: Benefits, Trends and Process

Competitive Advantage

The search for competitive advantage has led organizations to become more efficient and flexible in their utilization of human resources. Over the last two decades a common organizational response has been to reduce workforce numbers through downsizing (Brockner, 1988; Cascio, Young, & Morris, 1997). Organization downsizing involves “the planned elimination of positions or jobs” and can incorporate workforce reduction, work redesign and systematic change (Cascio, 1993, p. 96). Its prevalence in management literature and research since the 1980’s has stemmed from many organizations using downsizing as a tool to cut costs, and/or to improve profitability, share prices and productivity (Mone, 1994). Moreover, the increasing trend to put CEOs and senior managers on short-term contracts has added pressure on them to achieve short-term gains (Kets de Vires & Balazs, 1997). Nevertheless, there is current evidence to suggest that the much sought after cost reductions and increased efficiencies have not materialized (Cameron, 1998; Ebadan & Winstanley, 1997). One explanation stems from the process of how organizations decide which positions or jobs to eliminate. It is entirely possible that organizations may inadvertently target jobs or offer redundancy packages to employees who have key organizational competencies and/or be high performers (e.g., dysfunctional turnover) (Cameron et al., 1991; Campion, 1991). The aim of the present study is therefore to provide a better understanding of the processes involved in downsizing. To do this we analyze the antecedents derived from the economic, sociological, and psychological literature, as well demographic and reactions to change variables that serve to differentiate between employees who resign and those who are laid-off (i.e., offered redundancy packages). Downsizing Trends

Although the adoption of downsizing in Australia and New Zealand is a more recent phenomenon, the impact has been no less severe than experienced in the United States. Between 1993 and 1995, 57% and 48% of Australian and New Zealand organizations, respectively, downsized employees (Littler, Dunford, Bramble, & Hede, 1997). In Australia this trend actually increased in the late 1990s, with 62% of organizations reporting they downsized during the period of 1997–1998. (Dawkins et al., 1999). Some 36% of these organizations had downsized once, 30% on two occasions, and 34% had downsized three or more times (Dawkins et al., 1999). Moreover, the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) (1998) estimated that of all employees who have held a job in the three years to 30 June 1997, 7% or 685,400 employees had been retrenched or made redundant on one or more occasions during this period. Consistent with overseas trends in the health care industry, the hospital in the present study downsized four separate times during the study period (Luthans & Sommer, 1999). We follow a sample of hospital workers for five years proceeding the administration of the questionnaire to investigate whether a common set of factors account for layoffs and the voluntary turnover of employees using the statistical procedure of event history analysis. To understand the downsizing strategies implemented by the hospital we provide a brief overview of the process.

Downsizing Process

The public hospital commenced operations in 1991 as a result of a merger between two local hospitals. This required the amalgamation of all hospital services and a change in the traditional structure of medical, nursing, and administration to a matrix structure of programs. State and Federal governments in early 1990s changed the healthcare funding formula which resulted in budget cuts to the hospital. In response to these cuts the hospital sought and achieved major cost savings through workforce restructuring, particularly in the area of support services (e.g., food, car fleet, gardening). Of the original sample of 415 employees we surveyed in November 1992, only 194 remained by November 1997. Workforce reduction was primarily involuntary by means of targeted separation packages.2 This involved the selection and identification of departments and units as having surpluses, as well as the targeting of individual employees. The severance package entitled employees to 2 weeks of pay per every year of service up to a maximum of 10 years. Employees were also entitled to an additional 4 weeks of pay, accrued annual and long-service leave (3 months leave after 10 years of tenure). The maximum for the targeted separation package was 24 weeks pay, plus accrued annual and long-service leave entitlements.3 Part of the targeted approach was 13 weeks of redeployment which if not possible was also paid to the employee. Unfortunately, this engendered a “package culture”, whereby some employees requested to be made redundant only if they could receive a package. However, these employees tended to be in areas that were not targeted. As a

The merger to form the present hospital represents what Lee and Mitchell (1994) characterize as a shock. A shock refers to an “event that jars employees toward deliberate judgements about their jobs” (Lee & Mitchell, 1994, p.60). In this study, we predict how long employees stay or leave after the shock of the merger. This contributes to our understanding of how employees cope with change and the behavioral consequences (e.g., turnover) that may result.

Voluntary and Involuntary Turnover

As raised earlier in the paper, there is debate in the literature regarding the meaning and measurement of turnover (Abelson, 1987; Campion, 1991). This especially applies to the concept of involuntary turnover. In the present study we classify employees retrenched as involuntary turnover (Price, 1977) and those employees who resign (and not offered packages) as voluntary turnover (Price, 1977). Abelson (1987) classified turnover due to layoffs (i.e., reduction in workforce) and dismissals as different forms of involuntary turnover. This classification was again supported by Campion’s (1991) empirical work. Examining turnover data of former employees, and supervisors, as well as the organization’s personnel files, Campion concluded that turnover due to reduction in force be “viewed as involuntary” (p. 206). Nevertheless, layoffs should not be equated with dismissals, as the former is driven by the economic necessity to cut costs and improve efficiency, while the latter is linked with the poor job performance of employees. Bycio, Hackett, and Alvares (1990), for example, in meta-analysis of 45 studies observed an average corrected correlation of rc 5 2.52 between involuntary turnover (dismissals) and job performance. In our study, employees who voluntarily left for reasons other than job related (e.g., spouse moving) were deleted from the analysis as it was deemed to be beyond the control of the employee and the organization (i.e., unavoidable turnover) (Abelson, 1987; Shaw, Delery, Jenkins, & Gupta, 1998). Unfortunately, we are not able to conclusively determine from personnel records whether the turnover was functional (e.g., low performers) or dysfunctional (e.g., high performers) (Dalton, Todor, & Krackhardt, 1982). Although we did collect absenteeism data, our focus was on predicting layoffs and not dismissals. To untangle this complex turnover taxonomy, we draw on three disparate disciplines and a set of determinants derived from turnover research.

To address this deficiency we adopt a multi-disciplinary approach (Hom & Griffeth, 1995; Iverson & Roy, 1994; Maertz & Campion, 1998; Mueller & Price, 1990; Shaw et al., 1998). Variables are included from the disciplines of economics, psychology, and sociology, as well as demographic and reactions to change variables. These variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance, empirical support in the literature, and appropriateness to the research setting and sample studied. Mueller and Price (1990) note that recently, “organizational and industrial psychologists have challenged the strict economic explanation by developing models that key more on employee orientations and the decision making processes that lead to turnover” (p. 322). Sociologists integrate concepts by economists and psychologists and add structural variables (e.g., job related) in their explanation of employee turnover. In our model, we also include personal characteristics of employees, as well as their reaction to the hospital amalgamation. We propose these five sets of variables to differ in their importance in predicting the two forms of turnover. As cost savings characterized the merger and downsizing of the hospital, we expect the demographic (e.g., age) and economic variables (e.g., absenteeism) to be more salient in predicting employee layoffs. In contrast, we anticipate the sociological (e.g., distributive justice), psychological (e.g., intent to leave), and reactions to change variables to be better predictors of voluntary turnover. There is an abundance of evidence supporting the role of job related factors, employee orientations and acceptance of change in explaining employees’ resignation decisions (Hom & Griffeth, 1995; Lee et al., 1996; Mueller & Price, 1990; Price & Mueller, 1986). We elaborate on these variables in the following sections.

Demographic Variables

The first group of variables refer to the individual attributes that employees bring to the hospital. As posited above, we expect personal characteristics to better predict layoffs than resignations due to the targeted approach taken by the hospital.4 Age has been found to have a negative relationship with voluntary turnover (Cotton & Tuttle, 1986; Somers, 1996). That is, younger employees are more likely to initiate leaving the organization. This is consistent with the side-bets or sunk costs argument that older employees have more investments (e.g., superannuation, skills, knowledge) which lock them in the organization (Meyer & Allen, 1997). At the same time, as these older employees tend to earn higher wages, they are more likely to be laid-off in order to reduce labor costs (Barrick et al., 1994).

Although Hom and Griffeth (1995) in a meta-analysis of 15 studies (N 5 6,748) observed that females were no more likely to voluntary leave than males, an earlier meta-analysis by Cotton and Tuttle (1986) reported females were more likely to resign. In the present study we anticipate a positive relationship (Cohen, 1999). As females are over-represented in white-collar occupations (e.g., nurses) (to be discussed later in this section), we expect them to have greater job opportunities outside the hospital and therefore to resign more often (Price &

Barrick et al (1994), for example, in a study of downsized salespeople reported females were less frequently retrenched than males. As males tend to be overrepresented in support services (e.g., gardening) and as these services were targeted first, it is reasonable to expect that more males than females would be laid-off.

In terms of employment status, full-time hospital employees have been observed to display a negative relationship with voluntary turnover (Mueller, Price, & Iverson, 1989; Price & Mueller, 1986). Nonetheless, as cost-cutting underpinned the workforce reduction strategy in the hospital, we expect that full-time employees would be more frequently downsized than part-time employees.

Despite the fact that Price (1977) in a codification of the literature concluded that blue-collar employees were generally more likely to voluntarily leave than white-collar employees, we hypothesize the reverse. This is due to the climate of cost-cutting at the hospital. By focusing on increasing efficiencies in support services such as cleaning and food, it was anticipated that blue-collar employees would be more likely to be downsized. In contrast, white-collar employees because of their employability (Price & Mueller, 1986) would tend to resign. Moreover, as the downsizing process engendered a ‘package culture’, there was a reluctance of blue-collar employees to resign in the event that they may be offered a redundancy package.

Economic Variables

The economic perspective (e.g., Becker, 1964; Burton & Parker, 1969; Viscusi, 1979; Williamson, 1975) focuses on the rational cost-benefit assessment of organizations and employees (Mueller & Price, 1990; Shaw et al., 1998). If the benefits to costs ratio of staying at the hospital outweigh the same ratio at another organization, the employee will not voluntarily leave. From the perspective of the organization, a cost-benefit evaluation of each employee is also undertaken. An employee is more likely to be laid-off when an organization considers that its investments in and costs of retaining the employee outweigh the human capital of that employee. Hence, in the present setting, we posit the economic variables to display a more important relationship with layoffs than with voluntary turnover.

A dominant variable for economists is that of education. It reflects the human capital of employees (Becker, 1964; 1975) and is argued to increase mobility in the labor market (Iverson & Buttigieg, 1999). There is support in the literature for the positive impact on voluntary turnover (Cotton & Tuttle, 1986; Hom & Griffeth, 1995). We also expect education to have a negative relationship with downsizing given that organizations seek to retain employees with greater human capital (Becker, 1975). This is consistent with our previously hypothesis relating to white-collar employees.

The second variable, job opportunity, refers to the availability of jobs outside the organization (Price & Mueller, 1986). The supply and demand created by the job market either restricts or enhances the movement of employees. Therefore the greater the job opportunities, the greater the voluntary turnover (Mueller, Price,

In contrast to the common belief, at the macro-level downsizing is positively linked with upturns in the business cycle on the expectation of impending downturns (Budros, 1997). However, at the micro-level of the employee, it is plausible to hypothesize a negative relationship. Congruent with human capital theory (Becker, 1964; 1975), organizations may seek to retain employees who may be lured away by alternative employment prospects. Moreover, it is also plausible that employees perceiving there to be low alternatives may be more vulnerable to downsizing due to their position (e.g., support services) in the hospital.

Promotional opportunity is defined as the degree of movement between status levels in an organization (Martin, 1979). It relates to the internal labor market of organizations and has been linked with lower voluntary turnover (Stumpf & Dawley, 1981). We also expect a negative relationship with downsizing. Following the merger and restructuring (e.g., delayering) of the hospital, we anticipate that employees who consider there to be limited promotional opportunities are more likely to by laid-off (e.g., blue-collar employees). A critical factor for economists is that of pay, which is considered a primary motivator for employees (Mueller & Price, 1990). If pay is seen to be higher in the present job than others in the labor market then voluntary turnover is reduced (Mueller et al., 1989; 1994). Nonetheless, as these same employees represent costly labor it is reasonable to expect that they are more vulnerable to layoffs. As we control for the demographic effects of age, employment status (full- or part-time), and occupation (i.e., blue- or white collar) and the human capital variable of education (see Trevor, Gerhart, & Boudreau, 1997 for a comparable strategy), we hypothesize a positive relationship between pay and downsizing.

Absenteeism refers to the non-attendance of employees for scheduled work (Johns, 1978; Chadwick-Jones, Nicholson, & Brown, 1982). We focus on avoidable absenteeism as measured by the frequency of one and two days non-certified (medical certificate not supplied) absence. This measure is generally agreed to be associated with an employee’s motivation to attend to work (Brooke & Price, 1989). Both meta-analyses of Mitra, Jenkins, and Gupta (1992) and Hom and Griffeth (1995) report a significant positive relationship between absenteeism and voluntary turnover (i.e., rc 5 .33). Employees engage in absenteeism (i.e., temporary withdrawal) behavior before progressing to voluntary leaving the organization (Rosse´ & Miller, 1984). In relation to downsizing, we also expect a positive relationship. As absenteeism has an inverse relationship with job security (Iverson, Deery, & Erwin, 1995), the motivation to attend work should be enhanced due to the fear of losing one’s job from the merger. Moreover, as absenteeism is a major cost to organizations (Rhodes & Steers, 1990), there would be an increased impetus to target areas and employees with unacceptable levels.

In a meta-analysis of nine studies, Cotton and Tuttle (1986) found a significant negative relationship between the presence of a union and voluntary turnover. This finding is consistent with the exit-voice hypothesis (Freeman, 1980), which asserts that although union members voice greater job dissatisfaction they are less likely to leave than non-members. Moreover, as a basic objective of unions is to protect the interests of their members such as job security (Barling,

Sociological Variables

The third group of variables, sociological, (e.g., Price, 1977; Price & Mueller, 1981, 1986) stress the structural dimension relating to the work setting and environment. As previously outlined, sociologists build on both the economic and psychological models (Mueller & Price, 1990). This approach basically argues that there are specific working conditions that employees find attractive, which if not present will induce employees to leave. Given the changes that were taking place in the hospital, we argue that the following factors will be better predictors of voluntary turnover than layoffs.

Job security refers to the extent to which an organization provides stable employment for employees (Herzberg, 1968). Mobley (1982) noted that organizations that experience fluctuations in employment levels may find it difficult to retain employees due to fear of retrenchments. Hence, following the merger, we anticipate that employees experiencing lower job security are more likely to be both made redundant and voluntary leave due to their position in the hospital.

The social support variables of coworker (degree of consideration expressed by coworkers) (Blau, 1960) and supervisory support (degree of consideration expressed by immediate supervisor for subordinates) (Michaels & Spector, 1982) derive from an employee’s social network. They are important in buffering job stress (House, 1981) and reflect dimensions such as caring about the welfare of employees and listening to job related problems. It is posited that social support will be associated with lower voluntary turnover (Price & Mueller, 1986). In terms of layoffs, Armstrong-Strassen (1998) notes that employees when faced with uncertainty (e.g., following a merger) engage in various coping behaviors. One such strategy is that of help-seeking coping which involves seeking assistance or support from others. Alternatively, employees perceiving themselves to be potential ‘victims’ of the restructuring may cope by blaming others (e.g., coworkers and supervisors) for their situation (Cameron, 1994; Leana & Feldman, 1990). In any event, we predict a negative relationship between social support and downsizing.

The two job stress variables of work overload (extent to which the job performance required in a job is excessive) (Iverson et al., 1995) and role overload (extent to which employees lack the necessary skills to deal with the requirements of their jobs) (Iverson et al., 1995) are expected to display relationships with both types of turnover. Hom and Griffeth (1995) in a meta-analysis observed a moderate positive correlation (rc 5 .11) between role overload and voluntary turnover, while Mueller et al. (1994) reported work overload to have significant positive total effects on voluntary turnover. We hypothesize differential effects of these two stress variables for layoffs. Greenhalgh and Rosenblatt (1984) suggest that in an environment of job insecurity it is “rational for employees who feel insecure to exert more effort in order to become more valuable to the organization . . . ” (p. 443). Hence, a negative relationship is anticipated. Conversely, as the duties and responsibilities of jobs tend to be enlarged following

Psychological Variable

The psychological perspective (e.g., March & Simon, 1958; Mobley, 1977, 1982; Porter & Steers, 1973; Vroom, 1964) in contrast to the economic perspective focuses on the cognitive evaluations of employees (Mueller & Price, 1990). Employees have specific expectations regarding the organization and job. Rather than focus on the cost-benefit approach of the economists, psychologists conceptualize in terms of rewards and punishments (or costs) (Farrell & Rusbult, 1981). The variable of intention to leave is dependent on whether employees expectations have been met since entering the hospital (Vroom, 1964). This employee orientation refers to the behavioral attachment to the organization (Iverson & Roy, 1994). There is abundance of evidence to support that intent to leave is one of the best predictors of voluntary turnover (Hom & Griffeth, 1995; Mueller et al., 1994; Price & Mueller, 1986). Accordingly, we hypothesize a positive relationship between intent to leave and voluntary turnover. A similar relationship is also posited for layoffs. Following the merger, employees may view this as an opportunity to pursue other career interests outside the hospital. Bennet, Martin, Bies, and Bruckner (1995) classify this as problem-focused coping, which rather than being a negative may be a positive reaction to the shock. In addition, due the possibility of receiving a fairly generous redundancy package, the willingness to quit of employees may be heightened.

Reactions to Change Variable

In the present study, the change variable related to the amalgamation that occurred in forming the current hospital. Respondents were asked whether the hospital was a better place to work since the amalgamation. We hypothesize that employees who respond negatively to the changes are more likely to voluntarily leave the hospital (Greenhalgh & Rosenblatt, 1984). Employees whose jobs are less affected by the merger are also argued to be less vulnerable to downsizing. In this situation, we consider that these employees would rate the change more favorably than employees who had their jobs redesigned (e.g., enlarged). Therefore, a negative relationship is predicted.

event history analysis is the estimation of two related functions, the survival and hazard functions. The survival function at time t estimates the probability that an employee will stay beyond time t, whereas the hazard function estimates the instantaneous probability that an employee will quit at time t (given that the employee survives to time t). Event history analysis allows researchers to simultaneously predict both whether an event such as employee turnover occurred and the exact time at which this event did occur. In addition, explanatory variables which are time-dependent are able to be included in the analysis. In relation to age, for example, the hazard function should be higher for younger employees and lower for older employees as they approach retirement age (Morita, Lee, & Mowday, 1993). In this study we employ the regression-analog method as it permits for the “ . . . prediction of survival likelihoods from a set of predictor variables with varying measurement properties, such as continuous, categorical, and time-dependent variables” (Morita et al., 1993, p. 1431).

The measurement of tenure (the time until employees were laid-off or voluntarily left following the amalgamation) was assessed from hospital records as of November 1, 1997. The records reported the employment status of all employees in the sample. Employees were surveyed on November 1, 1992, and those who remained in the hospital were right censored as of November 1, 1997. In the analysis we treated tenure as continuous given the completeness of the hospital records which listed each respondents start and exit date (e.g., year, month, and day) and from the average length of service of the employees proceeding the merger (i.e., 1686 days). As the primary aim in this study was to predict both the downsizing and resignation of employees, we adopted a multiple event model.

In order to further disentangle the differences between these two forms of turnover, we also performed a standard discriminant function analysis procedure using the stepwise entry option (Norusˇis, 1994b). Discriminant function analysis is appropriate when the dependent variable is dichotomous (i.e., layoffs coded as 1 and resignations coded as 0). The advantage of discriminant function analysis is that it allows researchers to both describe group differences and to predict group differences. Specifically, the descriptive component gauges the differential effects of the demographic, economic, sociological, psychological, and reactions to change variables for employees who resigned and those who were laid-off. The predictive component produces a classification matrix to indicate the predictive accuracy of the discriminant function for these two groups of employees (i.e., resignations and layoffs).

Turning to the economic variables, absenteeism predicted layoffs. Counter to our expectations that following the merger employees feeling insecure would be motivated to attend work, absenteeism had a negative impact on downsizing. That is, employees displaying avoidable absence behavior were less likely to be retrenched. Bycio (1992) using data from 46 articles in a meta-analysis of the relationship between individual performance (e.g., supervisory ratings/rankings, non-rating quality indices, and non-rating promotional indices) and absenteeism concluded that on average “ . . . frequently absent employees also tend to be poor performers” (p. 204). As previously discussed, the hospital may have unwittingly eliminated positions containing employees with low absenteeism rates. Applying mixed redundancy approaches (i.e., targeting positions and individuals) as those employed at the hospital sometimes have unintended consequences of removing functional and not dysfunctional employees.

In relation to the sociological variables, downsizing also occurs when coworker support is low. This determinant had differential effects for the two forms of turnover. In the present study we found coworker support to be related with lower absenteeism (r 5 2 .173, p , .05). Hence, coworker support is associated with factors (e.g., reduced costs) that are functional for organizations. We argued that this type of support may reflect help-seeking coping behavior, enabling employees to deal with stressful situations (Armstrong-Strassen, 1998). Conversely, from the stress of the hospital merger, it is feasible that downsized employees may have blamed other employees with whom they interacted for their situation (Leana & Feldman, 1990). That is, they attempt to cope by attributing responsibility to others (e.g., loafing). This may account for the present finding, where downsized employees perceived that coworkers could not be relied upon when things got difficult on the job, were not willing to listen to job related problems, and were not helpful in getting the job done. Finally, it is also plausible that if employees feel a sense of ‘powerlessness’ to avert the perceived threat of redundancy (Greenhalgh & Rosenblatt, 1984) they may compete with rather than support each other (Cameron, 1994). Employees may consider this as the only course of action open to them in attempting to survive the merger.

Importantly, the discriminant function analysis also revealed that by working harder, employees displayed a lower probability of being laid-off than resigning. Employees’ ability to add-value to the organization reduces their perceived job insecurity. Surprising, we observed no relationship between work overload and absenteeism. Hackett and Bycio’s (1996) research suggests, that absence can be interpreted as a coping mechanism for employees experiencing stressful situations. The occasional absences enable employees to maintain their physical and psychological well-being. In the present study, employees rather than reacting to the merger by being absent, reacted by working harder.

The psychological variable of intent to leave was found to have an impact on voluntary turnover. There is an abundance of evidence to support that employees first display a propensity to withdraw before resigning (e.g., Hom & Griffeth, 1995). Interestingly, intent to leave also displayed a positive relationship with downsizing (r 5 .107, p , .05), although it was not significant in the event history analysis.

Yet some care must be taken in regards to the generalizability of the findings. First, the data comes from one hospital which predominately comprised unionized, female, white-collar employees. Second, the hospital in offering certain employees generous severance packages (voluntary and targeted) may have used this as a form of dismissal in managing out poor performers. Further, this would have been much more attractive for older, full-time, high-wage employees due to the superior redundancy package. Third, although we employed a design that measured the various determinants prior to the risk of turnover, these variables were collected at one point in time. This raises concerns regarding their effects. Fourth, the hospital downsized four different times during the study period. Unfortunately, these shocks were not accompanied by repeated surveys of the determinants. Finally, it was not possible to decompose the direct and indirect effects of the determinants on turnover. This may have shed more light on the decision process of employees.

Despite these limitations, our study has provided important insights on how employees cope with organizational shocks such as mergers and repeated downsizing. We identified organizational weaknesses of employing mixed redundancy approaches. For example, employees with higher absence levels were less likely to be retrenched. In addition, an often not cited problem of “package culture” was engendered. This financial incentive became a major demotivator for employees wanting to leave but not targeted for redundancy by the hospital. Importantly, we observed the differential effects of the various economic, sociological, psychological, demographic, and change variables. The utility of fully integrated models, as well as the advantage of applying event history analysis in turnover research (Morita et al., 1993; Somers, 1996; Somers & Birnbaum, 1999) was observed. In a climate of workforce reduction, our exploratory work provides the foundation for researchers to further disentangle the factors that predict employees who voluntarily leave and those who are laid-off. Future research should attempt to focus much more attention on predicting rather than just describing the downsizing process of employees if we are to better understand the antecedents and consequences of such organizational shocks.
