1 INTRODUCTION

The unemployment rate is one of the most widely recognized business cycle indicators. In turn, job separations are important for understanding unemployment fluctuations, especially during economic turning points.1 Therefore, policymakers and economists benefit from high-frequency and timely measures of job separations.2

In this paper, we develop and maintain a timely measure of layoffs using daily establishment-level data from Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act notices. Further, we present evidence that supports the use of WARN data for monitoring job separations, the unemployment rate, and other labor market variables in real time.

We collect data from advance layoff notices filed under the WARN Act. This act requires larger employers to notify affected workers at least 60 days before a potential mass layoff. We assemble about 75,000 WARN notices from 33 US states. For many large states our data begin in the 1990s. We update these data twice a month and aggregate them into an unbalanced monthly panel of state-level “WARN layoffs,” defined as the number of workers affected by WARN notices. We publish the data in real time on openICPSR at https://doi.org/10.3886/E155161 (Krolikowski & Lunsford, 2022). We obtain initial measures of WARN layoffs for the previous month about 2 weeks after the end of that month.

Four findings establish these WARN data as a useful indicator of aggregate job loss. First, in-sample state-month panel regressions show that increases in WARN layoffs predict increases in initial unemployment insurance (UI) claims and the unemployment rate and decreases in employment. The strongest predictions occur over the 2-month horizon, consistent with the 60 days' notice required by the WARN Act.

Second, aggregated WARN data track published layoffs well but are timelier and cover a longer period. We aggregate the unbalanced state-level data to a national-level indicator of job loss (the “WARN factor”) using a dynamic factor model (DFM) that we estimate with the algorithm in Ba ´nbura and Modugno (2014). WARN layoffs implied by our factor are highly correlated with the number of initial UI claimants from the defunct Mass Layoff Statistics (MLS) program and the number of layoffs and discharges among large establishments from the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS). Further, WARN layoffs lead these two indicators during the 2008–2009 recession. Our data also extend beyond May 2013 (when the MLS program was eliminated) and before December 2000 (when JOLTS began), and they are timelier than JOLTS data, which are published with a 2-month lag.

Third, in the vector autoregression (VAR) from Barnichon and Nekarda (2012), changes in WARN layoffs implied by our factor lead changes in the unemployment rate and the job separation rate from the Current Population Survey (CPS). Impulse response functions (IRFs) from this VAR show that the separation rate and initial UI claims rise immediately following a surprise increase in WARN layoffs. Consistent with these IRFs, the unemployment rate rises and changes in private employment fall, with statistically significant responses beyond the WARN Act's 60-day notice period. There is no short-term movement in the job finding rate, consistent with WARN data informing job separations.

Fourth, changes in WARN layoffs improve forecasts of the national unemployment rate and private employment changes in pseudo real time. We use the VAR and Barnichon and Nekarda's (2012) flows model to forecast the unemployment rate, and we also use the VAR to forecast private employment changes. Adding changes in WARN layoffs implied by our factor to these models improves forecast accuracy beyond 1 month for the unemployment rate and 6 months for private employment changes. For both variables, we reduce root mean squared prediction errors (RMSPEs) by 10% or more at longer horizons.

Our findings support the recent use of WARN data by other researchers, although none of that work evaluates WARN notices as an indicator of aggregate job loss. For example, Hall and Kudlyak (2020) use WARN notices from three states to measure mass layoffs. Kudlyak and Wolcott (2020) use WARN notices from several states to document the nature and timing of layoffs at the onset of the 2020 recession. Hernández-Murillo and Krolikowski (2020) study advance notice timing, industry mix, and geography of WARN notices in four Midwestern states during the 2020 recession. WARN data also provide a timely indicator that complements initial UI claims, which have at least two shortcomings. First, initial claims only proxy for layoffs because the UI take-up rate is well below 100% and moves over the cycle.3 Second, during the pandemic, processing delays, duplicate claims, and fraud made initial UI claims less accurate than in the past (Cajner et al., 2020).

2 THE WARN DATA

2.1 The WARN Act

The WARN Act seeks to provide workers with sufficient time to begin new job searches or to retrain. The act requires employers with 100 or more employees to provide their workers with at least 60 days' written notice prior to layoff. WARN notification is triggered by large, permanent reductions in establishment employment, defined in two ways. First, a “plant closing” is when an employment site is shut down and 50 or more full-time workers lose their jobs. Second, a “mass layoff” is when employment is reduced by 500 or more full-time workers at a given site or by 50 to 499 full-time workers if they make up at least one third of the employer's workforce.4 The act also applies when an employer announces that a temporary layoff (less than 6 months) becomes a permanent one. The act requires notices to include the name and address of the affected employment site, the expected date of the first layoff, and the anticipated number of affected employees.

The penalty for issuing too little notice can be severe. If an employer fails to give workers 60 days' notice for a qualifying WARN event, they are liable to each employee for back pay and benefits for the period of violation, up to 60 days. We discuss details about the act and the nonpecuniary costs associated with unrealized WARN notices in Appendix A.1 in the supporting information.

2.2 Data collection, revisions, and creating a monthly panel

We collect establishment-level WARN notices from state employment offices, via websites and contacting state officials. We often extend publicly available WARN data by using digital archives of the internet and documents from state officials. We discuss details about our data collection efforts and missing data in Appendix A.2 in the supporting information.

We currently update our WARN database in the middle of the month and at the end of the month. We consider the number of workers affected by WARN notices in a given state and month as complete when we observe a WARN notice for that state in a subsequent month. We observe the most recent WARN notices at different times for each state because of different WARN release schedules and infrequent WARN notices for smaller states. In Appendix A.2 in the supporting information, we discuss extending historical data and our updating schedule, and we highlight the timeliness of our data relative to the employment report (BLS, 2022) and initial UI claims.

We remove revisions of original WARN notices for two reasons. First, including original and revised notices would overcount the number of affected workers. Second, including only original notices improves the timeliness of our data and minimizes data revisions. However, only some states in our sample differentiate revised notices from original notices. For other states, we identify revised notices with an algorithm, which removes no more than 3% of any state's notices, as described in Appendix A.2 in the supporting information.

To obtain a monthly state panel, we sum the expected number of affected workers by WARN notices across establishments based on notice dates in each state-month cell and seasonally adjust this series for each state using the model in Wright (2013, pg. 80). We refer to the number of workers affected by WARN notices in a given state and month as “WARN layoffs,” although firms do not have to follow through with the announced layoffs. We drop several states from our sample due to restrictions on WARN data, and our analyses use several published data sets, as discussed in Appendix A.2 in the supporting information.

2.4 WARN data coverage

The WARN Act covers firms that represent a majority of employment and our recent sample of states with WARN data covers most of the United States. According to the Business Dynamics Statistics from the US Census Bureau (Census, 2019), between 1990 and 2019, 60% to 67% of employment was in firms with at least 100 workers. As such, the WARN Act covers up to two thirds of all employment.7 In addition, the initial UI claims in our sample of states in 2018 cover almost 85% of national initial UI claims.

2.5 How much advance notice do employers give?

Our WARN data will help assess current and future labor market conditions if employers issue timely WARN notices. In this section, we document the advance notice issued by employers, as measured by the number of days between the date a notice was filed and the anticipated layoff date for each WARN notification.

Advance notice data before 2020 have three features that suggest that WARN notices provide substantial advance notice, as shown in Figure 1 (gray bars). First, there is a large spike at 60 days, consistent with the 60 days' advance notice required by law. Second, almost two thirds of all notices provide 40 to 80 days of advance notice. Third, there is a substantial number of WARN notices with about 90 days' advance notice because NY has required 90 days' advance notice since February 2009, as discussed in Section 2.1. The spike at 0 days reflects employers that file the WARN notice the same day they began laying off workers.

Most employers issued little advance notice during 2020 and 2021, as shown in Figure 1 (black bars). The vast majority of notices provided about 0 days of notice, and only a small fraction provided 60 days or more. This short advance notice suggests that the pandemic was unexpected by businesses (Hernández-Murillo & Krolikowski, 2020). Furthermore, employers sometimes issued notices after layoffs began (negative advance notice) when they, for example, realized that temporary layoffs must become permanent or when they issued a notice with an exception. We find that the median advance notice does not vary much over the business cycle, except during the pandemic recession, as discussed in Appendix A.4 in the supporting information.

4 NATIONAL-LEVEL AGGREGATION WITH A DFM

In this section, we aggregate our state-level panel data to a national-level labor market indicator. Aggregating our data is challenging because the state-level panel is unbalanced in two ways. First, the state-level data do not all begin at the same time (see Section 2.3). Second, states have nonsynchronized data releases (see Section 2.2), leading to a “ragged” edge problem in real time (Ba ´nbura et al., 2013). We could restrict our sample to those states that are available at a point in time and simply sum those states going forward (as we do in Figures 2 and 3), but this approach limits both the historical time-series and the cross-sectional observations and the ragged edge problem remains.

To manage these data challenges, we model our unbalanced panel with a DFM, which we estimate with the expectation maximization (EM) algorithm in Ba ´nbura and Modugno (2014). Our DFM assumes that the state-level data share a common, national-level factor or “WARN factor,” scaled by state-specific factor loadings. In addition, the DFM assumes that the state-level data have state-specific shocks. Intuitively, the WARN factor explains the common comovements in the state-level data while filtering out the state-specific shocks. When states have missing observations, the EM algorithm uses the common comovements of the nonmissing states to estimate the WARN factor. DFMs are common, and other recent research has used them to estimate the common comovements in disaggregated price data (Luciani, 2020) in disaggregated wage data (Ahn et al., 2020) and in a variety of inflation expectations data (Ahn & Fulton, 2020). We describe our DFM and the EM algorithm in this section and provide details in Appendix B in the supporting information.

We first prepare the data. To reduce excess kurtosis in WARNs,t, we use the log transformation xs,t = ln(WARNs,t). If WARNs,t = 0, we call the log of the observation an outlier. We treat outliers as missing as in Ng (2020), and our EM algorithm ignores these observations. Because zeros typically occur in smaller states, treating these observations as missing puts more weight on larger states for estimating the WARN factor in the corresponding months. After dropping 0s and taking logs, we make no other adjustments for outliers. That is, WARN data performed best during the most severe portion of the 2008–2009 recession and the turning point into the subsequent recovery. During these periods, the RMSPE reductions can be between 0.2 and 0.3 percentage points for h = 12. Giacomini and Rossi's (2010) fluctuation test statistics support these findings by detecting statistically significant forecast improvements early in our evaluation sample (see Appendix D.2 in the supporting information). We also find that usinĝ WARNt does not increase RMSPEs in any 30-month rolling window from 2008 through 2019 for horizons h = 6, h = 12, and h = 18.

The IRFs in Figure 7 provide intuition for the horizon of the forecast improvements. The SSUR models use labor market flows to construct unemployment rate forecasts. In Figure 7, a surprise change in̂ WARNt implies consistently statistically significant changes in the job separation rate at horizon 3 and after. Further, Figure 7 shows that a surprise increase in WARNt implies increasingly negative effects on the job finding rate beyond horizon 3, although statistical significance does not occur until after 20 months. Hence, WARN layoffs give the largest improvements in the SSUR model at longer horizons. Forecast evaluation results for the job separation and finding rates align with this intuition (Appendix D.3 in the supporting information).

Row (4) shows the RMSPEs of the five-variable VAR model when forecasting the unemployment rate. Rows (5) and (6) show that including WARN data improves the forecast accuracy of the VAR models and that̂ WARNt improves the accuracy slightly more than the WARN factor. The statistically significant forecast improvements occur at horizon h = 2 and grow with h, as in Panel A. This roughly aligns with the in-sample IRFs in Figure 7. In that figure, an increase in̂ WARNt in month 0 precedes an increase in the unemployment rate in month 1 and a continued gradual rise in the unemployment rate out to about 2 years.

Row (7) shows the RMSPEs of the five-variable VAR model when forecasting changes in private employment. Rows (8) and (9) show that including WARN data improves forecasts at longer horizons and usinĝ WARNt gives larger improvements than using the WARN factor, consistent with the results for the unemployment rate. In Appendix D.4 in the supporting information, we show that forecasting improvements with WARN data are diminished when we subtract manufacturing employment from private employment. These findings are consistent with our findings that WARN notices tilt toward manufacturing establishments and that loadings in the factor model generally increase with the share of manufacturing employment.

Overall, Table 4 highlights the real-time usefulness of WARN data for monitoring the labor market. WARN data provide statistically significant forecast improvements for the unemployment rate when using both the SSUR model, which forecasts the unemployment rate as a function of labor market flows, and the VAR model, which includes the unemployment rate directly. WARN data also provide statistically significant forecast improvements for changes in private employment. Further, in Appendix D.5 in the supporting information we highlight some robustness features of our results. First, our results do not materially change for different choices of estimation and evaluation samples. Second, WARN layoffs continue to yield statistically significant RMSPE reductions with direct forecasts.

7 CONCLUSION

Previous research has documented that job loss is important for understanding aggregate unemployment fluctuations. We compile a unique database of about 75,000 establishment-level WARN notices about impending layoffs. Our data cover 33 US states, and, for many large states, our data begin in the 1990s. We aggregate these data to the state level and establish that they are a useful indicator of aggregate job loss. We find that the number of workers affected by WARN notices (“WARN layoffs”) leads other state labor market indicators, such as initial UI claims and changes in the unemployment rate and private employment. The lead relationship is strongest in the two months following WARN notification, consistent with the 60 days' notice required by the WARN Act. Using a DFM, we aggregate the unbalanced state-level data to a national-level indicator of job loss (the “WARN factor”). WARN layoffs implied by our factor correlate highly with MLS and JOLTS layoffs. IRFs from an in-sample VAR show that the job separation rate and the unemployment rate from the CPS, as well as initial UI claims, increase immediately and persistently following a surprise increase in WARN layoffs implied by our factor. A pseudo real-time forecasting exercise shows that changes in WARN layoffs implied by our factor improve the accuracy of unemployment rate forecasts from 2008 to 2019 in both a VAR and the flows model in Barnichon and Nekarda (2012). We also find that WARN layoffs improve forecast accuracy for private employment changes.

We update state-level WARN layoffs and the WARN factor twice a month and make these real-time data publicly available (Krolikowski & Lunsford, 2022). Policymakers and economists can use our data to monitor job loss in real time and to predict unemployment fluctuations.
