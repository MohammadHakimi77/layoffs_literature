WHO IS LEAVING AND WHY? THE DYNAMICS OF

HIGH-QUALITY HUMAN CAPITAL OUTFLOWS

SIMA SAJJADIANI University of British Columbia

JOHN KAMMEYER-MUELLER

University of Minnesota

ALAN BENSON University of Minnesota

This study proposes a unified, dynamic framework based on turnover event theory to evaluate the effects of dismissals, layoff announcements, and voluntary turnover on subsequent work-unit voluntary turnover. Applying our approach to 1,620 retail stores over 22 months, we show that modeling exit events as a dynamic and interdependent system adds to our ability to predict subsequent human capital outflow. Dismissals had the weakest total effect on subsequent voluntary turnover, layoff announcements had the strongest and most immediate effects, and voluntary turnovers had moderate but lasting effects. We also find that these three exit reasons each exhibit a distinct pattern of subsequent turnover intensity and longevity. Based on characteristics of work units in our setting, our results correspond to a cumulative worker-level average effect of 0.17 quits following dismissals, 0.23 quits following quits, and 2.2 quits following a layoff. We find that these “multiplier” effects are concentrated among workers of similar performance: high performer exits beget high performer quits, just as low-performer exits beget lowperformer quits. Our findings suggest analyses offering individual-level estimates of turnover will generally underestimate the broader, work-unit level consequences of individual exit events on similar workers.

Exit events—including dismissals, layoffs, and voluntary turnover—change operational and social contexts in the workplace, prompting further human capital flows in the form of voluntary turnover (Laulie & Morgeson, 2021). For example, following voluntary turnover events, subsequent voluntary turnover can spread like a contagion (Felps, Mitchell, Hekman, Lee, Holtom, & Harman, 2009; Porter & Rigby, 2021). Layoff events can enhance feelings of job insecurity among those who remain, which results in higher subsequent voluntary turnover

throughout a work group (Friebel, Heinz, Weller, & Zubanov, 2021). Because voluntary turnover is costly and detrimental to unit performance (Porter & Rigby, 2021; Reilly, Nyberg, Maltarich, & Weller, 2014), researchers and practitioners seek to understand and manage the impact of employee exit events at the unit level (human capital outflows) on unit-level voluntary turnover. Previous studies have noted, however, that turnover is not a universally negative event for organizations with regard to the quality of the human capital being lost (Nyberg & Ployhart, 2013; Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020). Given the key role that the quality of human capital outflow can play in the operational and social fabric of a work unit, exit events among high performers may have especially important consequences on subsequent unit-level voluntary turnover.

Finally, does exit among high/low performers have a disproportionate effect on exits among other high or low performers? Although prior work has established that turnover begets turnover (e.g., Heavey, Holwerda, & Hausknecht, 2013; Trevor & Nyberg, 2008), there is still a need for an integrative approach that encapsulates the endogenous, coevolving, and dynamic nature of exit events within the dynamic system of human capital flows. Such an approach can more accurately anticipate the dynamic consequences of turnover events given what it is known about each event.

To produce a model of voluntary turnover amenable to comparing the relative dynamic effects of exit events while considering the quality of the human capital outflow, we build upon the foundations laid by “turnover event theory” (TET) (Laulie & Morgeson, 2021). TET proposes that turnover events, depending on their strength, enact structural changes (i.e., alter job demands, resources, including human capital resources, and social relationships) and influence subsequent voluntary turnover. An event is “strong” according to the degree that it is novel (i.e., the extent to which events are uncommon or unexpected), disruptive (i.e., the extent to which events cause discontinuity and change routines), and critical (i.e., the extent to which events demand attention and influence valued outcomes). TET affords a dynamic perspective that underscores “the complexity of [turnover events] within organizations” and identifies key elements that contribute to the strength, magnitude, and duration of a particular event’s effects within the dynamic system of a work unit (Laulie & Morgeson, 2021: 18). We extend TET by comparing the dynamic effects of different exit events on subsequent unitlevel voluntary turnover. Our theory also provides an explanation of how the quality of human capital lost in each exit event changes the strength of the event and affects subsequent voluntary turnover rates in a dynamic system.

Our theoretical framework contributes to the turnover literature in a number of ways. First, we distinguish between and compare the effects of different types of turnover (dismissals, layoff announcements, and voluntary quits) on subsequent voluntary turnover. Second, we evaluate how the effects of turnover events vary depending on whether the instigating events involved the departure of high or low performers. Third, we treat human capital flow

as a dynamic system, whereby the effects of turnover may be small or large, immediate or delayed, or subject to “ripple” effects if subsequent turnover following an initial event begets turnover in and of itself. From these key features, we provide distinct predictions regarding the dynamic effects of turnover sensitive to differences in types of turnover and the quality of human capital. This approach responds to mounting evidence that exit events are best interpreted as constituent parts of a dynamic system (Call, Nyberg, Ployhart, & Weekley, 2015; Nyberg & Ployhart, 2013; Rubenstein, Eberly, Lee, & Mitchell, 2018). We further respond to calls for a systematic approach that evaluates the collective-level consequences of various exit events while taking into account the nuances of the disruption these events cause and the quality of the human capital outflows (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020).

Empirically, we study turnover dynamics using “panel vector autoregression” (PVAR). This method estimates the effect of a set of variables on the same set of variables in the future; for example, who turns over and why, given who turned over and why in the past. We implement these methods to examine human capital flows at 1,620 work units (stores) at a large national retailer over a 22-month period.

Our theory, analytical approach, and results lead us to a central claim:

Different types of employee exit events, in terms of turnover reasons and differences in the quality of human capital outflows, have varying implications for future voluntary turnover and the quality of the human capital associated with these subsequent outflows.

We hypothesize and demonstrate that, as antecedents of unit-level voluntary turnover, layoff announcements have comparatively large and immediate effects, voluntary turnovers have moderate but relatively long-lasting effects, and dismissals have relatively minor and fleeting effects.

Our study presents practitioners with an empirical method for understanding the distinctive organizational consequences of dismissals, layoff announcements, and quits. In our setting, we find the consequences of exits do not stop with the workers who depart, but, rather, exhibit a “multiplier” effect on overall unit-level voluntary turnover—particularly among workers of similar productivity. These multiplier effects suggest that organizations and researchers underestimate the true human capital costs of exit events if they disregard the interdependence of these events.

THEORY AND HYPOTHESES

A core theme in TET is that exit events, depending on their levels of novelty, disruption, and criticality, can lead to various levels of structural changes related to job demands, resources (including human capital resources), and social relationships. These changes prompt stayers to make sense of the new situation and respond accordingly (Laulie & Morgeson, 2021). Following from these ideas, we derive predictions related to the direction and comparative magnitude of the effects of exit events on subsequent voluntary turnover rates. We also introduce the quality of human capital outflows as a moderator. As noted earlier, the quality of human capital outflows is central to both theory and practical implications for a great deal of turnover research. Drawing on the TET framework, we expect that exit events can initiate a “disruption phase” in the work activities of a unit. A “recovery phase” occurs following a disruption phase (Hale, Ployhart, & Shepherd, 2016), during which organizations employ activities and procedures aimed at stabilizing job attitudes and obtaining the required knowledge to implement new routines. The team adaptability literature highlights the importance of time in the recovery process, as unit members dynamically learn how to adapt to a new context (Burke, Stagl, Klein, Goodwin, Salas, & Halpin, 2006; Hale et al., 2016; LePine, 2003). Although all exit events precipitate disruption and recovery, the magnitude and duration of their effects on job demands, resources, and social relationships vary based on the strength of the event (i.e., the extent of their novelty, disruption, and criticality). These structural changes, in turn, will affect subsequent unit-level voluntary turnover. The proportion of the unit affected by the exit event is another important factor that influences the event’s strength (Hausknecht & Holwerda, 2013). Events that involve more members of the work unit will have a correspondingly greater impact on later structural changes and subsequent voluntary turnover.

Exit Events, Dynamic Processes, and Voluntary Turnover

Dismissals. “Employee dismissal” is defined as termination due to poor task performance, lack of integrity, violation of rules and policies, behavior that disrupts the work unit, or other similar reasons (Batt & Colvin, 2011; McElroy, Morrow, & Rude,

2001) with a plan for later replacement (Maltarich, Reilly, & DeRose, 2020). The literature includes relatively few studies on dismissals (Heavey et al., 2013; Rubenstein, Kammeyer-Mueller, Wang, & Thundiyil, 2019), particularly with regard to subsequent effects on unit-level voluntary turnover. However, this limited literature has afforded key insights into changes in job demands, human capital resources, and social relationships that occur in work units following dismissal events.

Compared to other exit events, dismissals might not cause strong operational disruptions that lead to increased job demands for remaining workers— particularly if the events are initiated by the organization to eliminate those who have lower levels of performance, unreliable attendance, or do not follow organizational policies. Such workers contribute little to the human capital of the unit (Maltarich et al., 2020). Lee (2018) found that dismissals contribute to improving unit-level performance. Maltarich et al. (2020) explained that, for retail firms, a more active approach to dismissals (higher rates of dismissals) can improve a unit’s quality of human capital. Barsade (2002: 669) explained that a work unit can be negatively affected by a disruptive member “who causes the entire group to feel apprehensive, angry, or dejected, leading to possible morale and cohesion problems, unrealistic cautiousness, or the tendency to disregard creative ideas, thus ‘spoiling the barrel.’” The negative effects of disruptive members on the quality of social relationships and unit functioning that increase voluntary turnover (Cole, Walter, & Bruch, 2008; Felps, Mitchell, & Byington, 2006; Hemingway & Smith, 1999; Pelled & Xin, 1999) are therefore alleviated following dismissals. Assuming dismissals are perceived as fair by workers, dismissing unit members who are either low performers, disruptive, or both, can reduce workload and job demands and improve the overall quality of human resources and social relationships within the work unit.

Given the above arguments, compared to other exit events, dismissals are relatively weak. They are moderately uncommon (moderately novel) relative to other exit events. Sometimes, managers are hesitant to even dismiss disruptive employees (Rubenstein et al., 2019). Given that the removal of disruptive members is expected to alleviate unit disruptions and improve unit performance, we do not anticipate strong disruptions, discontinuity, or changes in a unit’s routines following dismissals. Therefore, these events should be only minimally disruptive. We expect the criticality of dismissals to be relatively minimal compared to other exit events. The dismissal

Layoff announcements. “Layoffs” are planned workforce reductions that eliminate certain positions to lower costs. At the unit level, announcements of upcoming changes, such as layoffs, serve as warning signs that jobs are at risk (Friebel et al., 2021; Shoss, 2017). We expect that layoff events’ effects on subsequent voluntary turnover rates will be strongest when they are first announced (Cascio, 2002; Trevor & Nyberg, 2008). To provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of layoff events, we began our analyses at the time layoffs were announced and continued to examine the effects after the layoffs occurred.

The unit-level changes in job demands, human capital resources, and social relations due to the announcement of layoffs are expected to be strong, because, unlike other types of turnover, those positions affected by layoffs are not expected to be filled (Trevor & Nyberg, 2008). These unfilled positions lead to operational disruption and increased job demands for those who remain (Hausknecht & Holwerda, 2013). They are therefore more impactful than other exit events in which positions are usually promptly filled. By increasing perceptions of job insecurity, layoff announcements pose a threat to a work unit’s viability and the effort level of its members (Friebel et al., 2021). From the perspective of TET, because they have such consistent and unambiguous effects on remaining unit members, layoff announcements should therefore strongly affect subsequent voluntary turnover. Consistent with the TET framework, the impacts of layoff announcements on voluntary turnover are expected to diminish over time as the events become less salient (Allen, Freeman, Russell, Reizenstein, & Rentz, 2001).

Voluntary turnover. “Voluntary turnover” events, unlike other exit events, are not initiated by the organization and arise when current employees choose to leave the organization. Therefore, these events lead to a sudden depletion of human capital (Hausknecht, Trevor, & Howard, 2009; Kacmar, Andrews, Van Rooy, Chris Steilberg, & Cerrone, 2006). These unplanned changes result in a lowerthan-desired level of staffing, which necessitates changes to the routines of the work unit. This loss of human capital and reduction in staffing increases

job demands (Reilly et al., 2014) and pressure on other unit members who must divide their resources and attention to cover the tasks of those who have left until new hires come on board (Hausknecht et al., 2009; Shaw, Duffy, Johnson, & Lockhart, 2005). Furthermore, voluntary turnover events disrupt stayers’ social networks and social relationships (Mitchell, Holtom, Lee, Sablynski, & Erez, 2001).

Relative to the other exit events, voluntary turnover is a moderately strong event. Voluntary turnover is seldom as novel as layoff announcements or dismissals to fellow coworkers (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020), but it is at least moderately disruptive as the organization and work group leaders are unable to plan for these events in advance and do not select who will leave. They may also have trouble filling the position in a timely fashion. Research on the “contagion” effect of voluntary turnover suggests that the departure of coworkers in a group signals the existence of superior alternatives (Felps et al., 2009; Krackhardt & Porter, 1986; Porter & Rigby, 2021). The event is therefore moderately critical as it captures the attention of the remaining employees, diverting their attention toward considering employment alternatives that can provide improvements in critical work outcomes like wages, working hours, and working conditions. Voluntary turnover events are not as critical as layoff announcements because, whereas voluntary turnover events signal available or preferable employment alternatives, layoff announcements signal the more salient threat of job insecurity.

Taken together, voluntary turnover is a comparatively low-novelty exit event, with moderate levels of disruption, and moderate levels of criticality. Therefore, we expect that subsequent voluntary turnover will be increased following voluntary turnover events to a greater degree than the inconsistent effects of dismissals (which have moderate levels of novelty and weak disruption and criticality), but to a lesser degree relative to the strong impacts of layoff announcements (which have high levels of novelty, disruption, and criticality). We anticipate that the subsequent increase in a work unit’s voluntary turnover rate in the wake of voluntary turnover diminishes over time largely due to decreased disruption. Following the initial shock of departures, a unit adjusts its routines and returns to a predeparture level of functioning. The voluntary turnover of a colleague conveys information concerning employment alternatives, but this effect also decreases and becomes less salient over time.

Moderating Effects of Human Capital Flow Quality

Next, we turn to the moderating effects of human capital flow quality. We will describe how the quality of human capital flows influence elements of TET. Specifically, our predictions are based on the premise that the levels of novelty, disruption, and criticality of turnover events will differ based on the quality of the human capital of those leaving the organization. Consistent with previous literature (e.g., Campbell, Liao, Chuang, Zhou, & Dong, 2017; Kim & Glomb, 2014), we identify “high-quality workers” as those whose job performance is relatively higher than that of their peers. We index “human capital quality” using average performance ratings for the unit, thereby defining high-quality workers as those whose performance is above the unit’s average.

Human capital flow quality and dismissals. Although there is empirical evidence that low performers are more likely on average to be dismissed (Maltarich et al., 2020), it is possible for high performers to be dismissed as well. Previous studies have demonstrated a low correlation between performance and the type of organizational deviance typically associated with dismissals (Sackett & DeVore, 2001). For instance, individuals with a history of high job performance may still be caught stealing, reducing voluntary helping behavior, showing up late, or missing shifts altogether. All of these activities are grounds for dismissal in most organizations. If the dismissed workers are higher performers, we expect the events to lead to more structural changes related to the job demands and human capital resources of the work unit. The removal of high performers reduces the overall quality of the human capital in the unit (Nyberg, 2010). It is also likely to increase job demands to a greater extent than would low performers’ exit, as high performers are more instrumental to unit performance (Nyberg & Ployhart, 2013).

The quality of the dismissed workers will influence the degree to which dismissal events are novel,

disruptive, and critical. Low performers are typically those who are dismissed; therefore, the removal of high performers is expected to be more surprising and novel to other workers (Maltarich et al., 2020). Due to their track record of making a lower contribution to the work of the unit, the dismissal of low performers is unlikely to create functional disruptions, making this event less disruptive and critical. In this sense, the dismissal of noncontributing low performers is a clear advantage to the remaining workers. In contrast, the dismissal of high performers who have contributed to the work unit successfully is considerably more ambiguous in terms of their leaving being advantageous to the organization, by reducing misconduct or rule violations, but disadvantageous, given that the high performers’ contributions to the workload of the unit are also removed. Additionally, as noted earlier, the basis of the psychological contract is that employees contribute their time and effort to the work group and organization and the organization responds by providing unwritten benefits such as investment in employees and job security (De Meuse, Bergmann, Vanderheiden, & Roraff, 2004; Morrison & Robinson, 1997). Low-performing employees may be seen by their coworkers as not having made this investment, and their dismissal may be perceived as fair. However, the dismissal of high-performing employees for reasons that might not be as salient to the other workers as low performance (e.g., violation of rules and policy) can signal to the remaining workers that high performance does not confer greater job security, leading employees to be concerned about whether their own contributions will lead to job security. The dismissal of high relative to low performers is, therefore, a more critical event.

Hypothesis 2a. Higher leaver performance moderates (decreases) the negative relationship between unitlevel dismissals and subsequent unit-level voluntary turnover.

Human capital flow quality and layoff announcements. Layoffs are based on the financial and operational viability of a work unit as a whole. Therefore, decisions regarding who is to be laid off are not always based on employee performance (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020). For example, a retail organization might make the decision to reduce certain departments from the store like a perfume counter, leading to the end of employment for everyone in the department. Such significant losses eliminate work for good and poor performers alike. However, workers may still expect that those who have higher levels of performance will be protected from being

This elimination of jobs greatly increases job demands for remaining workers, especially in the case that high performers are being laid off. Similarly, if higher performers are being laid off, the human capital resources lost will amount to a greater decline in the human capital of a work unit.

As stated, because high performers are generally less likely to be laid off, layoff announcements that implicate high performers should be perceived as more unexpected or novel than layoff announcements that implicate low performers. Further, the greater the proportion of higher performers who are being laid off, the more disruptive the event will be for operational functioning (Nyberg & Ployhart, 2013). Human capital flow quality and voluntary turnover. Finally, the voluntary turnover of high performers will have especially detrimental effects on work-unit functioning relative to the voluntary turnover of lower performers (Hausknecht & Holwerda, 2013). Studies examining turnover regularly find that, notwithstanding the existence of important contextual moderators, voluntary turnover rates among high-performing workers are on average lower than voluntary turnover rates among low performers (e.g., Allen & Griffeth, 1999; Hausknecht & Holwerda, 2013). This is because high performers are better able to positively contribute to the group, are given more positive treatment by coworkers and supervisors, and experience more intrinsic enjoyment due to their success (Judge, Thoresen, Bono, & Patton, 2001). Therefore, the voluntary turnover of high performers is more unexpected than that of low performers.

Previous studies have noted that the voluntary departure of high performers exacerbates the already negative effects of voluntary turnover on job demands, human capital resources, and social relations (Call et al., 2015; O’Boyle & Kroska, 2017). Therefore, these departures are stronger events relative to the voluntary turnover of low performers: these unplanned disruptions are more acute than the disruption due to the departure of low performers because other unit members must abruptly divide their resources and attention to coordinate the tasks that had been disproportionately completed by high performers (Hausknecht et al., 2009; Shaw et al., 2005). These events are also more salient and critical relative to the departure of lower-performing workers, as they demand more attention from remaining workers to maintain previous levels of unit performance, cover the high performer’s more intensive workload, and train their replacements (Call et al., 2015; Hausknecht et al., 2009). High-performer voluntary turnover events can further divert stayer attention by signaling the presence of viable employment alternatives (Hausknecht et al., 2009; Trevor, 2001). In sum, there is greater novelty, disruption, and criticality associated with the departure of high performers relative to the departure of low performers. We therefore anticipate that the departure of high performers will have a stronger impact on subsequent voluntary turnover.

METHODS

Setting

Our data come from administrative records of a large U.S. retailer. Retail is a notoriously competitive industry, with low margins, substantial training and labor costs, and staffing requirements that can vary widely throughout the year. Because we collected data from a single organization, we were able to “control” for common characteristics (e.g., industry) that may also affect exit events (Shaw, 2011).

We collected administrative data from 1,620 stores that operated continuously from January 2014 through October 2015, yielding a complete storemonth panel. The stores in our sample were relatively large compared to average U.S. retail

establishments. During the study period, the stores employed 93.6 workers (SD 5 33.5) on average.1

The raw data included the date that each employee was hired and the date and reason corresponding to their turnover, if turnover occurred. From these data, we constructed a person-month panel with a continuation code for the number of months a worker was regularly employed without exiting the unit. Following prior studies (e.g., Maltarich et al., 2020; Reilly et al., 2014; Wang & Zatzick, 2019), we aggregated these data to a store-month panel and operationalized exit events as the count of the exit occurrence divided by the store’s headcount.

Measures

Dismissal rate. For each store-month, we calculated the store-level dismissal rate as the ratio of the number of dismissals in a store due to employee poor performance, poor attendance, lack of integrity, or violations of organizational rules and policies to the number of store employees in that month. To calculate the store-month “population,” we counted all the employees who worked at the store in that month without exiting the unit, regardless of their employment status. Therefore, if they left the store at any point during the month, they were not counted in the store-month population.

Dismissal events are comparatively uncommon in this organization. Dismissals occurred every two months on average, and the average number of workers affected by these events was two workers per event. Dismissals are somewhat correlated with performance. However, there are also cases in which otherwise high-performing employees were dismissed due to poor attendance (29% of the dismissals in this category were high performers), lack of integrity (39% of the dismissals in this category were high performers), or violations of organizational rules and policies (35% of the dismissals in this category were high performers).

Layoff announcement rate. Following previous studies (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020), we defined the “layoff rate” as the ratio of the number of

nontemporary workers who were involuntarily terminated due to the elimination of their positions to the number of employees who worked in the store in that month. We excluded temporary and seasonal positions from our sample, because the elimination of such positions is planned at the time of hire (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020). Common reasons for layoffs in our sample included staff reductions and subunit closures. In this organization, layoffs were the rarest exit events, occurring every five months, on average, and in each occurrence an average of three workers were affected. Layoffs within this organization are typically announced in the media about two to three months before the events occur. Therefore, we measured the layoff announcement rate based on the layoff rate three months later, assuming that employees were informed about layoffs within their units approximately three months before the events. In our data, 44.30% of laid off employees were high performers.

Voluntary turnover rate. We calculated the store-level voluntary turnover rate for each storemonth as the ratio of the number of voluntary turnover events in a store to the average number of store employees. Common reasons for voluntary turnover included personal reasons, career advancement, returning to school, and job dissatisfaction. Voluntary turnovers occurred every 1.3 months on average, and six workers, on average, quit in each occurrence. In our data, 41.91% of employees who quit are high performers.

Hiring rate. Although we do not theorize about the effects of hiring on subsequent voluntary turnover, due to the interdependence of human capital inflows and outflows, we included hiring rates in our analysis to ensure we take into account this interdependence (Nyberg & Ployhart, 2013; Reilly et al., 2014; Wang & Zatzick, 2019). We calculated the store-month level hiring rate as the ratio of the number of new hires in a store to the number of store employees in that store-month. Similar to previous studies (e.g., Reilly et al., 2014), we only included external hires who joined the unit from outside the organization in our hiring rate, as rehired employees were mainly those whose contracts were renewed. Therefore, they were not new to the work unit and were familiar with the job and the unit’s culture. Likewise, we did not consider transfers and promotions as new hires because the majority of transfers and promotions were within the same unit. In this organization, hiring occurred every 1.5 months on average, and the average number of new hires in each occurrence was 4.6 workers.

Dynamic Analysis

Testing our hypotheses required a general framework for estimating the magnitude and duration of the effects of exit events within and across work units. Static models, and even many longitudinal models with time lags, are not designed to evaluate

the coevolving and interdependent exit events that determine human capital flows (Reilly et al., 2014). Our empirical approach follows Reilly et al.’s (2014) use of PVAR. PVAR is typically used when a model consists of variables that may be mutually endogenous, auto-correlated, and coevolving over time.

PVAR simultaneously estimates the relationship among variables over time using a system of general methods of moments equations (Enders, 2014; Reilly et al., 2014; Wooldridge, 2010). PVAR allowed us to separate these cross-influences and decompose how a change in each exit event variable affected the other events and their future values. For example, PVAR can determine if higher dismissal rates at Time 1 lead to changes in voluntary turnover rates at Time 2. However, it is expected that changes in the voluntary turnover rates at Time 2 may feed back into and influence dismissal rates at Time 3. Because these rates can depend on one another in complex ways, it is difficult to separate how each is contributing to changes in the others using simpler models that do not take into account these complexities and interdependence. PVAR addresses these concerns by estimating the system jointly and decomposing the marginal effect of each exit event on the other events across subsequent periods. Rather than a single static coefficient, PVAR yields an impulse response function, reflecting the marginal effect of a one standard deviation (SD) shock in one exit event on the other exit events over time (Hamilton, 1994; Koop, Pesaran, & Potter, 1996; Pesaran & Shin, 1998; Srithongrung & Kriz, 2014). Impulse responses can vary in magnitude and duration; for instance, one type of exit event may beget a short-term spate of voluntary turnover, while the effects of another type of exit event may be weak but lingering. We are also interested in whether responses to exit events depend on the quality of human capital outflow.

Empirical Implementation

Several implementation decisions are required to meet the identification requirements of a PVAR model. These decisions involve both the ordering of variables and the number of lags in the model. The decision related to ordering is based on theoretical reasons, such that more exogenous variables enter the model earlier, and more endogenous variables enter the model later. As such, the variables entering the model later only affect the future values of the variables that were entered earlier (Love & Zicchino, 2006). This ordering only affects the estimates of the same-period relationships, but future effects, which

Page 11

are of interest in our paper, will still be estimated regardless of the order of entry. In other words, each variable can predict future values for all other variables, but, in the same-period analysis, each variable predicts same-period values of only those variables that follow it in the order in which they were entered into the model.

We entered the variables into the model in the following order: (a) layoff announcements, (b) dismissals, (c) hiring, and (d) voluntary turnover. Organizations typically make layoff decisions in advance and based on financial performance. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that other exit events do not have a contemporaneous effect on layoff announcements. We entered dismissals into the model after layoff announcements, since these decisions also typically depend on an individual’s long-term track record of poor performance. The order of hiring and voluntary turnover in the model is more difficult to determine, as they could theoretically have contemporaneous effects on each other in the same month. We considered voluntary turnover as the most endogenous variable and the last variable that entered into the model. However, to ensure that this ordering did not impact the results, we repeated the analysis once with hiring as the last variable entered. The results were robust to the ordering.

Next, we chose the number of lagged terms in our model using Andrews and Lu’s (2001) three-model selection criteria—minimizing modified Bayesian information criteria, modified Akaike information criterion, and modified quasi information criterion—and the overall coefficient of determination. Applying the selection criteria to models incorporating one to five lags yielded the result that the PVAR model with three lags was optimal. To address potential dimensionality problems that arise when estimating several latent variables, we applied forward mean-differencing using the Helmert transformation (Arellano & Bover, 1995; Love & Zicchino, 2006; Reilly et al., 2014). This procedure removes the forward mean (i.e., the mean of future observations for each store-month) and yields unbiased coefficients while preserving the lagged observations as instruments in the PVAR model. The resulting PVAR model enabled us to forecast the strength and significance of the mutual effects of exit events over subsequent months. We conducted 2,000 Monte Carlo simulations to generate 95% confidence intervals for these effects (Love & Zicchino, 2006).

PVAR models estimate lagged covariances, which can obfuscate the intuitive meaning of effect sizes for audiences interested in cumulative worker-level

effects. Fortunately, approximating the cumulative effect sizes in standard deviation and converting them to the worker level is straightforward because PVAR uses the same scale for the outcome and predictor variables (SDs) and assumes linearity. To do so, first, for each of the 2,000 Monte Carlo simulations, we summed the 12-month impulse response terms to measure the cumulative effects (in SDs) of a 1 SD increase in exit events on subsequent exit events. We used the distribution of the bootstrapped results to provide standard errors for the estimated effects of exit events. To provide a more clear intuition of the cumulative effect sizes, we also report the equivalent worker-level effects in the Results section.

Theoretical Implications

We contribute to the strategic human resources and turnover literatures in several respects. First, we provide a generalizable description of the factors that make exit events distinct in their impacts on subsequent voluntary turnover. We used the constructs of novelty, disruption, and criticality from TET to differentiate and compare the consequences of different exit events. This enabled us to generate specific and testable hypotheses related to the consequences of different forms of exit events. One important implication of this dynamic and systemic approach concerns studies that treat a single exit event as an outcome that is conditionally uncorrelated with other potential exit events. In general, such analyses may underestimate effects on work-unit exit because effects probabilistically “multiply” across workers of similar types.

Second, we made novel distinctions within the original TET constructs of voluntary and involuntary

turnover events by separating the construct of involuntary turnover into dismissals and layoffs. We further specified between the consequences of layoff announcements versus layoffs. Distinguishing turnover event constructs into more specific exit events allowed us to elaborate their unique effects and shed light on some theoretical models that have not yet been empirically examined. For instance, previous studies conceptually argued that voluntary turnover should increase more in response to voluntary turnover than involuntary turnover without differentiating between dismissals and layoffs (Porter & Rigby, 2021; Trevor, 2001). Other studies (e.g., Batt, Colvin, & Keefe, 2002; Trevor & Nyberg, 2008) demonstrated that layoffs are predictive of subsequent voluntary turnover, but did not include a contrast between dismissals and layoff announcements. Our results show that layoff announcements have a strong positive effect on the subsequent voluntary turnover rate. However, dismissals slightly decrease the subsequent voluntary turnover rate for a short period of time. Concerning the effects of layoff announcements on voluntary turnover, our results demonstrate the importance of distinguishing between layoffs and layoff announcements (Friebel et al., 2021). Our supplemental analyses confirmed that the effects of layoff events on subsequent voluntary turnover rates predominantly occurred promptly after the layoffs were announced. We did not observe effects on subsequent voluntary turnover rates following layoffs. This may be because many of those who intended to leave already did so after the layoffs were announced. Layoff announcements provide signals regarding the outcomes within the organization that lie outside of the workers’ control, including increased job insecurity, the urgency to find a new job, and a breach of a psychological contract. Voluntary turnover events, conversely, provide signals related to options outside of the organization that do lie within the workers’ ability to respond to them. Therefore, those who leave after voluntary turnover may feel they have more time and agency in reacting to the event and will therefore be more discerning when searching for a new position, as compared to those who leave after layoff announcements, who may be less selective out of a sense of urgency.

Third, none of our predictions about leaver performance as a moderator of the departure-subsequent voluntary turnover relationship received significant support. Our expectations about event novelty, disruption, and criticality associated with the departure of high performers being more substantial overall than the departure of low performers did not correspond to the pattern of relationships in these data. These results suggest that, at the very least, there are some important nuances to prior theory regarding the quality of human capital outflows and disruption in work units (Hausknecht & Holwerda, 2013). Our hypotheses were based on the premise that the reactions of the work unit as a whole were dependent on the relative disruption caused by the departure of high versus low performers. It may be that departures have different effects on different types of unit members. We investigated this possibility in our supplementary analyses by distinguishing between the effects of exit events of high and low performers on the subsequent voluntary turnover of high performers versus low performers and did find a pattern of differential effects.

Although we did not hypothesize these differential effects, TET could provide a tentative explanation. The results revealed that high performers reacted to events involving other high performers, and low performers reacted to events involving other low performers. The concepts of comparative levels of disruption and criticality are especially relevant in explaining these results. It is possible that high performers experience greater levels of disruption when other high performers leave. It may be that the departure of high performers is particularly disruptive to the well-established patterns of successful

workers (i.e., comparatively higher disruption) who have a higher level of investment in the work (i.e., comparatively higher criticality). It is also possible that the impact of departing high performers will be less disruptive for low performers because their routines are neither strongly established (i.e., comparatively lower disruption) nor are the workers as invested in the organization (i.e., comparatively lower criticality). Further research should endeavor to address the mechanism through which this specific form of contagion occurs. This higher level of disruption and criticality for different types of workers may be explained by social network theories. Prior evidence suggests that high performers tend to be more connected to other high performers through their advice and friendship networks, and may experience more powerful events when these connections are severed (H^ancean, Perc, & Lerner, 2021). On the other hand, low performers may be more connected to other low performers within the organization, and may be more likely to leave when these connections are severed. Conversely, this effect might also be explained through the lens of social comparison theory (e.g., Porter & Rigby, 2021; Trevor, 2001), where high-performing employees might observe those considered to be similar to themselves making decisions to leave and decide that is the proper course of action for themselves as well. Low performers may not make a comparison between their status and the status of high performers and thus not use these observations to inform their decision-making.

Furthermore, our results imply that the voluntary turnover of high performers may not only motivate other high performers to leave, it also may decrease the voluntary turnover of low performers, making voluntary turnover of high performers particularly costly. This can be because voluntary turnover of high performers might be a particularly critical event for other high performers as it sends signals to other high performers that there are external opportunities, increasing high-performer perceptions of ease of movement (Trevor, 2001). This signal might not be as relevant to the low performers.

The differentially patterned results for involuntary turnover events could also be explained by signaling theory or social comparison theory (Porter & Rigby, 2021; Trevor, 2001). Both of these theories suggest that we evaluate the implications of events in terms of “if it happened to someone like me, it could happen to me too.” This differential result can be linked to event criticality—in other words, events can be particularly critical for certain groups of

As we noted, the involuntary departure of high performers due to either layoffs or dismissals might be perceived as a violation of the psychological contract in which those who have contributed the most to the organization are not given parallel consideration by the organization (Friebel et al., 2021; Morrison & Robinson, 1997). Other high performers might believe that high performance is not rewarded within their organization, making these departures relevant as threats to critical outcomes. Regarding our findings showing that low performers quit more in response to the termination of other poor performers, previous studies have also alluded to the notion that the perceived possibility of discharge may drive some poor performers to quit before they are terminated (Jackofsky, 1984; Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020). Our findings provide some empirical evidence for this proposition concerning the valued voluntary exits of lowperforming employees.

Practical Implications

This paper developed a relative order for the strength of each exit event’s effects on subsequent voluntary turnover, identifying layoff announcements as the strongest voluntary turnover-increasing exit event—especially when high performers are those being laid off. Exit events spill over to future voluntary turnover, so organizations facing high volatility in unit membership may not be able to maintain effective interdependent teams. In light of these findings, the long-standing advice that organizations should make efforts to minimize voluntary turnover takes on an elevated level of importance. Organizations with long training periods or that face the adverse consequences of understaffing or overstaffing might make particularly strong efforts to minimize the occurrence of exit events to increase operational stability and return on investment from developing human capital. Managers and administrators should carefully consider the consequences of announcing layoffs, as, according to our model, layoff announcements are the most salient voluntary turnover-increasing exit event.

Furthermore, organizations should recognize that the departure of high performers has compounding negative effects on unit-level human capital. We found that the voluntary turnover of high performers was associated with a spiraling effect on human capital flows as it increased the voluntary turnover of other high performers and decreased the voluntary

turnover of low performers. This means that the problem of departing high-quality employees is even more dire than our original hypotheses predicted, as a vicious cycle of declining human capital quality occurs. The voluntary turnover of high performers decreases human capital directly, just as the further loss of other high performers in subsequent unitlevel voluntary turnover continues to deplete human capital. However, that low performers are more likely to remain with the unit following the voluntary turnover of high performers further compounds these deleterious effects on unit-level human capital. Regarding the tendency for low performers to follow other low performers after their dismissal, organizations can view these exit events as valued exits (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020). Motivating other low performers to quit and seek out other employment opportunities before being formally dismissed can potentially prevent consequent legal or social ramifications (Trevor & Piyanontalee, 2020).

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Our study has several limitations that highlight opportunities for future research. First, our results can be generalizable to the retail sector or other occupations in which replacement of human capital requires minimal preparation and training. Such jobs broadly correspond to low task-complexity occupations. Workers are also less invested in these jobs because they are simply less invested in their work. If our conceptual model is correct, this means that a unit’s response to losses in human capital might be greater in occupations with higher stability in staffing, greater complexity of routines, and higher human capital investment. These job-related factors correspond to novelty, disruption, and criticality, respectively. Future research might build on our framework by evaluating different occupations and work contexts that are likely to have different levels of event strength. As an example, it might be expected that the downstream impacts of voluntary turnover would be greater in work settings where voluntary turnover happens less often because such events would be more novel and disruptive relative to a very large firm in the retail industry (Oslund, 2019). Second, although we considered some heterogeneity in exit events, such as the quality of departing workers, our data prevented us from accounting for other likely sources of variation in the exit events, such as job level or employment type. effects on workers in various job levels (e.g., management- vs. floor-level workers) or employment types (i.e., full- vs. part-time workers).

Third, while we could operationalize human capital flow quality using supervisor-designated performance scores, we could not ascertain a more objective measure of human capital quality. Furthermore, as performance scores were completed annually, we only had data for those who had been with the organization for at least one year. By accessing objective metrics for employee performance throughout their period of employment, researchers can circumvent this limitation. Future studies should also seek more objective and direct measures of event novelty, disruption, and criticality.

Finally, we found that high and low performers respond differently to exit events and discussed some possible mechanisms that can lead to these results. Future studies could potentially shed light on these results by exploring the mechanisms that led employees to react more strongly to events that involved employees with similar levels of performance.

CONCLUSION

In sum, our study represents a novel extension of previous work related to exit events and quality of human capital outflows. We demonstrate that turnover events, even when they may be perceived as valued exits, lead to different subsequent unit-level voluntary turnover depending on the nature of the event—the degree to which an event is novel, disruptive, or critical, and whether the event involves high or low performers.
