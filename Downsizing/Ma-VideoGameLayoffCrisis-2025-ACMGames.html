From the Atari Shock to a Modern Crisis: Analyzing Mass Layoffs in the Post-Pandemic U.S. Video Game Industry

JIESI MA , Washington University in St Louis, St. Louis, United States

Since 2023, the video game industry (VGI) has faced a severe workforce reduction marked by widespread layoffs, studio closures, and project cancellations, with the United States being the most affected nation. This study employs comparative analysis to investigate the underlying drivers of this layoff crisis, drawing parallels with the 1983 Video Game Crash (Atari Shock) to identify historical commonalities and contem- porary divergences. Using a three-level framework, it analyzes macroeconomic shifts (e.g., monetary policy changes), industry dynamics (e.g., management inefficiencies, rising costs), and project-level risks (e.g., failed live-service transitions, imitative design) to interpret systemic vulnerabilities through established theories in business studies, project management, and game development. Notably, the industry’s resilience—rooted in globalized production networks and diversified revenue streams—has averted systemic collapse, instead containing the crisis as a supply-side turbulence.

1 Introduction

After decades of development, the video game industry ( VGI ) has become a global industry [ 1 ], providing hundreds of thousands of direct and indirect job opportunities worldwide. However, since late 2022, VGI has faced major workforce cuts, with mass layoffs and studio closures posing profound challenges [ 2 ]. During this period, widespread project cancellations and organizational downsizing led to 10,500 job losses in 2023, 14,600 in 2024, and a continuing trend in 2025 [ 3 ]. Notably, every week of 2024 saw layoffs or business closures, disproportionately impacting North America and Europe—with the U.S. most affected [ 4 ]. The Game Developer Conference ( GDC ) 2025 report notes 11% of developers experienced layoffs in the prior year. Corporations cite re- structuring, declining revenue, and market shifts as reasons, while developers attribute layoffs to COVID-19 effects, corporate greed, and previous over-hiring [ 5 ].

2 Research Questions This study investigates the following interconnected questions, examining the phenomenon of massive layoffs within the U.S. VGI through both historical and contemporary lenses:

• What potential factors contributed to the mass layoffs across U.S. VGI following the COVID-19 pandemic? • How do the mechanisms driving post-pandemic workforce reductions compare to those during the Video Game Crash of 1983? • What actionable strategies can developers employ to enhance resilience?

By addressing these questions, this research aims to provide a holistic and historically grounded understanding of the recent waves of layoffs in the U.S. VGI. The findings seek to yield insights pertinent to the present industry context while contributing meaningful perspectives to the aca- demic discourse on VGI dynamics.

3 Background and Context

It is well known that the video games originated in the U.S. [ 10 ]. The origin of U.S. VGI was intrinsically linked to the 1950s–1960s nascent U.S. computer industry, drawing talent from early computer hardware ecosystem around Massachusetts Institute of Technology ( MIT ) and Sili- con Valley [ 11 ]. MIT graduate Steve Russell programmed one of the earliest games, Spacewar! , on a PDP-1 computer in 1962 [ 12 ]. In 1972, Nolan Bushnell founded Atari, which earned traction with Pong [ 13 ]. In 1977, Atari achieved dominance in the U.S. home console market with the success of its Atari 2600 [ 14 ].

Until 1979, Atari retained exclusive software development rights for its Atari 2600. Although maintained dominance in the late 1970s, Atari released numerous overhyped yet poorly designed games, such as Superman [ 15 ], due to mismanagement. Then, four engineers upset at their treatment by Atari left to form Activision. Atari initially threatened legal action but eventually adopted a royalty system for third-party developers. 4 Methodology

Over the past decades, scholars have proposed different explanations for the Atari Shock. A study by Ernkvist proposes a key framework called the “3Ds” model [ 16 ]. This analytical framework attributes the systemic failure to: (1) Declining entry barriers that caused market saturation; (2) Decreasing product differentiation in the market; (3) Disruptive innovations that obsoleted incum- bent firms’ core competencies. This model explains how these factors combined to bring about the Video Game Crash of 1983 and will also be adopted in this study.

Through comparative analysis [ 33 ], this study investigates the causes of the current layoff crisis by comparing it with the historical Atari Shock. Drawing on media sources, published histories of early VGI, and academic analyses, it centers on the business aspects of VGI. Additionally, while the “3Ds” framework explains the Atari Shock’s primary causes, it overlooks the economic context in which early 1980s game projects operated at a significantly smaller scale. Therefore, analyzing modern phenomena like post-COVID layoffs requires expanding the “3Ds” framework through economic, project management and game development lenses. This study analyzes the current crisis using a three-level framework: Macro (economic conditions), Meso (industry dynamics), and Micro (game development practices). Key factors within each level are interpreted using these dis- ciplinary perspectives. The discussion compares factors by consolidating findings from the current crisis analysis with literature-defined 1983 crash factors, identifying critical convergences and di- vergences.

5 Macro-Level: Economic Conditions

In the 2010s, the VGI underwent a decade of rapid growth. This led industry practitioners and observers to become accustomed to the growth, frequently overlooking the impact of macroe- conomic factors on the sector. Yet history provides warnings: As Figure 3 indicates, PC/console sectors endured a three-year downturn following the 2008 Great Recession [ 32 ]. Going further back, an often-overlooked fact is that the Atari Shock was also associated with the global reces- sion of 1982 [ 21 ]. These historical contractions clearly reveal the VGI’s vulnerability to macroeco- nomic trends. Similarly, the VGI’s growth during the COVID pandemic stemmed from converging macroeconomic forces—including expansive monetary policies and surging consumer demand— yet this growth trajectory reversed quickly after the macroeconomic conditions shifted. Analysis of this boom-and-bust cycle from 2020 to 2025 contextualizes recent mass layoffs and reveals pat- terns that may help mitigate future crises.

6.1 Rising Costs and Risks The traditional video game production model carries high risks for investors and developers, as it demands substantial time and capital upfront, while market reception remains uncertain until after release. Investors are exposed to liquidity and market risks [ 72 ], while developers face significant tech and cost risks [ 73 ].

Meanwhile, modern game development faces significantly increased financial risks due to exponentially rising production costs [ 74 ], driven by extended development cycles and team expansion. To meet growing player expectations for performance, narrative, and graphics [ 75 ], teams have expanded to include diverse roles such as design, programming, art, sound, management, and quality assurance [ 76 ]. However, as developers expand their team size, efficiency and productivity do not maintain a linear positive correlation [ 77 ]. This phenomenon, commonly termed Brooks’ Law in software project management [ 78 ], stems from multiple factors. While initial expansion may benefit from labor specialization [ 79 ], exceeding the optimal team size raises management difficulties [ 80 ]. The growth in communication channels escalates coordination costs [ 81 ], and documentation volume increases disproportionately. Furthermore, necessary sharing of critical assets between departments often triggers interdepartmental conflicts in large corporations [ 82 ]. These compounding factors collectively contribute to diseconomies of scale in development [ 83 ], ultimately diminishing team productivity and compromising game quality.

Over the decades, the development timelines of video games have been substantially increased (see Figure 8 ). Release intervals have likewise expanded: Between 2009 and 2015, Naughty Dog consistently put out a new game every two years. Yet after Uncharted 4 (2015), players waited five years for their next title, The Last of Us Part II (2020), and their next game is reported to be released no earlier than 2027. Other U.S.-based AAA studios like Bethesda and Gearbox Software show similar patterns. This temporal expansion, combined with organizational scaling, creates a dual cost multiplier, propelling development costs to unprecedented heights.

For AAA games, marketing expenses that commensurate with development costs significantly increase the total budget. Current AAA games budgets routinely exceed $100 million, with flagship titles like Call of Duty: Black Ops Cold War reportedly approaching $700 million in development costs [ 86 ]. Since 2020, game development costs in the U.S. have reached the world’s highest levels, with per-developer expenditures typically doubling or tripling those in Eastern Europe and China [ 87 ]. These soaring costs were manageable during industry expansion but became unsustainable amid revenue stagnation or decline. Public financials reflect the impact: EA’s net income declined after 2021 [ 88 ], while Take-Two’s similarly fell following its acquisition of Zynga in 2022 and turned negative [ 89 ]. 6.2 Corporate Management Challenges

For major gaming corporations in an industry boom, both investment decision and postinvestment management present multifaceted challenges. According to agency cost theory [ 90 ], key motives for excessive investment activities in large corporations include empire-building [ 91 ], tunneling [ 92 ], or even personal preference [ 93 ]. Driven by private benefits [ 94 ], influential man- agers in such corporations may invest in negative net present value ( NPV ) projects or pet projects [ 92 ], both at the expense of shareholder interests. Research also indicates that managerial overconfidence can lead executives to overestimate investment returns, thereby distorting corpo- rate investment decisions [ 95 ]. While significantly expanding the size of corporation, these invest- ments and mergers do not necessarily enhance its profitability or enterprise value [ 96 ].

Post-M&A corporate integration has also been widely studied, with many studies attributing poor post-acquisition performance to inadequate governance and integration. Overall, factors in- fluencing post-M&A performance are complex, including planning and execution of integration [ 97 ], resolution of managerial conflicts of interest [ 98 ], establishment of suitable reward systems [ 99 ], clarification of corporate vision, and cultivation of a unified organizational culture [ 100 ].

Examining M&A cases within the VGI during 2020–22 reveals that many underperformers were directly linked to post-acquisition integration failures (e.g., [ 101 , 102 ]). For VC-backed small to medium-sized studios, the challenges manifest differently. The highly modular and specialized labor structure required in the game production process compounds these challenges for new ventures [ 103 ]. These companies must navigate a range of issues during development, such as controlling cost and quality [ 104 ]; managing risk [ 73 ]; and handling human capital management [ 105 ]. Startups typically lack resources to address modern VGI’s specialized complexity, while VCs often lack technical expertise to effectively tackle these challenges. 6.3 Hypercompetitive Crowded Markets The contemporary gaming market faces fierce supply-side competition, particularly evident in platform distribution dynamics. On Steam, the world’s largest digital game distribution platform [ 106 ], the annual number of new game releases serves as a direct indicator of the intensifying supply-side competition in the VGI.

In 2017, Steam replaced its community-driven Greenlight system with Steam Direct, which allows anyone to publish a game [ 107 ]. This transition reduced Valve’s oversight of game quality and game supply skyrocketed (see Figure 9 ). A study revealed that while the number of players on Steam increased linearly, the number of games released grew exponentially [ 108 ].

This oversupply has precipitated a pronounced Matthew effect [ 109 ], with top-performing titles dominate most of the player base [ 110 ]. Third-party statistics further substantiate this phenom- enon. According to VGInsights data, 75% of paid games on the Steam platform generate lifetime revenues below $10,000, while the top 1% soar to $7 million [ 111 ]. Steam’s visibility algorithm, which prioritizes sales performance and wishlist accumulation [ 112 ], reinforces market concen- tration by promoting commercially successful titles. This creates a downward spiral where games which have weak launch performance face algorithmic demotion, further constraining their visi- bility and recovery potential. In the hyper-competitive environment after the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of games achieving profitability has become even scarcer.

7 Micro-Level: Game Development Practices

7.1 Live Service Dilemma In the 1980s, most games were designed for one-time experiences with limited replay value. How- ever, the rise of online games and microtransactions after 2000s has embedded the live service model into players’ consuming habits [ 121 ]. The 21st century introduced live service titles—such as World of Warcraft and League of Legends on PC, along with mobile successes like Honor of Kings and Monster Strike —that have remained popular for over a decade. Through ongoing updates and varied in-game purchase designs, this business model secures stable cash flows for video game companies [ 122 ]. By generating reliable revenue that provides resilience against market volatility, the live service approach, also known as Games-as-a-Service ( GaaS ) [ 123 ], has become vital to the modern video game business.

Although the transition from traditional premium AAA games toward GaaS models began to be explored by video game companies in the 2010s [ 124 ], it was not widely adopted due to the high expertise requirements for developers and the need for sophisticated data analytics infras- tructures. Leading companies like Sony and Microsoft continued to rely primarily on traditional AAA models in this period. However, in the 2020s, the increasing development cycles and costs of AAA games have inevitably led to gaps in release schedules and unstable cash flow for publishers. Even industry leaders like Microsoft experienced a notable gap, lacking first-party AAA releases for over 12 months since March 2022 [ 125 ]. Major corporations begun to perceive GaaS titles as both generators of steady long-term revenue streams and catalysts for strategic transformations, driving substantial acquisitions of such games/developers. For example, under Jim Ryan’s leader- ship, Sony Interactive Entertainment pivoted its strategy toward the GaaS model in 2022 [ 126 ]. At its peak, Sony had 12 live service games in development while acquiring studios like Bungie and Firewalk Studios specializing in GaaS development [ 127 ]. Similarly, Microsoft bolstered its portfolio through the Activision Blizzard acquisition, gaining long-running live-service franchises including Call of Duty, World of Warcraft and Candy Crush Saga [ 45 ].

While major companies acknowledge GaaS’s importance, transitioning from traditional AAA single-player games to a GaaS model remains challenging. This shift requires extensive user metric tracking and analysis [ 128 ], with ongoing calibration of in-game economies and mone- tization based on such data. However, most traditional AAA studios lack these capabilities due to fundamental differences in data paradigms: key live service metrics such as player retention and payer conversion are neither monitored nor relevant in single-player games. 7.2 Imitative Strategies

The VGI’s rising development costs, intensifying competition and escalating risks have led to what scholars term an “innovation dilemma” [ 131 ]. Though creativity is crucial for entertainment prod- ucts, the industry now faces shorter product life cycles, rapidly evolving technologies, and shift- ing business models. To mitigate the rising risks, developers have grown more conservative and adopted an imitative strategy, focusing on incremental improvements rather than actual innova- tion [ 132 ]. At the same time, numerous studies have shown that imitation can narrow the perfor- mance gap between industry leaders and followers [ 133 ], or even enable followers to achieve mar- ket leadership through imitation [ 134 ], further reinforcing this trend in the market. This pattern became particularly evident after 2020, with new AAA projects prioritizing graphical upgrades within established design formulas, rather than exploring new gameplay mechanics.

Another reason for the imitative strategy is that it is well-known the VGI is inherently hitdriven [ 135 ]. Few products achieve success, but successful ones generate substantial revenue. The game production process involves high risks, as both developers and publishers find it difficult to predict a product’s sales performance and profitability in advance [ 136 ]. Accordingly, when a game achieves success, imitators often emerge to replicate its success.

Developers generally agree that there is no clear consensus on the boundary between inspiration and imitation, recognizing it as a complex grey area [ 137 ]. Moreover, imitation has become a common practice in the VGI, and some developers acknowledge that imitation is also a part of in- novation [ 138 ]. However, what developers often overlook is that imitation is subject to diminishing marginal returns [ 139 ], and excessive imitation can ultimately exhaust its benefits.

The widely used imitation strategy has led to most original IPs produced by major AAA game companies and released after 2020 being either benchmarked against or imitating existing games. For example, Starfield was marketed as “Skyrim in space” [ 140 ]. However, just like during the early 1980s, as the market becomes saturated with numerous games lacking differentiation, players’ pa- tience gradually wanes. The same phenomenon has occurred among gamers since 2022: Due to insufficient differentiation from the games they emulate, media coverage of these imitative titles tends to be blunt, and players’ feedback is usually negative (e.g., References [ 141 –143 ]). Subse- quently, most of these games underperformed both critically and commercially.

8 Results: The Outbreak of Mass Layoffs The causes of this industry-wide layoff crisis emerging after 2022 are diverse and complex, with layoffs at various companies often resulting from multiple concurrent factors from various levels. From a macroeconomic perspective, the sudden rise in interest rate significantly increased the dif- ficulty for video game companies to secure funding as investors retreated from the risky gaming sector (e.g., References [ 165 , 166 ]), accelerating layoffs among startups relying primarily on equity financing (e.g., References [ 167 –169 ]). Declining revenue and stagnant player growth, alongside pandemic-driven investment boom, led to market saturation and fierce competition. To mitigate risks from rising costs and fierce competition, major gaming corporations adopted conservative and imitative strategies. While established intellectual property (IP)-backed products leveraged existing fanbases, imitative products without an IP faced significantly greater challenges. Players grew increasingly weary of plentiful clones, causing many such games to fail commercially and leading the developers to implement mass layoffs or studio closures (e.g., References [ 170 –172 ]). Meanwhile, AAA studios attempting to pivot from traditional single-player models to GaaS have consistently failed due to a lack of core live service skills: user analytics, in-game event manage- ment, and virtual economy design (e.g., References [ 173 –175 ]).

Furthermore, excessive mergers and acquisitions created significant management challenges, including frequent shifts in parent company strategy and ineffective control of subsidiaries, which also contributed to numerous project failures (e.g., References [ 170 , 176 , 177 ]). VC-backed small and medium-sized studios demonstrated weaker risk resilience due to limited development capability and expertise, usually releasing low-quality games that met commercial failure (e.g., References [ 178 –180 ]). Simultaneously, many metaverse-focused video game companies or internal divisions of major corporations, born of abundant investments during COVID, faced substantial layoffs and

From this perspective, the U.S. VGI, which has been most heavily impacted by the current wave of layoffs, may still experience a period of layoffs before recovery materializes.

9 Discussion: A Historical Comparison Through the preceding analysis, it is clear that, due to differing historical contexts, several contem- porary factors driving the current wave of layoffs in the VGI—such as soaring development costs, the exhaustion of the player demographic dividend, management challenges from overexpansion, failures in live service model transitions, and the collapse of metaverse concepts due to market rejection—were absent during the Atari Shock era.

However, several factors parallel the Atari Shock era (see Figure 9 ), including a VC-fueled influx of new entrants, monetary tightening, market saturation, fierce competition, changing player preferences, and widespread game cloning. Within the "3Ds" framework of the Atari Shock – De- clining entry barriers, Decreased product differentiation , and Disruptive innovation – at least two and a half mechanisms recur today. First, lower platform barriers and excessive COVID-era invest- ments led to market saturation, reflecting Declining barriers . Second, imitative products developed by AAA studios mirrors past Decreased differentiation . Third, while no true Disruptive innovation has threatened gaming, player attention shifting to social media and video platforms partly echo the migration to emerging platforms (such as PCs) during the Atari Shock. Beyond the “3Ds”, monetary tightening remains a critical yet overlooked precursor in both crises [ 198 ].

Notably, the overall impact on the VGI appears less severe than during the Atari era. Unlike the Atari Shock, which devastated consumer demand, the current layoffs have primarily affected the industry’s supply side while leaving demand relatively stable. This resilience stems from two major factors: globalization and diversification.

This growth was driven by broader global capitalism and industry globalization in the early 21st century [ 199 ]. Market instability in the West also drove firms to reduce costs through structural and geographic expansion [ 32 ]. These factors accelerated the globalization of production networks and spurred an explosion in the worldwide player base, transforming gaming into a global phenomenon.

Unlike the early 1980s when players could only buy games from local developers and publishers at physical stores, online distribution platforms now make games from around the world accessible to every player [ 200 ]. This worldwide accessibility guarantees that even when production issues, like oversupply or deteriorating quality, impact one region, gamers can still buy games developed in regions that are less affected. Thus, while the current layoff crisis has hit North America hardest [ 4 ], Europe experienced a less severe investment bubble during the 2020–2022 boom, with coun- tries like Germany less affected by production issues due to fewer mergers and stronger dismissal protections [ 201 ]. Similarly, Japan, where mergers were minimal and which maintains a tradition of lifetime employment [ 202 ], also experienced relatively limited layoffs. In reality, games from Europe and Japan, such as Baldur’s Gate 3, Helldivers 2, The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom , and Palworld , have achieved commercial success in the video game market after 2023.

9.2 Diversification

The diversification of gaming platforms and business models has significantly bolstered the industry’s ability to withstand disruptions. Unlike the Atari era, when players were confined to arcade and home console games, today’s gamers have a variety of options across mobile devices, PCs, and consoles [ 203 ]. Mobile platforms offer a wide range of experiences, from full-fledged apps to instant-play mini-games [ 204 ]. This variety ensures that even if game production and quality one segment of the industry are negatively impacted, players can easily shift their attention to other platforms.

As previously discussed, the live service model has also provided the VGI with stable cash flows and now constitutes a significant portion of industry revenue. This represents another form of diversification in revenue channels. Moreover, it is noteworthy that regions like China and Japan, which have a strong presence of mobile and live service game developers, have been less impacted by the recent wave of layoffs compared with regions like North America and Europe, which are dominated by the traditional premium business model. This resilience can be partly attributed to the advantages of diversification in releasing platforms and business models, which mitigates risks and ensures stability amid industry fluctuations.

10 Conclusion The mass layoff crisis that has gripped the U.S. VGI since 2023 is a multifaceted phenomenon rooted in interconnected economic shifts, industry-specific vulnerabilities, and challenges in game devel- opment. This crisis, while distinct in its contemporary context, echoes the structural fragilities of the Atari Shock in 1983, particularly in its exposure to market oversaturation, product homogene- ity, and the shifts of consumer habits. However, the modern industry’s resilience—shaped by glob- alization and platform diversification—has prevented a collapse of the scale witnessed in the 1980s.

At its core, the 2023–2025 crisis emerged from a confluence of factors: macroeconomic tightening—marked by rising interest rates and liquidity contraction—that reversed the pandemic- era investment boom; industry-wide pressures, including inflated development costs and manage- rial inefficiencies; and game production challenges, such as failed transitions to live-service mod- els, overreliance on imitative design, and the collapse of metaverse-related projects due to market rejection. Collectively, these factors triggered a supply-side crisis, with layoffs cascading across large corporations, mid-sized studios, and VC-backed startups alike.

Comparisons with the Atari Shock reveal parallels: both crises were characterized by crowded market caused by the rising number of new entrants, imitative strategy that leads to homoge- nized products, and shifts in player behaviors. Yet critical differences define the modern landscape. Unlike the Atari era’s near-total demand destruction, today’s industry demonstrates remarkable shock absorption through two adaptations: First, globalized production networks mitigate re- gional instability, as evidenced by limited layoffs in regions with robust employment protections.
