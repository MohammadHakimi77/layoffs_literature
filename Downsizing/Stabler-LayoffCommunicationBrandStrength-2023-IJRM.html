Firms usually undertake layoffs to improve financial performance. However, layoffs often have negative effects on various stakeholders, including consumers. In this paper, we examine the magnitude and duration of the potential negative effect of layoff announcements on brand strength. We also examine how a firm’s communication accompanying a layoff can potentially counteract the observed negative effect of layoff announcements on brand strength. We compare how advertising communication intensity, social media communication (i.e., brand-initiated tweets), public relation (PR) communication, and communication of CSR initiatives moderate the main effect of layoff announcements on brand strength. Using an error correction model and drawing on 366 announcements of layoff events in Germany, this study identifies the magnitude and duration of the main effect. An examination of five years of weekly consumer brand perception data across multiple industries and domestic and foreign firms shows that advertising communication intensity and social media communication amplify the negative impact of layoff announcements on brand strength. Conversely, PR communication and communication of CSR initiatives help mitigate the negative effect. These findings provide guidance on the best way for firms to design firm communication in the context of layoff announcements.

1. Introduction

Layoff announcements appear regularly in leading business and public media and are particularly prevalent during economic crises, such as the financial crisis of 2008–2009 or the COVID-19 crisis beginning in 2020. For instance, in November 2020, IBM announced the elimination of approximately 10,000 jobs in Europe (Bloomberg 2020). Travel disruptions caused by the coronavirus forced the travel firm TUI and the German airline Lufthansa to cut 8,000 jobs and 22,000 jobs, respectively, worldwide (Euronews 2020). Even in the previously booming tech industry, companies laid off approximately 160,000 employees worldwide in 2022 and already roughly 157,000 people within the first three months of 2023 (Layoffs.fyi 2023).

Layoff researchers suggest that downsizing events are a ‘‘triumph of short-termism” (Bidwell & Cappelli 2016) that sometimes have long-term detrimental effects (Sucher & Gupta 2018). Extant research shows that these negative impacts relate to stakeholders directly affected by the layoff such as employees who lose jobs, customers who experience product quality reductions, and shareholders whose stock prices decline (e.g., Datta et al. 2010; Habel & Klarmann 2015; Homburg et al. 2012; Love & Kraatz 2009). Anecdotal evidence suggests that the brand perception of seemingly unaffected consumers deteriorates, too. One example is the former cell phone market leader Nokia, whose downsizing activities in Bochum (Germany) in January 2008 (New York Times 2008) triggered a loss in YouGov’s brand strength1 measure from 46 to 1 (on a scale from 100 to + 100) within half a year. Firms are eager to understand how to maintain their brand strength, and which managerially actionable factors influence their stakeholders’ perceptions during layoffs. However, previous research on layoffs has focused almost exclusively on moderating factors relating to strategy, governance, and HR policies (Datta et al. 2010). Little of this was useful to marketing managers who need to know how to mitigate the harm to the company’s brand. Against this background, our study attempts to answer the following research questions:

1. How do corporate layoff announcements affect consumer brand strength over time? 2. How do different types of firm communication moderate the focal relationship between corporate layoff announcements and brand strength?

We focus on the effects of layoffs on cumulative brand strength over time because managers are interested in the lasting detrimental effects of communicated layoff plans to make better-informed decisions about cutting the workforce (e.g., Sucher & Gupta 2018). In addition, brand strength is a ‘‘sticky” construct that does not change rapidly (Edeling & Fischer 2016; Hanssens et al. 2014), so a consequential impact should be observable only after consumers have been able to fully process the new information and to form new associations in their memory.

Parallel to the communication of a layoff, firms constantly send other messages to consumers, investors, and other stakeholders through various forms of communication. Consequently, the impact of layoff announcements on brand strength may also depend on different types of firm communication activities surrounding the layoff. We compare how advertising communication intensity, social media communication via brand-initiated tweets, public relation (PR) communication, and communication of corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives moderate the main effect of layoff announcements on brand strength. For example, it is not clear how advertising communication intensity impacts brand strength in times of layoffs. Since layoff announcements create consumer uncertainty about product quality (e.g., Habel & Klarmann 2015), consumers may rely more heavily on the firm’s advertising as a source of information that might mitigate the uncertainty and the negative effect on brand strength. On the opposite, consumers know that advertising is costly, and layoff announcements signal cost-cutting activities with detrimental effects for employees. Concurrent advertising communication intensity and layoff announcements may arouse consumers’ distrust and thus amplify the negative effect on brand strength.

To gain insights into how layoff announcements and accompanying firm communications affect brand strength, we analyzed 366 layoff announcements of 82 firms from durable goods, nondurable goods, services and retailing industries from 2008 to 2012 as well as weekly brand strength data. Using an error correction model, we identify significantly negative cumulative effects of layoff announcements on brand strength over time. On average, brands needed seven weeks to recover from a layoff announcement. Overall, a firm’s advertising communication intensity and social media communication via brand-initiated tweets amplify the negative effects, while communications of CSR initiatives and PR communication attenuate the detrimental brand strength consequences.

Our findings contribute to the marketing-related research of layoff announcement effects in several ways. First, previous research has examined the impact of corporate layoffs on metrics such as customer satisfaction (e.g., Habel & Klarmann 2015) or sales (Landsman & Stremersch 2020). We investigate the influence of layoffs on brand strength, which represents a core element of customer-based brand equity (Keller 1993). Second, our weekly brand strength data allow us to determine the cumulative impact of layoff announcements on brand strength over time. Third, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the moderating impact of different facets of firm communication on consumer reactions to layoff announcements. These findings should help marketing strategists better manage the impact of layoff announcements on their brand.

Finally, we would also like to note that the results of the existing literature on the impact of layoffs cannot simply be transferred to brand strength effects. For example, the extant body of literature argues that customer satisfaction often declines following a layoff because customers lose their contact persons in companies (e.g., Habel and Klarmann 2015). In contrast, brand strength has a product-focus and does not require an existing customer relationship (unlike customer satisfaction). Results for corporate reputation (e.g., Love and Kraatz 2009) are also not transferrable to brand strength. Corporate reputation is an aggregate construct with a general firm focus that refers to company goodwill (Ettenson and Knowles 2008; Himme and Fischer 2014). It includes components such as corporate governance and the quality of management (e.g., Fombrun and Shanley 1990). This is also reflected by the operationalization of corporate reputation in these studies by yearly data via Fortune’s America’s Most Admired Companies (AMAC) database.

3. Conceptual framework and hypotheses

3.1. General overview of the main elements of the framework

Fig. 1 depicts our conceptual framework. We define layoffs as the permanent removal of a significant number of employees from the payroll of an organization (e.g., Freeman & Cameron 1993). If the business and public media communicate about a planned or conducted downsizing initiative that includes laying off part of the workforce, we call this event a layoff announcement.

Brand strength is our focal outcome variable, for which we derive the first hypothesis that layoff announcements negatively influence cumulative brand strength. We focus our theoretical hypotheses on brand strength effects. However, given that consumers need to be aware of a layoff announcement in order to negatively consider it in their decision-making process, we empirically test via several pre-studies whether layoff announcements increase brand awareness (see Section 4 below).

Managers face a wide choice of communication forms with their stakeholders (e.g., Dijkstra et al. 2005; Keller 2008). We analyze how advertising communication intensity, social media communication (i.e., brand-initiated tweets), PR communication, and communication of CSR initiatives could potentially moderate the hypothesized negative relationship between layoff announcements and brand strength. We emphasize these forms of firm communication because we believe they offer the most direct instruments for mitigating the potential adverse effects of layoff announcements. These forms of communication reflect different possibilities by which the firm can establish a dialogue and build relationships with their customers and other stakeholders (Keller 2001; Kotler & Keller 2015). Subsequently, we define the four forms of firm communication.

Advertising communication intensity refers to the brand’s industry share of any paid form of nonpersonal presentation and promotion of products via print, broadcast, network, and display media (Kotler & Keller 2015). Social media communication in our case refers to brand-initiated tweets designed to engage consumers or prospects and directly or indirectly raise awareness, improve image, or elicit sales (Kotler & Keller 2015). 3.2. Main effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength

Several theories suggest that layoff announcements may have a negative impact on brand strength over time (i.e., cumulative brand strength). First, the balance theory of attitude change suggests that a person is motivated to maintain balance in his or her relationship with another individual or object (Heider 1958). Individuals seek to preserve attitudinal consistency with each other. Thus, when an individual no longer shares the same attitudes or values as another individual or object, the relationship falls out of balance (Anaza et al. 2021). To achieve attitudinal consistency, individuals consider the decency of firm transactions they observe, even if a transaction does not directly affect them (e.g., Kahneman et al. 1986). Layoff announcements represent transactions that may be interpreted as a violation of fairness standards and an abandonment of stakeholders who deserve support (e.g., Landsman & Stremersch 2020; Love & Kraatz 2009; Pavlou & Gefen 2005). Thus, such layoff announcements may lead to imbalance between the brand perception and the actions of the brand because they potentially reflect an undesired attitude on the side of the brand (i.e., unfairness). To reestablish balance, the individual must adjust his or her attitude with a more negative perception of brand strength.

Second, social relationship theory suggests that individuals build relationships with brands in a manner similar to how they form relationships with other people in a social context (Aggarwal 2004). Such relationships carry with them norms of behavior that guide individuals’ evaluations of their relationship partner. In the context of layoff announcements, we can conclude that when individuals form relationships with brands that mirror their social relationships, norms of social relationships are used as guiding principles in their interactions with brands. When brands behave like socialized members of a culture, they are evaluated by the rules that govern the society and must act in line with these rules (e.g., Bhattacharya & Sen 2003). Although layoffs may be legally harmless, individuals may perceive layoffs as a violation of a brand’s relationship with its employees (De Meuse et al. 2004). This perceived misconduct may disturb the relationship between the brand and individuals, negatively affecting brand strength.

Third, from an information economics perspective, products can be classified into three categories—search, experience, and credence goods—that reflect the individual’s ability to assess product quality before actual use (Darby & Karni 1973). For many goods, judging quality ex ante is difficult. Thus, individuals may perceive high risks in purchase decisions

3.3. Attributes that differentiate firm communication with stakeholders

The extent to which the assumed negative layoff announcement–brand strength effect is amplified or mitigated by forms of firm communication is likely to be contingent on perceived attributes that are associated with each type of firm communication. Put differently, consumers might associate certain characteristics such as high costs or an entertainment focus with each type of firm communication, which could moderate the assumed negative layoff announcement–brand strength effect.

The literature suggests a wide range of attributes that distinguish different types of firm communication (e.g., Batra & Keller 2016; Danaher & Rossiter 2011; Katz et al. 1973; Kotler & Keller 2015). Two steps are necessary: First, we need to select from this wide range of attributes those that are relevant in the context of layoff announcements. Second, we need to evaluate the four forms of firm communication according to these selected communication attributes to derive their amplifying or mitigating effect. While both steps could be based solely on theoretical reasoning, we additionally conducted a consumer survey as an exploratory study to guide hypothesis development (Section 3.3.1, Tables 2 and 3). After describing the selected attributes in sections 3.3.2 – 3.3.7, we use Section 3.4 to formulate our moderation hypotheses based on the findings of the exploratory survey and theoretical arguments (see Table 4).

3.3.1. Exploratory consumer survey to support hypothesis development For first insights on how consumers differentiate and understand the selected types of firm communication and potential communication attributes in the context of layoff announcements, we conducted an online survey with 99 consumers in Germany who were recruited via Clickworker (mean age = 38.6, 61.1% male) in May 2022 (see Web Appendix A for details). This exploratory study provides two sets of results. First, we were interested in identifying communication attributes that are critical in the context of layoff announcements. We started with a list of nine potential attributes, which were based partially on intuition (‘‘high cost” and ‘‘benefit to other stakeholders”) and on an article by Danaher & Rossiter (2011) on marketing communication perceptions. Of these, six attributes were evaluated with a score of 6 or higher (using a Likert scale assessing criticality of attributes from 0 [very low] to 10 [very high]). As Table 2 shows, we find that the attributes ‘‘high cost”, ‘‘trustworthiness”, ‘‘entertaining‘‘, ‘‘informative”, ‘‘benefit to other stakeholders” and ‘‘annoying” are perceived as more critical than the other three attributes: ‘‘time-consuming”, ‘‘easy to reject” and ‘‘disruptive”. This empirical finding and the goal to keep the number of attributes manageable led to our decision to include six attributes in our theoretical reasoning.

Second, we asked consumers to evaluate the selected forms of firm communication according to the six focal attributes (regardless of layoffs). Table 3 shows the results. For example, we found that consumers perceive advertising communication intensity and communication of CSR activities as significantly more costly than PR and social media communication. This exploratory analysis guides and supports the hypothesis development.

3.3.2. Communication costs The attribute communication costs describes how costly consumers perceive a type of firm communication to be. Research shows that perceived communication costs influence consumers’ brand perception (Homer 1995; Kirmani 1990; Kirmani and Wright 1989). As the ratings in Panel A in Table 3 and prior research shows (Barnett and Salomon 2012; Kirmani 1990), advertising and CSR are associated with high costs, while PR and social media are perceived to be low-cost forms of communication. As consumers perceive layoffs as a cost-cutting action that significantly affects the socioeconomic conditions of employees (Landsman & Stremersch 2020), it is conceivable that the perception of the brand through costintensive forms of communication may amplify the perceived imbalance between the brand image and its actions, according to balance theory.

3.3.3. Beneficial to other stakeholder groups The attribute beneficial to other stakeholder groups describes to what degree the form of firm communication is perceived to benefit stakeholders other than employees. Granting direct benefits to other stakeholder groups is a key differentiator within our focal forms of firm communication (e.g., Batra & Keller 2016; see also Panel B in Table 3). Advertising, social media, and, maybe to a lesser degree, PR aim to persuade the consumer to buy the product (Landsman & Stremersch 2020). In contrast, communication of CSR initiatives signals direct benefits to other stakeholders in addition to consumers as it shows that the firm has improved a variety of stakeholders’ socioeconomic conditions. In the context of layoff announcements, consumers often feel that the welfare of laid-off, and possibly also existing employees, is significantly reduced (e.g., Love & Kraatz 2009). According to balance theory, compensating signals (e.g., CSR initiatives for the general society or specific other stakeholder groups) may mitigate the perceived imbalance between the brand perception and brand actions (e.g., Anaza et al. 2021).

3.3.4. Trustworthiness The attribute trustworthiness describes whether consumers perceive firm communications as honest and truthful. According to social relationship theory, a high level of trustworthiness is a key element of a well-functioning relationship between brands and consumers (e.g., Bhattacharya & Sen 2003). Our focal forms of firm communication differ regarding their perceived level of trustworthiness (e.g., Danaher & Rossiter 2011; see Panel C in Table 3). Advertising and social media are sent unfiltered directly to consumers. Research has shown that PR and CSR communication lead to news stories in public media, which audiences tend to perceive as more trustworthy than paid media such as advertising (e.g., Keller 2001; Nielsen 2015). Following layoff announcements, consumers often sympathize with the affected employees and consider the firm’s brands less trustworthy (e.g., Klein et al. 2004). In terms of social relationship theory, consumers’ level of trust in the brand may therefore be further damaged by any firm communication that is perceived by the public to be less trustworthy (i.e., advertising and social media communication).

3.3.5. Entertaining The attribute entertaining describes whether consumers perceive a form of communication as providing amusement or enjoyment. Advertising and social media are often designed and perceived to be more entertaining than communication of CSR initiatives and PR communication (e.g., Danaher & Rossiter 2011; Kotler & Keller 2015; see Panel D in Table 3). Layoffs, on the contrary, have a variety of negative influences on both dismissed and retained employees (e.g., Flanagan & O’Shaughnessy 2005) that are not associated with entertainment at all. Layoffs reduce the motivation, satisfaction, and commitment of those who remain and undermine the work climate. Against this background, consumers may pay attention to how seriously a brand behaves in the wake of layoff announcements (e.g., Flanagan & O’Shaughnessy 2005). According to balance theory, if a brand appears too funny or entertaining against such a serious background, consumers will have inconsistent perceptions between the brand and its actions.

3.3.6. Informative The attribute informative describes to what degree consumers perceive firm communication as being able to provide useful and detailed information. Our focal types of firm communication differ according to how informative they are perceived to be (e.g., Danaher & Rossiter 2011; Thomas & Sullivan 2005; see Panel E in Table 3). While advertising and social media also serve an informative role (e.g., Landsman & Stremersch 2020), this role, unlike for PR communication and communication of CSR initiatives, is secondary to entertainment (e.g., Batra & Keller 2016; Kotler & Keller 2015). Based on an information economics perspective, brand strength is a function of consumers’ confidence in the brand’s ability to fulfill promised benefits (e.g., Fischer et al. 2010). Layoff announcements may lead consumers to question this ability and may create uncertainty among consumers (e.g., Habel & Klarmann 2015). In such contexts, consumers may rely more heavily on more informative types of firm communication such as PR communication to mitigate such uncertainty.

3.3.7. Annoyance The attribute annoyance describes whether consumers feel irritated by certain types of firm communication. Annoyance is a frequent and relevant differentiating attribute of firm communication (e.g., Kronrod & Huber 2019; Shen & Villas-Boas 2018). Consumers find advertising much more intrusive and annoying than PR and the communication of CSR initiatives (e.g., Batra & Keller 2016; Danaher & Rossiter 2011; see Panel F in Table 3). A firm that announces layoffs may be perceived as constituting a broken commitment to its employees, which is annoying (Skarlicki et al. 1998). In line with social relationship theory, in the context of layoff announcements, consumers’ perception of a brand may be further aggravated by firm communication that is perceived to be annoying.

3.4.1. Advertising communication intensity and social media communication Advertising communication intensity and social media communication are perceived to provide no direct benefits to other stakeholders. Advertising’s and social media’s main aim is to persuade consumers to buy the product or to build up a brand image (Batra & Keller 2016; Landsman & Stremersch 2020). In this respect, they benefit consumers in the sense that they facilitate the purchase decision process (e.g., Dijkstra et al. 2005). Other stakeholders, on the other hand, cannot derive any further direct benefit from advertising and social media communication (e.g., Kotler & Keller 2015). According to balance theory, the negative effects of layoff announcements are increased because advertising and social media cannot compensate for the reduced benefits of existing/remaining employees by addressing the needs of other stakeholders.

Advertising and social media communication are both considered relatively less trustworthy, more entertaining with less information content, and more annoying (e.g., Batra & Keller 2016; Danaher & Rossiter 2011). Layoff announcements reduce trust in a brand, and advertising as well as social media communication may also be perceived negatively as these messages are sent from a source the recipient now trusts less (Landsman & Stremersch 2020; Van Heerde et al. 2007). High entertainment through advertising and social media communication may be perceived as inappropriate if the consumer is aware that employees are ‘‘suffering”. Consequently, perceived inconsistency of brand image and brand action may increase, and therefore, the negative impact of layoff announcements on brand strength may be amplified. Layoff announcements may create uncertainty with respect to product quality (e.g., Homburg et al. 2012), and advertising and social media communication as a source of information may mitigate such uncertainty (Landsman & Stremersch 2020). However, this function of providing information is relatively subordinate to other goals, such as entertainment value or image conveyance (e.g., Danaher & Rossiter 2011). Consumers perceive advertising and social media communication as relatively annoying (e.g., Kotler & Keller 2015. Being confronted with an annoying layoff announcement and advertising and social media communication at the same time may therefore lead to even stronger disidentification with the brand (Anaza et al. 2021).

Finally, research has shown that consumers associate advertising communication with high costs for the company (Kirmani & Wright 1989; Kirmani 1990). Accordingly, high advertising communication costs are not seen as compatible with a strategy of cost-cutting measures such as layoffs. In contrast to advertising, social media is perceived as less costly (Kotler & Keller 2015), which means social media messages may provide a better buffer for the negative effect of layoff announcements on the company’s brand. In sum, we suggest that the six attributes taken together provide evidence that advertising communication intensity and social media communication in the context of layoff announcements strengthen the negative effect of layoff announcements on brand strength (see Table 4 again). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2: Advertising communication intensity amplifies the negative effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength.

H3: Social media communication amplifies the negative effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength.

3.4.2. PR communication and CSR communication initiatives PR communication and communication of CSR initiatives are classified as having relatively low entertainment value, high trustworthiness, and triggering little annoyance (e.g., Kotler & Keller 2015; Sen & Bhattacharya 2001), which may mitigate the negative brand strength effect. Furthermore, PR communication is perceived as very information-rich (e.g., Kotler & Keller 2015). Given that layoff announcements may create consumer uncertainty and signal a firm in decline (Landsman & Stremersch 2020), PR communication may help give consumers a sense that the brand is still able to maintain its purchase risk reduction and information function (e.g., Fischer et al. 2010).

The information value of the communication of CSR initiatives is rather ambiguous. On the one hand, this form of communication does not provide information regarding product/service quality that may help the consumer overcome uncertainties created by layoff announcements. On the other hand, consumers learn about social initiatives the company is supporting and might share such content with others (Berger & Milkman 2012). Support of CSR initiatives may also reassure consumers that the company will survive, as such support suggests that the firm has financial resources to support CSR initiatives (Sen & Bhattacharya 2001). Due to these latter points, we assume a relatively high information value (which is also supported by our exploratory survey; see Panel E in Table 3), which in turn may lead to a mitigating effect of the negative relationship between layoff announcements and brand strength.

Regarding perceived costs, PR communication is seen to be a relatively low cost communication channel, which in turn aligns with cost-cutting measures as opposed to CSR initiatives, which are perceived to be costly (e.g., Kotler & Keller 2015).

Finally, regarding the attribute beneficial to other stakeholders, CSR initiatives support good causes that often yield direct benefits for other external firm stakeholders. As a result, CSR initiatives have a positive effect on consumers’ evaluations of a firm (e.g., Brown & Dacin 1997; Klein & Dawar 2004; Sen & Bhattacharya 2001). Since CSR is directly beneficial to other stakeholders, balance theory suggests that consumers may be willing to forgive a company for an occasional, possibly inadvertent lapse (Bhattachary & Sen 2003). In contrast to the communication of CSR initiatives, PR communication does not provide any direct benefits to other stakeholders.

In sum, we hypothesize the following (see Table 4 again): H4: PR communication mitigates the negative effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength. H5: Communication of CSR initiatives mitigates the negative effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength.

3.5. Control variables

We consider where the announced layoff takes place (domestic vs. foreign layoff), the extent to which layoffs have happened before (layoff history), and layoff size (e.g., Habel & Klarmann 2015). Media coverage is a further control variable that can be a very important accelerator of a brand/firm crisis (e.g., Stäbler & Fischer 2020). In addition, stakeholders may react differently to what motivated the firm’s layoff announcement (e.g., Landsman & Stremersch 2020): Jobs may be cut completely or retained by outsourcing abroad (relocation motive), or they may be cut to enhance future organizational performance (proactive motive) or to address poor financial performance (reactive motive). Finally, as layoff decisions involve various stakeholders (e.g., top management, employees, work councils), information leakage may occur. Thus, we also control whether the layoff announcement is a rumor or a fact confirmed by the firm.

Besides considering layoff-related controls, we also capture brand-related controls. We control for brand strength prior to the layoff announcement. A strong brand serves as a protective shield against negative publicity (e.g., Ahluwalia et al. 2000). To the extent that brand strength is a sticky asset accumulated through a history of actions, prior brand strength may protect the company’s image against the negative effect of a single strategic decision such as a layoff (Love & Kraatz 2009). The second brand-related control variable refers to the industry (i.e., nondurables, durables, retailers, services) where the layoff takes place (Datta et al. 2010). As a third brand-related control, we consider whether the layoff is announced by a domestic or foreign brand (e.g., Landsman & Stremersch 2020). Consumers may assess the unfair behavior of domestic brands (i.e., in-group members) differently than the unfair behavior of foreign brands (i.e., out-group members).

Finally, contextual controls include positive (e.g., sales increases), negative (e.g., sales decreases), and neutral (e.g., merger and acquisition announcement) financial confounding events and the year of the announcement. Companies regularly report financial information, which is then picked up by public media and which may influence how consumers assess the reasons for the layoff. The year generally controls for the surrounding economic climate. For example, consumers may be less surprised that firms must lay off workers during economic downturns (e.g., Flannagan & O’Shaugnessy 2005). Web Appendix B provides a more detailed description of the control variables and the assumptions regarding the direction of their moderating impact.

4. Pre-studies: Showing the effect of layoff announcements on brand awareness

Before empirically investigating how layoff announcements affect brand strength, we need sufficient evidence that consumers become aware of layoff announcements. While we do not formulate a hypothesis for the effect of layoff announcements on consumers’ level of awareness, we consider an awareness effect to be a prerequisite to investigate the impact of a layoff announcement on brand strength (Keller 1993). Pre-studies 1, 2, 3, and 4, which are summarized in Table 5, demonstrate the awareness effect of layoff announcements (more detailed information is provided in Web Appendix C and D). Prestudies 1 and 2 apply primary data approaches to support general and event-specific awareness effects of layoff announcements. Pre-study 3 applies a textual analysis of user comments on Twitter and Facebook for layoff announcements that took place in Germany in 2020 and 2021. Finally, Pre-study 4 uses the same dataset as in the main study, i.e., 366 layoff events between 2008 and 2012, and investigates their effects on the variable brand communication awareness provided by the market research company YouGov. In summary, these triangulation tests reinforce our assumption that consumers are aware of layoff announcements beyond the general knowledge that the respective firms are not doing well.

5. Main study: Design and data

5.1. Research design

We use an econometric approach to estimate the impact of layoff announcements on brand strength over time. Brand strength data are provided by the market research company YouGov (see Web Appendix E for details). To identify layoff announcement events, we performed an extensive public news media analysis. Our observation period (2008–2012) covers historical data for 366 layoff announcements by 82 corporate brands in the retail, services, durable, and nondurable goods industries.

To include moderator and control variables, we obtained data from Ebiquity, YouGov, Compustat, LexisNexis, Yahoo Finance, and Twitter and manually collected data from several other secondary data sources, such as company annual reports and websites.

6. Main study: Methodology

6.1. Model idea

We use an econometric approach and estimate an error correction model (ECM) to investigate how layoff announcements affect brand strength over time. The ECM is widely used in marketing research (e.g., Datta et al. 2022; Van Heerde et al. 2013). Its former application to the effectiveness of marketing instruments in the context of product-harm crises (Van Heerde et al. 2007)—a context similar to ours—further supports the selection of this econometric model.

Fig. 2 illustrates an example of a time series within our dataset that shows the brand strength levels of a German energy provider and its development in the 20 weeks before and after a layoff announcement. The ECM enables us to precisely estimate the immediate effect (A), the duration of the effect (B), and the cumulative effect over time (C) of the layoff event. The cumulative effect combines the immediate effect and the effect over time (as illustrated in orange in Fig. 2). To test our hypotheses, we focus on the cumulative effect over time (C). In addition, we also report the estimates of (A) and (B). ECMs allow us to estimate the duration of the cumulative effect from the model itself. It describes the time until brand strength returns to a (new) equilibrium. Therefore, we do not need to define a predetermined period for the cumulative effect. In the next section, we specify the ECM. We provide the formal derivation and more details about the ECM in Web Appendix K.

Additional moderation analyses to investigate the timing of communication variables As prior literature indicates that the effects of pre-crisis communication may differ from post-crisis communication (e.g., Cleeren et al. 2013), we pursue an additional exploratory empirical approach (Golder et al. 2022) and consider the timing of firm communication elements. This might provide an additional understanding of the timing effect and allows to elaborate on communication strategy implications. Accordingly, we conduct an additional analysis where we separate the forms of firm communication in a pre- (12-week prior), during (in the event week), and post-announcement (12-week post) period.

6.4. Accounting for endogeneity

As this study relies on observational data, we may face endogeneity issues. In our first-stage analysis, one of the explanatory variables is the industry’s brand strength. However, the layoff announcement itself could also influence the industry’s brand strength. To reduce the risk of biased estimates, we apply an instrumental variables approach and use predicted industry brand strength values in Eq. (1). We follow prior ECM studies (e.g., Van Heerde et al. 2007) and use both the firstdifferenced and lagged values of the average brand strength values in other industries to identify the first-differenced endogenous variables. These predicted brand strength values of competitors serve as ‘‘quasi” controls in Eq. (1) (see Section 6.2).

In addition, endogeneity issues may affect the variables in our second-stage analysis. For example, firms may change their advertising communication intensity strategically in anticipation of changes in economic performance (Rubel et al. 2011). To minimize potential endogeneity issues that may arise from advertising communication, we follow extant literature (e.g., Frennea et al. 2019) and measure advertising communication relative to the industry. The share of spending is set exogenously and not in anticipation of brand performance outcomes.

In a similar vein, social media communication, PR communication, and communication of CSR initiatives might be prone to endogeneity issues, as they can be initiated strategically. For these variables, we use a two-stage control function approach (Wooldridge 2015). In the first stage, we predict social media communication, PR communication, and communication of CSR initiatives based on instrumental variables. Specifically, to predict social media and PR communication, we follow Stäbler and Fischer (2020) and use temporal patterns such as periods of winter and summer sales, the Christmas period, global sporting events, and the largest international trade fairs because they may generate news about promotional activities, sponsorships, and new product introductions. We show that these temporal patterns serve as exogenous predictors for social media and PR communication. To predict communication of CSR initiatives, we use the number of CSR initiatives from the previous year—an approach similar to the one that Dinner et al. (2019) use to predict marketing crises. Again, the instrument serves as a strong predictor. We then compute the residuals from our first-stage models and add them to our focal models. We do not find support for endogeneity concerns. We report these estimations in full detail in Web Appendix M, including descriptions of the selection and validity of the instruments.

7.4. Robustness checks

We conducted several robustness checks. First, we tested alternative model specifications of our focal second-stage analysis. For example, instead of brand-specific random effects, we included brand-specific fixed effects. The results do not lead to any other conclusions. Second, we ran a wide range of models varying the sample composition. First, we included only the strongest 50% of corporate brands (based on brand strength). The intuition is that newspapers are more likely to cover strong brands (Stäbler & Fischer 2020). Second, we ran the analyses for a sample of layoff events for which no financial news was communicated at the same time (46% of the sample). Third, we ran the analyses for subsamples and remove layoff events for which we had, for example, missing layoff size information (10% of the sample). While all of these efforts reduced the sample size considerably, the results are still robust and lead to similar conclusions. None of the coefficients switched signs, and most are still significant (see Web Appendix P).

7.5. Practical effect sizes

Fig. 4 presents an effect strength analysis in which the brand strength effects of the focal types of firm communication are simulated. In the base scenario, derived from the mean coefficient in the first stage of the ECM, cumulative brand strength decreases by 18%. To put this percentage value into perspective, we refer to the average, generalizable firm-value elasticity of 0.33, identified for brand-related assets in the broad meta-analysis by Edeling and Fischer (2016). An average announcement would be associated with a firm-value decrease of 18% x 0.33 = 5.94%. Taking the mean total market capitalization of the S&P 500 as of March 31, 2023, i.e., $71.823 billion (S&P Global 2023), we arrive at a value loss of $4.266 billion. The bottom line is that layoff announcements can lead to substantial economic losses incurred through brand strength transgressions.

Fig. 4 shows how firms can apply the different forms of firm communication to weaken these brand strength deteriorations. Our findings indicate that managers should be more reserved with respect to advertising communication intensity (increase from 18% to 24%). During layoffs, they should make use of communicating CSR initiatives (reduction from 18% to 11%) and PR communication such as new product introductions (reduction from 18% to 10%). Finally, the use of social media communication via brand-initiated tweets leads to an increase of brand strength loss (increase from 18% to 28%).

8. Discussion

8.1. Research implications

Layoffs are a highly relevant and frequently applied strategic management instrument. Layoff decisions are regularly triggered by stagnating market demand or industry-wide economic effects and aim to improve future firm performance. However, researchers have only recently started to examine the impact of layoffs on consumer perceptions (e.g., Landsman & Stremersch 2020). Our findings regarding the main effect of layoff announcements on cumulative brand strength indicate that consumers care about layoffs, even though they are not directly affected by these decisions. Downsizing has no effect on customer satisfaction if consumers rely on brands as the primary cue in purchasing decisions (Habel & Klarmann 2015). However, we demonstrate that although downsizing may have no impact on customer satisfaction, it does affect brand perception. Thus, our research provides a new link between a strategic management decision (i.e., layoffs) and a core marketing metric—brand strength—that is among a firm’s most important intangible assets.

Our findings contribute to the research aiming to identify the hidden costs of layoffs (Williams et al. 2011), which represent a loss in brand equity that was built by marketing investments. The negative impact on brand strength seems to be a cost of downsizing that researchers and managers have overlooked and that has negative consequences for future revenues (Landsman & Stremersch 2020). Hence, our results may explain why layoffs often do not lead to the expected results.

Our study is the first to investigate the moderating impact of different types of firm communication on brand strength surrounding layoff announcements. We derive several theoretical implications from these results. First, we differentiate different types of firm communication according to a wide range of attributes. We highlight the importance of connecting these attributes to the situation a company is facing. Attributes that are considered positive under ‘‘normal” circumstances can have negative effects in the context of corporate decisions that are legally acceptable but ethically questionable. For example, in contrast to previous literature emphasizing the positive brand effects of being entertaining (e.g., Rosengren et al. 2020), we show that being entertaining—an attribute closely related to advertising communication intensity—increases the loss in brand strength in the context of layoff announcements.

Second, our results add to the findings of Landsman and Stremersch (2020). They show that after layoffs, firms experience adverse changes in sales. They ask for further research that analyzes consumers’ responses to firms’ marketing mix decisions in the context of layoff announcements. We derive a negative layoff announcement–brand strength relationship that may partially explain their derived adverse changes in sales. Landsman and Stremersch (2020) further show that advertising elasticity with respect to sales decreases after layoffs. We find that advertising communication intensity even harms brand strength in the wake of layoff announcements. Thus, our conclusion with respect to advertising communication intensity differs from theirs: Whereas they suggest increasing advertising investments after the announcement ‘‘as long as advertising elasticity remains positive” (p. 138), we suggest marketers concerned about the firm’s brand strength should shift budgets away from advertising communication to types of firm communication that are perceived as less costly and more trustworthy (e.g., PR communication).

Third, consumers appear to appreciate firms’ maintenance of their investments in another important stakeholder group, society in general, despite the economic pressure that led to the layoff decision. This positive moderating effect of communication of CSR initiatives is consistent with earlier work finding that consumers attribute less blame to firms with strong CSR initiatives that suffer from a product-harm crisis (Klein & Dawar 2004). Importantly, the effect is also congruent with prior findings that only CSR initiatives in domains other than initial corporate social irresponsibility (to which ‘‘workforce reductions” belong) have a firm value-enhancing effect (Lenz et al. 2017).

Fourth, our work shows that pre-layoff brand strength serves to buffer the impact of negative consumer perceptions. This aligns with other studies by brand academics that have shown that brand strength protects a firm from the adverse effects of crises triggered by product safety incidents (e.g., Ahluwalia et al. 2000). Thus, our results support the idea that brand equity is a rather ‘‘sticky” construct (Hanssens et al. 2014) that, after being built up over time, does not depreciate quickly.

Finally, our study extends earlier research methodologically, as we are the first to estimate the cumulative effects of layoff announcements. A comparison of the brand strength recovery time from the announcement with other types of brand crises (product harm and corporate social irresponsibility) shows that the maximum (48 weeks vs. 9.5 months) and mean durations (7 weeks vs. 2.6 months) are comparable (Backhaus & Fischer 2016), further demonstrating the relevance of layoff announcements for marketing research.

8.2. Managerial implications

8.2.1. Actionable implications for firm communication surrounding layoff announcements Our results offer managers practical guidance for how to better align their decisions regarding firm communication in the context of layoff announcements.

With respect to advertising communication intensity and social media communication via brand-initiated tweets, companies should be more restrained. Prior research has shown (Hansen et al. 2018) that crisis events may lead to negative online firestorms that amplify the negative consequences for brand perception. Firms could strategically lower the amount of social media communication via brand-initiated tweets before, during, and after layoff announcements to limit the chance that consumers use these tweets as triggers to complain about these workforce reductions.

Similarly, as advertising communication intensity hurts brand strength in the wake of layoff announcements, firms should avoid extensive investment in advertising in this context. Consumers might perceive advertising communication as entertainment-related and cost-generating, which could be evaluated as inappropriate in the context of layoff announcements. While we cannot make specific recommendations on advertising communication content, the attributes (e.g., entertainment value) in our theoretical framework suggest that post-layoff companies should change the content of their

S. Stäbler, A. Himme, A. Edeling et al. International Journal of Research in Marketing 40 (2023) 700–723

advertising communication and move away from entertainment-related to more information-related content. However, while advertising communication intensity hurts before and during the layoff announcement, we do not find an effect of advertising communication intensity after the event. In fact, post-crisis advertising communication may help in the long run in the context of product-harm crises (Cleeren et al. 2013). We show that increasing advertising communication intensity after the layoff announcement at least does not hurt and may help rebuild the brand in the long run.

Should firms be more reserved regarding other forms of firm communication? No—with respect to PR communication and the communication of CSR initiatives, companies should stay active. When confronted with layoff scenarios, companies should consider the strategic launch of other neutral or positive brand news, such as communication of CSR initiatives or PR communication that relates to the marketing mix. These types of firm communication seem to be appropriate measures to restore trust. This recommendation is supported by Stäbler and Fischer (2020), who argue that firms may diminish the negative consequences of crisis events caused by ethical misbehavior if they launch brand-related news.

The effects of the control variables also provide actionable implications. Firms are well advised to invest in their brands continuously, as a strong brand strength acts as a buffer against negative consumer brand perceptions after layoff announcements. Media coverage is the most powerful lever in terms of effect strength (increasing media coverage by one S.D. leads to a 34% brand strength loss). Stäbler and Fischer (2020) provide managers with guidelines for limiting media coverage in the case of CSI events, many of which might be transferrable to the layoff context.

8.2.2. Implications regarding marketing’s seat at the executive table Our results also underscore the need for marketing executives to have a seat on executive boards. While marketing mix activities seem unrelated to layoff decisions, our study demonstrates that consumers connect layoff announcements with advertising communication intensity, social media communication, PR communication, and the communication of CSR initiatives. Marketing managers need to create awareness that seemingly unrelated company decisions such as layoffs are also relevant for communication that is initiated by marketing. Consequently, layoff events need to be better planned strategically. For example, top executive managers could better align their decisions internally across different functional departments (e.g., executive board, strategy, finance, marketing, PR). This alignment is especially important in an increasingly complex world in which the interests of several stakeholder groups must be given equal consideration. The Business Roundtable (2019) decision to move away from the idea of pure shareholder value is emblematic of this evolution.

8.2.3. Implications for internal brand perception monitoring Our results underscore the usefulness of disaggregated consumer mindset metrics for precisely attributing the effect of nonmarketing-related firm actions on consumer perceptions. Market research companies such as YouGov or Morning Consult have recently begun to collect, track, and process such data frequently (e.g., daily). They overcome the main limitation of more aggregated consumer mindset metrics from providers such as ACSI or Young & Rubicam (yearly collection), where an unequivocal brand-perception attribution of events such as layoff announcements is not possible. Managers should be encouraged to use these new data, as this information can serve as a useful early warning for changes in the brand and the financial performance of firms.

8.3. Limitations and avenues for further research

Our study has some limitations. First, our sample covers firms that are contained in the YouGov database, subjecting this study to the inclusion requirements of the YouGov database. Nevertheless, our robustness checks indicate that our results hold if we vary the sample composition.

Second, it could be worthwhile to intensify the analysis of the effect of layoff announcements on online word of mouth. Our preliminary analysis of 2020 layoff announcements (Pre-study 3, Section 4) shows that in recent times, Twitter and Facebook users tend to pick up such news. Future research could track the volume and valence of layoff-related online word of mouth, as in work on product recalls (Borah & Tellis 2016). Alternatively, studies could apply the novel social media-based brand reputation tracker (Rust et al. 2021) to see whether our findings, which rely on a rather traditional survey approach, hold for unstructured textual data.

Finally, we highlighted that layoffs can be regarded as a strategic, non-marketing-related firm decision, similar to tax evasion and avoidance, corporate sociopolitical activism, and lobbying. Future research could integrate all these events and investigate which of them affect brand strength the most and how the impact of different types of firm communication differs across them.
