Financialization and corporate downsizing as a shareholder value strategy

Jiwook Jung * and Yin Lee

School of Labor and Employment Relations, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Champaign, IL, USA

*Correspondence: jwjung@illinois.edu

Abstract

How has financialization contributed to the changing nature of employment relations? Using the case of downsizing, we provide a cultural explanation of how the growing influence of the financial sector has reshaped employment relations at nonfinancial firms. We focus on the role of financial professionals in promoting the norm of shareholder value maximization around which financial market expecatations are formed and imposed upon firms. Our analysis of downsizing announcements made by the largest US firms shows that firms downsize when they miss earnings forecasts of securities analysts—a clear indication that firms fail to maximize shareholder value. We also show that institutional investors and financial executives promoted downsizing after missed analyst forecasts while labor unions resisted it. Despite the latter group’s resistance, in the context of the declining countervailing influence of labor, our findings suggest that downsizing has become institutionalized as a shareholder value strategy in the era of financialization.

Key words: financialization, employment relations, shareholder value, financial professionals, downsizing

JEL classification: J53, J63

1. Introduction

The changing nature of employment relations and its consequences have recently received keen attention from scholars from various disciplinary backgrounds. Beginning in the late 1970s, a set of rules, norms and practices that governed employment relations at major US firms began to disintegrate (Appelbaum and Batt, 1994; Kochan et al., 1994; Cappelli, 1999; Osterman, 1999). In response, firms moved away from their reliance on internal labor markets toward more reliance on external ones (Bidwell, 2013; Cobb, 2015). Previous studies have provided a variety of explanations for the shift toward employment relations based on external market mechanisms, including deregulation, technological change, globalization and foreign competition (Harrison and Bluestone, 1988; Baumol et al., 2003; Autor et al., 2008).

An emerging stream of research adds another important factor to the list—financialization, broadly defined as ‘the increasingly important role of financial markets, motives, actors and institutions in the operation of the economy’ (Fligstein and Goldstein, 2015, p. 575). Existing studies in the literature have offered two core mechanisms through which financialization can reshape employment relations. At the sectoral level, financial firms have gained economic power, leading to rent extraction from the economy as a whole. In turn, this has allowed financial firms and their employees to enjoy oversized rewards while reducing the share for non-financial firms and aggravating their labor relations (Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin, 2011; Godechot, 2012; Philippon and Reshef, 2012; Lin and Tomaskovic-Devey, 2013). At the firm level, non-financial firms have increasingly channeled resources into financial activities, rather than productive activities, to achieve higher rates of return for investors (Krippner, 2005; Orhangazi, 2008; Davis and Kim, 2015; Tori and Onaran, 2020). As firms have become more dependent on earnings generated from financial activities, they undermine labor’s bargaining power and constrain employment and wage growth (Alvarez, 2015; Tomaskovic-Devey et al., 2015; Lin, 2016). Altogether, these sectoral- and firm-level mechanisms offer a compelling explanation for how financialization has led to reduced job growth and unstable employment relations in the non-financial sector.

Nevertheless, one intriguing issue is the relationship between the sectoral- and firm-level mechanisms of financialization. Most prior studies treat one or the other mechanism separately without considering how sector-level processes reinforce firm-level outcomes or the other way around. In this article, we provide a cultural explanation that connects the dominance of the financial sector and changing employment relations at non-financial firms, focusing on the role of financial professionals in promoting the norm of shareholder value maximization around which financial market expecatations are formed and imposed upon firms. Studies on shareholder value suggest that the new norm of corporate governance has significantly undercut the bargaining power of workers as firms try to cut labor costs for the sake of shareholder value maximization (Fligstein and Shin, 2007; Jung, 2015). But how much profit should firms generate to meet the goal and, more importantly who decides the goal? In this respect, we highlight the role of securities analysts as a prominent group of financial professionals in the contemporary financial markets. We examine the impact of the firmly entrenched expectation in the US financial market that firms should meet quarterly earnings forecasts by securities analysts as a de facto indicator of shareholder value maximization (Dobbin and Zorn, 2005). We argue that downsizing has become an institutionalized practice to reassure the financial community when firms fail to satisfy the market expectations set by analyst forecasts.

Furthermore, we explore how other financial professionals have facilitated the process and whether workers have mounted any meaningful resistance. Scholars of financialization suggest that it is a fundamentally political project with the dominating role of finance within the economy and the concentration of wealth in the hands of financial interest groups (Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin, 2011; Godechot, 2012; Roberts and Kwon, 2017). Using data on downsizing announcements made by the largest US firms from 1984 to 2006, our analysis shows that the rate of downsizing significantly increases soon after firms fail to meet or beat analyst forecasts. These results hold even after we control for various firm-level measures of financialization, suggesting that employment decisions by firms are influenced not just by firm-level financialization but also by extra-firm influences stemming from financial markets. By elaborating how financialization affects firm-level decisions on employment practices, our study also demonstrates the merit of combining insights from the financialization and shareholder value literatures. Such an approach, we believe, will extend our understanding of how the broad economy-wide turn to financialization reshapes firm-level distribution of power and resources.

2. Financialization and changing employment relations

Over the past decades, employment relations at US firms have undergone significant changes (Osterman, 1999). Among many possible causes of the changes, financialization has received growing attention. Financialization is often defined as ‘the pattern of accumulation in which profits accrue primarily through financial channels rather than through trade and economy’ (Krippner, 2005, p. 174). It is manifested into two interrelated, multilevel processes. The first process, which occurs at the sectoral level, is the increased importance of finance in the economy (Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin, 2011). It is well documented that wealth accumulation has been disproportionately concentrated in the financial sector since the late 1970s as a result of deregulation and global competition (Van der Zwan, 2014; Lin, 2016). For instance, Krippner (2005) and Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin (2011) show that the proportion of financial sector profits out of total profits in the US economy has steadily increased since the 1980s, and was further accelerated by a dramatic uptake in the 2000s. Kaplan and Rauh (2010) also show that Wall Street’s (e.g. investment bankers and hedgefund investors) representation at the top end of the income distrubution surpassed that of the Main Street (e.g. non-financial firm top executives).

The second process, which occurs at the firm level, is the increased importance of financial activities in non-financial firms’ bottom line (Krippner, 2005; Van der Zwan, 2014). In those firms, primary profit-generating activities have shifted from investing in production and service activities to engaging in financial activities (Lin, 2016; Tori and Onaran, 2020). For instance, Orhangazi (2008) shows that there is a negative relationship between real investment and financialization in non-financial firms. This suggests that the increase in financial activities can disincentivize managers from engaging in real investments that might yield

These two interrelated processes of financialization can have a significant impact on employment relations. By diverting resources toward financial activities, financialization can reduce the resources that can be used for productive activities and additional employment. Using industry-level data from the USA, Lin and Tomaskovic-Devey (2013) show that increased reliance on financial income in the non-financial sector was associated with a decrease in workers’ share of income in addition to rising income inequality between executives and non-executive workers and among workers themselves. Using country-level data for 21 Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OCED) member countries, Meyer (2019) also shows that equity market development weakens the institutional foundations of labor unions’ bargaining power, such as wage bargaining coordination and centralization. Based on firm-level data on financialization and employment, Lin (2016) suggests that the increased financial activities at non-financial firms can lead to employment stagnation. His analysis shows that firms that make more financial investments, rely more on debt and provide shareholders with more rewards through stock repurchases and dividends have lower levels of employment growth.

To sum up, the existing literature on financialization convincingly demonstrates the sectoral-level shift in the distribution of economic resources and the corresponding redistribution of resources at the firm level. One issue that requires more research is the mechanisms that link the two processes at sectoral and firm levels. For instance, why do non-financial firms acquiesce to the increasingly commanding role of the financial sector? What are the mechanisms through which the financial sector binds non-financial firms to act in its interests? Relatedly, how does the growing influence of finance reshape firm-level configurations of competing stakeholder groups—such as managers, shareholders and workers—and their ability to promote or resist firm-level financialization?

Building on insights from the shareholder value literature, we examine how the norm of shareholder value maximization leverages the finance sector’s influence over non-financial firms and facilitates the diversion of resources away from productive activities, and how several groups of financial professionals lead the charge. It is important to elaborate such mechanisms that link the rise of the shareholder value model of the firm and changing employment relations, because despite the consensus on the normative grip of the new governance model, the debate continues about whether the model has fundamentally reshaped corporate decisions and practices (Shin, 2013). Firms may merely symbolically embrace the model, adopting a set of shareholder value practices without implementing them (Westphal and Zajac, 2001; Zajac and Westphal, 2004). Managers may even resist the model and related practices and even use them to advance their interests, not those of shareholders (Goldstein, 2012; Benton 2016; Pernell et al., 2017). In the following, we offer a mechanism of financial market control through the role of securities analysts.

3. Securities analysts and the institutionalization of downsizing as a means to manage market expectations

The central tenet of the shareholder value model of the firm is that the sole goal of corporations is to maximize returns to shareholders (Fligstein and Shin, 2007). But how do firms

know whether or not they manage to maximize shareholders’ wealth? Enter securities analysts. Through their quarterly earnings forecasts for firms (analyst forecasts, henceforth), they not only channel but also actively shape financial market expectations for firms against which their commitment to shareholder value maximization is assessed (Dobbin and Zorn, 2005). The original purpose of these forecasts was to assist investors in making informed investment decisions, but analyst forecasts soon became the de facto performance target that could not be missed (Collingwood, 2001). The prominence of analyst forecasts sharply divides acceptable from unacceptable performance. Much financial and accounting research has demonstrated that stock prices now rise and fall on the strength of profits vis-a`-vis analyst forecasts (Bartov et al., 2002). The magnitude of market reactions, which includes rewards for meeting analyst forecasts and penalties for failure, has increased over time (Lopez and Rees, 2002). The possibility of harsh penalties for missing analyst forecasts induces firms to proactively manage their earnings to avoid earnings surprises. It is well known that managers engage in various earnings management practices for that purpose (Degeorge et al., 1999; Bergstresser and Philippon, 2006; Zhang et al., 2008). Managers can also influence analyst forecasts, by issuing their own forecast guidance and making conference calls with analysts (Washburn and Bromiley, 2014).

Such strategic engagement by firms, however, may not diminish the importance of meeting analyst forecasts. It can reinforce the institutional standing of the practice (Mazmanian and Beckman, 2018). Sauder and Espeland (2009, p. 76) explain this ironic outcome using the concept of gaming, defined as ‘cynical efforts to manipulate the rankings data without addressing the underlying condition that is the target of measurement’. They argue that although gaming can challenge the legitimacy of rankings through cynicism, distancing and localized resistance, it helps solidify their legitimacy by increasing the amount of effort and investment that organizations put into to enhance their rankings. Similarly, firms’ active involvement in the construction of their own earnings forecasts can make it even more important for them not to miss analyst forecasts.

The institutionalized expectation that firms should meet or beat quarterly earnings projections of securities analysts pushes firms to adopt drastic reforms when they fail so as to regain investor confidence (Puffer and Weintrop, 1991; Zuckerman, 2000; Zhang and Gimeno, 2010; Benner and Ranganathan, 2012). For instance, in a series of studies, Zuckerman (1999, 2000) illustrates how securities analysts advanced the norm against excessive corporate diversification and penalized firms that did not conform. In the 1980s, diversification was identified as the main example of managerial opportunism that would reduce shareholder wealth (Amihud and Lev, 1981; Jensen, 1986). This view was against the financial orthodoxy that insisted that investors, not firms, should diversify. Moreover, diversified firms performed poorly compared with focused firms (Berger and Ofek, 1995). Zuckerman (1999, 2000) shows that diversified firms suffered from low valuation because, compared with focused firms, securities analysts who specialized by industry paid them less attention, and therefore, those firms faced pressure to de-diversify and focus on their core competencies.

We suggest that when firms fail to meet analyst forecasts, they will announce downsizing plans to signal their willingness to compensate for their failure and to reassure analysts and investors. Firms increasingly engaged in downsizing in the 1980s and 1990s under pressure to maximize shareholder value (Fligstein and Shin, 2007; Lazonick and O’Sullivan, 2000; Jung, 2015). Previously, firms temporarily laid off workers during business downturns with a promise of recall when economic conditions improved; since the 1980s, layoffs predominantly meant permanent termination of employment ties (Osterman, 1999). Firms dismissed large numbers of employees even when times were good (Cappelli, 1999; Osterman, 1999). Fortune 500 companies such as Kodak and Xerox made massive layoffs even as they reported record profits in the 1990s. One possible reason is that even seemingly well-performing firms may not live up to the financial market expectations indicated by securities analysts’ earnings forecasts.

In fact, when announcing downsizing plans, firms often describe their performance vis-a`- vis analyst forecasts, sometimes even explicitly mentioning that the move is intended to compensate for their failure to meet forecasts. In May 2006, when announcing a plan to cut 4000–5000 jobs, Jonathan Schwartz, the newly appointed CEO of Sun Microsystems, noted that the expected cost savings from the job cuts would help the company meet analyst forecasts of a loss of 2 cents a share (Flynn, 2006). In other cases, even good performers that barely missed analyst forecasts tried to appease the market by initiating downsizing. In July 2005, Abbott Laboratories, a healthcare company, posted a 38% gain in second-quarter profits compared with the same quarter the previous year but predicted that earnings in the next quarter would fall short of analyst forecasts. Its stock price fell immediately, and, in a bid to quell the market, the company announced a plan to cut 700 jobs (Colias, 2005).

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Missing quarterly earnings forecasts by securities analysts will increase the likelihood that firms will make downsizing announcements in the next fiscal quarter.

4. The role of other financial professionals and the countervailing influence of labor

The increased influence of finance over non-financial firms is also likely to reshape power relations within those firms over the distribution of resources (Tomaskovic-Devey and Avent- Holt, 2019). We suggest that the changed configurations of group interest and power have also contributed to the institutionalization of downsizing as a shareholder value strategy. On the one hand, we examine the role of other ascending groups of financial professionals inside and outside the firm, such as institutional investors in capital markets and financial executives within the top management team. Their influence is likely to push firms to engage in downsizing when they fail to keep up with financial market expectations. On the other hand, we examine the countervailing influence of workers and their unions. From the perspective of workers, downsizing can be seen as a tool of rent transfer from workers to shareholders and top managers (Sørensen, 2000; Dencker, 2012). Hence, the collective voice of workers through labor unions will discourage firms from engaging in downsizing even after missing analyst forecasts. While all of these competing groups may attempt to influence corporate downsizing decisions to their advantage, we argue that the declining political power of workers allows firms to engage in downsizing to satisfy the demand of financial community.

As they are paid based on the performance of the funds they manage, it is in their interest to maximize the share prices of firms in their portfolios. Hence, their presence is likely to increase the need for compensatory action when a firm misses the performance target set by securities analysts—a clear indication that the firm has failed to maximize shareholder value. Institutional investors include several different types and have different investment strategies depending on the size of their holdings and investment time horizons (Bushee, 1998). Accordingly, their tactics to pressure management also differ. For instance, institutions with large holdings tend to have a long-term time horizon, and therefore they likely turn to ‘voice’ strategies, directly challenging management through anti-management resolutions and private negotiation (Carleton et al., 1998; Gillan and Starks, 2000). In contrast, institutions with small holdings tend to have a short-term time horizon, and therefore they prefer ‘exit’ strategies by selling under-performing stocks (Parrino et al., 2003).

Either way, their presence will put firms under significant pressure when they miss analyst forecasts. Stock price declines after earnings surprises can increase the risk that the firm will become a target of shareholder activism in the future (Del Guercio and Hawkins, 1999). Also, sell-offs by mobile investors can further depress the stock price of the firm. We expect that in order to avoid either direct challenge from activist institutional investors or massive sell-offs by passive ones, a firm is more likely to engage in downsizing after missing analyst forecasts when a sizable portion of its stock is owned by institutional investors.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Institutional ownership will increase the likelihood of downsizing announcements after missed analyst forecasts.

4.2 Internal promotion by CFOs While the influence of institutional investors and securities analysts can increase external pressure upon firms that miss analyst forecasts, the presence of internal actors who embrace their concerns and preferences can enhance the receptivity of firms to such pressure. Financial experts within the top management team can play such a role. In this respect, we emphasize the role of CFOs. Unlike treasurers or controllers whose tasks are mostly confined to bookkeeping, they are actively involved in handling investor relations and communicating with analysts, by issuing regular reports, holding conference calls and issuing earnings preannouncements (Zorn, 2004). Meeting future earnings projections of securities analysts are crucial for CFOs to maintain their credibility with investors and analysts and sustain the firm’s stock market performance (Graham et al., 2005). Also, as a member of the top management team, CFOs have a critical say in corporate decision-making including downsizing decisions (Jung, 2015). Therefore, we expect that firms with a CFO are more likely to initiate downsizing after they miss analyst forecasts to avoid the same failure in the future.

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The presence of a CFO will increase the likelihood of downsizing announcements after firms miss analyst forecasts.

5. Data and methods

5.1 Sample We tested our hypothesis using a sample of the largest, non-financial US firms from the third quarter of 1984 and the fourth quarter of 2006. Our primary sampling framing was the list of Fortune 500 companies, a list of large, publicly traded companies in the USA.1 We focused on the largest firms as prior studies suggest that the turn to financialization among non-financial firms has been most salient among the largest ones (Lin, 2016; Davis, 2018). In addition, these firms used to offer stable jobs with generous benefits (Osterman, 1999; Cobb, 2015). By focusing on them, we can explore how financialization has led to unstable employment relations. We tried to sample a roughly equal number of firms from each of the 23 industries represented in the Fortune list, except for finance.2 We also tried to sample about the same number of firms from each year’s list to ensure that our sample would include firms in both rising and declining industries. Combining these two approaches, in most cases, we randomly drew one company name from each industry for each year of our study period. For declining industries (e.g. textile), we had few new firms to add in the later period, whereas in the case of emerging industries (e.g. information technology), we had few firms earlier. In these cases, we left the cells blank. After eliminating duplicated cases and those with missing values, we had a sample of 647 firms. We collected data on the sampled firms for the entire observation period using various sources that we will explain. The number of firms actually used for analysis is 460 because our event history analysis with firm fixed effects excludes firms that never experienced downsizing events during our observation window. We explain this issue when we discuss our statistical methods.

5.3 Statistical methods We analyzed the timing of downsizing announcements between the third fiscal quarter of 1984 and the fourth fiscal quarter of 2006 using event history analysis. Event spells are defined as time intervals (i.e. the number of days) between announcements (Box-Steffensmeier and Jones, 2004). Accordingly, we reset the duration time to zero every time an announcement was made. There are 5265 event spells, and 605 of them (about 12%) are right censored. Also, the first spell is left-truncated for most firms. Left truncation is not a serious concern as long as one knows the exact starting point for the left-truncated spells—the date of the prior announcement in our case (Singer and Willett, 2003). For each firm with a lefttruncated spell, we traced their downsizing history beyond the onset of our observation period until January 1, 1980; before then, media coverage of downsizing announcements was sparse. For 391 firms, the beginning of their first event spell was therefore set to January 1, 1980.7

We used a Cox regression model to estimate the rate of downsizing announcements. It is a semi-parametric model; unlike other parametric models that assume functional forms of the time dependency of the baseline rate of event occurrence, the Cox model lets the data to determine the shape of the baseline rate (Allison, 1995). This feature of the model is advantageous for our purpose because without knowing the baseline rate of downsizing announcements, any choice of the functional form for the baseline rate function can be arbitrary. We used the Efron method for tied events.

7. Conclusion

In this article, we examine how the rise of financialization has contributed to the changing nature of employment relations using the case of workforce downsizing. While previous studies demonstrate how the reallocation of resources toward financial activities, at the sectoral as well as firm level, constrains employment growth (Lin and Tomaskovic-Devey, 2013; Lin, 2016), our study offers a mechanism through which the financial sector shapes downsizing decisions of non-financial firms. Our analysis using a dataset on the largest US firms shows that their downsizing decisions have become closely tied to their ability to meet expectations set by securities analysts through their quarterly earnings forecasts. When they fail, they engage in downsizing in a bid to reassure investors who closely follow analyst forecasts and firms’ performance against the benchmark. The binary indicator of whether a firm meets or fails to meet such expectations dominates other measures of corporate performance, such as whether they outperform their past performance. In this way, our study tells a story of the institutionalization of downsizing as a shareholder value strategy in the era of financialization.

By focusing on firm-level mechanisms of financialization and its impact on the changing nature of employment relations at the largest US firms, our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, our study demonstrates an important mechanism through which the

financial sector can exert influence over corporate employment decisions through the role of securities analysts as key financial professionals (Zuckerman, 1999; Dobbin and Zorn, 2005). One of the central aspects of financialization is the increased influence of the financial sector and its key players over non-financial firms (Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin, 2011; Appelbaum and Batt, 2014; Van der Zwan, 2014). Our study illustrates the role of financial professionals in shaping market expectations about firms and their future performance and making these expectations have material impacts on corporate decisions. Our analysis shows that the increased pressure put forth by securities analysts upon firms to meet earnings forecasts induces firms to embrace drastic restructuring measures, such as downsizing. While the original purpose of analyst forecasts was to help investors make informed decisions about firms’ prospects, in a process similar to performativity (Mackenzie and Millo, 2003), these forecasts dictate how firms should perform once market expectations are firmly formed around the forecasts. In this way, the role of securities analysts provides a powerful channel through which financial market participants can exert influence over corporate decisions to their advantage.

Second, our study draws attention to the influence of competing stakeholder groups as an important factor that can mediate the impact of financialization over firm-level employment decisions and outcomes. While many studies in organizational analysis suggest that large-scale economic, political and social changes can reshape firm behavior and strategic decisions, many other studies contend that firms do not passively yield to external changes (Westphal and Zajac, 2001; Zajac and Westphal, 2004; Goldstein, 2012; Benton 2016). A similar issue can be raised in the case of financialization. Financialization is fundamentally a political project that involves rent extraction between sectors and among actions within the firm (Tomaskovic-Devey and Lin, 2011; Lin, 2016). As such, it is a setting ripe for power struggle among stakeholder groups with competing interests. In this respect, the outcome of changing power relations among stakeholder groups and decision-making structures can significantly moderate the impact of financialization, for which our findings provide some support. In line with the growing calls for firm-level or workplace-level mechanisms of resource distribution (Tomaskovic-Devey and Avent-Holt, 2019; Tomaskovic-Devey et al., 2020), more research seems to be warranted about how the impact of financialization varies across firms and what the organizational sources are for such variation.

Overall, our study demonstrates the benefit of a more integrated approach to the research on financialization. Our investigation of the firm-level inter-group power relations helps elaborate how large national- or sector-level structural changes channel into firm-level decisions with enduring impact on corporate employment policies and practices. On the one hand, while the rise of shareholder value has been understood as part of the broader trend toward financialization, most studies on shareholder value tend to focus on the firm-level power struggle between shareholders and managers without paying closer attention to the overarching socio-economic context within which such struggle occurs (Van der Zwan, 2014; but see Davis, 2009). On the other, studies of financialization, while revealing the shift toward financial activities at the national, sectoral or firm level, often lose sight of the concrete actors and the struggle among them over financialization. As a result, it remains unclear how key players in the financial sector have exerted influence over non-financial sectors and firms. It also remains uncertain how the rise of finance has reshaped power relations among competing stakeholder groups. The increased influence of financial markets on corporate downsizing decisions and employment relations in general that we demonstrate in this study raises several important policy issues. First, it may explain the short-term orientation of US firms toward human resource management (Kochan, 2012; Weil, 2014). Viable human resource strategies require long-term planning and investment. The frequent use of downsizing and its responsiveness to short-term financial market expectations may make such long-term goals difficult if not impossible. It also means that finance penetrates the lives of ordinary citizens, by exposing the job security of working and middle-class citizens to the vagaries of financial markets. This trend is particularly concerning because it is unlikely that most workers can manage such risks as effectively as firms or financial institutions (Davis, 2009; Lin and Tobias Neely, 2020). The impact of the 2008 financial crisis—massive job losses and prolonged hiring freezes during the Great Recession and the jobless recovery afterward—is a drastic example that shows the broad social repercussions of financial risk (Grusky et al., 2011). But our study suggests that if financial market control over corporate employment decisions remains, the transformation of financial risks into social risks will continue.
